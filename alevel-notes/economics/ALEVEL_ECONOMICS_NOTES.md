## 1.1 Scarcity, Choice & Opportunity Cost

### Key Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Scarcity** | The fundamental economic problem — unlimited wants vs. limited resources. Forces choices to be made. |
| **Choice** | The decision made from alternative options due to scarcity. |
| **Opportunity Cost** | The next best alternative foregone when a choice is made. |
| **Economic Good** | A good that is scarce and has an opportunity cost (e.g., cars, food). |
| **Free Good** | A good with no opportunity cost — unlimited supply (e.g., air, sunlight). |

### The Basic Economic Problem

The core issue in economics is that **human wants are unlimited**, but **resources are limited**. This creates three fundamental questions every economy must answer:

1. **What to produce?** — Which goods and services should be produced?
2. **How to produce?** — What combination of resources and methods?
3. **For whom to produce?** — How should output be distributed?

### Opportunity Cost Formula

```
Opportunity Cost = Return on Best Foregone Alternative
```

> **Exam Tip:** Always identify the **next best alternative**, not just any alternative. Opportunity cost is not the sum of all foregone options.

### Production Possibility Curve (PPC) / Production Possibility Frontier (PPF)

The PPC shows the **maximum combinations of two goods** an economy can produce using all available resources efficiently.

#### Shape of the PPC

| Shape | Explanation | Cause |
|-------|-------------|-------|
| **Concave (bowed out)** | Increasing opportunity cost | Resources are not perfectly transferable between uses |
| **Straight line** | Constant opportunity cost | Resources perfectly transferable |
| **Convex** | Decreasing opportunity cost | Rare; specialisation benefits |

**[DIAGRAM: Standard concave PPC showing increasing opportunity cost with points for efficient, inefficient, and unattainable production]**

#### Points on the PPC

| Position | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| **On the curve** | Productively efficient — all resources fully employed |
| **Inside the curve** | Inefficient — unemployed/underutilised resources |
| **Outside the curve** | Unattainable with current resources and technology |

#### Shifts in the PPC

| Direction | Cause | Examples |
|-----------|-------|----------|
| **Outward shift** | Economic growth | Improved technology, more resources, better education, discovery of raw materials |
| **Inward shift** | Economic decline | War, natural disasters, emigration, depletion of resources |
| **Pivot** | Growth in one sector only | Technological improvement in one industry |

**[DIAGRAM: PPC outward shift showing economic growth; PPC pivot showing biased growth in one good]**

> **Exam Tip:** A movement along the PPC is not economic growth — it is reallocation. Only a shift of the curve itself represents growth or decline.

## 1.2 Positive vs Normative Statements

| Aspect | Positive Statements | Normative Statements |
|--------|---------------------|----------------------|
| **Nature** | Objective, fact-based | Subjective, opinion-based |
| **Testability** | Can be tested/verified with evidence | Cannot be proven true or false |
| **Contains** | "is," "was," "will be," "causes" | "ought to," "should," "fair," "best" |
| **Examples** | "A rise in interest rates reduces investment." | "The government should raise the minimum wage." |
| **Role in Economics** | Used in economic analysis and models | Used in policy recommendations |

> **Exam Tip:** In essay questions, distinguish clearly between positive analysis (what happens) and normative judgement (what should happen). Strong answers use positive economics to support normative conclusions.

## 1.3 Economic Systems

| System | What is Produced | How | For Whom | Examples |
|--------|-----------------|-----|----------|----------|
| **Free Market** | Whatever is profitable | By private firms competing | Those who can pay | Hong Kong, Singapore (mostly) |
| **Planned/Command** | Whatever government decides | By state-owned enterprises | According to government plan | Former USSR, North Korea |
| **Mixed** | Mix of market and government | Private + public sector | Mix of market income + welfare | Most countries (UK, US, EU) |

### Market Economy Characteristics
- Private ownership of resources
- Profit motive drives production
- Prices determined by supply and demand
- Consumer sovereignty
- Limited government intervention

### Planned Economy Characteristics
- Government ownership of resources
- Central planning sets production targets
- Prices set by government
- Equality objectives
- No private profit motive

### Mixed Economy
- Most common in practice
- Market forces dominate but government intervenes to:
  - Provide public goods
  - Correct market failures
  - Redistribute income
  - Regulate monopolies

## 1.4 Resource Allocation

### Factors of Production

| Factor | Description | Reward |
|--------|-------------|--------|
| **Land** | Natural resources (oil, forests, water, minerals) | Rent |
| **Labour** | Human effort (physical and mental) | Wages |
| **Capital** | Man-made aids to production (machinery, factories, tools) | Interest |
| **Enterprise** | Risk-taking and organisation by entrepreneurs | Profit |

### Specialisation and Division of Labour

**Advantages:**
- Increased productivity (workers focus on what they do best)
- Time saved (no switching between tasks)
- Innovation (workers find better ways to do specific tasks)
- Lower unit costs (economies of scale)

**Disadvantages:**
- Boredom and alienation
- Loss of flexibility (workers cannot switch tasks)
- Over-dependence (if one worker is absent, production stops)
- Deskilling

## 2.1 Demand

### Law of Demand
As price falls, quantity demanded rises (and vice versa), **ceteris paribus**.

### Reasons for Downward-Sloping Demand Curve
1. **Income effect** — Lower price increases real purchasing power
2. **Substitution effect** — Lower price makes good more attractive relative to substitutes
3. **Diminishing marginal utility** — Each additional unit gives less satisfaction

### Determinants of Demand (Shifters)

| Factor | Effect on Demand |
|--------|-----------------|
| **Income** | Normal goods: demand rises with income. Inferior goods: demand falls with income |
| **Prices of substitutes** | Substitute price rises → demand for this good rises |
| **Prices of complements** | Complement price rises → demand for this good falls |
| **Tastes/preferences** | Favourable change → demand rises |
| **Population/demographics** | More consumers → demand rises |
| **Expectations** | Expected future price rise → current demand rises |

### Individual vs Market Demand
- **Individual demand:** One consumer's demand at various prices
- **Market demand:** Horizontal sum of all individual demands

---

## 2.2 Supply

### Law of Supply
As price rises, quantity supplied rises (and vice versa), **ceteris paribus**.

### Reasons for Upward-Sloping Supply Curve
1. **Profit motive** — Higher prices mean higher profits
2. **Increasing marginal costs** — Producing more requires less efficient resources

### Determinants of Supply (Shifters)

| Factor | Effect on Supply |
|--------|-----------------|
| **Input prices** | Input cost rises → supply falls (curve shifts left) |
| **Technology** | Improved technology → supply rises (curve shifts right) |
| **Number of producers** | More firms → supply rises |
| **Taxes/subsidies** | Tax rises → supply falls. Subsidy rises → supply rises |
| **Expectations** | Expected future price rise → current supply falls (hoarding) |
| **Weather/natural factors** | Favourable conditions → supply rises |

## 2.3 Price Determination & Elasticity

### Market Equilibrium

**Equilibrium price:** Where quantity demanded = quantity supplied

**Excess demand (shortage):** Price below equilibrium → upward pressure on price
**Excess supply (surplus):** Price above equilibrium → downward pressure on price

**[DIAGRAM: Supply and demand curves intersecting at equilibrium price P* and quantity Q*]**

### Consumer and Producer Surplus

| | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Consumer Surplus** | Difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay (area below demand curve, above price) |
| **Producer Surplus** | Difference between price received and minimum price producers would accept (area above supply curve, below price) |

### Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

```
PED = (% Change in Quantity Demanded) / (% Change in Price)
```

Or using point elasticity:
```
PED = (ΔQ / Q) / (ΔP / P)
```

| Value | Classification | Effect of Price Rise on Total Revenue |
|-------|---------------|--------------------------------------|
| PED > 1 | Elastic | Total revenue falls |
| PED = 1 | Unit elastic | Total revenue unchanged |
| PED < 1 | Inelastic | Total revenue rises |
| PED = 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Total revenue rises proportionally |
| PED = ∞ | Perfectly elastic | Total revenue falls to zero |

**Determinants of PED:**
- Availability of substitutes (more substitutes = more elastic)
- Necessity vs luxury (necessities = inelastic)
- Proportion of income (higher proportion = more elastic)
- Time period (longer time = more elastic)
- Habit-forming goods (addictive = inelastic)

### Income Elasticity of Demand (YED)

```
YED = (% Change in Quantity Demanded) / (% Change in Income)
```

| Value | Classification |
|-------|---------------|
| YED > 0 | Normal good |
| YED < 0 | Inferior good |
| YED > 1 | Luxury good (income elastic) |
| 0 < YED < 1 | Necessity (income inelastic) |

### Cross Elasticity of Demand (XED)

```
XED = (% Change in Quantity Demanded of Good A) / (% Change in Price of Good B)
```

| Value | Relationship |
|-------|-------------|
| XED > 0 | Substitutes |
| XED < 0 | Complements |
| XED = 0 | Unrelated |

### Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)

```
PES = (% Change in Quantity Supplied) / (% Change in Price)
```

| Value | Classification |
|-------|---------------|
| PES > 1 | Elastic |
| PES = 1 | Unit elastic |
| PES < 1 | Inelastic |
| PES = 0 | Perfectly inelastic |
| PES = ∞ | Perfectly elastic |

**Determinants of PES:**
- Spare capacity (more spare capacity = more elastic)
- Availability of stocks (more stocks = more elastic)
- Time period (longer = more elastic)
- Ease of factor substitution (easier = more elastic)
- Mobility of factors (more mobile = more elastic)

## 2.4 Theory of the Firm

### Short Run vs Long Run

| | Short Run | Long Run |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Period where at least one factor is fixed | Period where all factors are variable |
| **Fixed factors** | Capital, factory size, machinery | None |
| **Variable factors** | Labour, raw materials | All |

### Costs

| Cost | Formula | Behaviour |
|------|---------|-----------|
| **Total Fixed Cost (TFC)** | — | Constant regardless of output |
| **Total Variable Cost (TVC)** | — | Rises with output |
| **Total Cost (TC)** | TFC + TVC | Rises with output |
| **Average Fixed Cost (AFC)** | TFC / Q | Falls continuously |
| **Average Variable Cost (AVC)** | TVC / Q | U-shaped |
| **Average Cost (AC)** | TC / Q or AFC + AVC | U-shaped |
| **Marginal Cost (MC)** | ΔTC / ΔQ | U-shaped; intersects AC at minimum |

**[DIAGRAM: Cost curves showing MC cutting AC and AVC at their minimum points]**

### Revenue

| Revenue | Formula |
|---------|---------|
| **Total Revenue (TR)** | Price × Quantity |
| **Average Revenue (AR)** | TR / Q = Price |
| **Marginal Revenue (MR)** | ΔTR / ΔQ |

### Profit Maximisation

```
Profit = Total Revenue − Total Cost
```

**Profit-maximising output:** Where **MC = MR** (and MC is rising)

**[DIAGRAM: MC and MR curves intersecting at profit-maximising output]**

> **Exam Tip:** Profit maximisation assumes firms have perfect information and aim to maximise profit. In reality, firms may satisfice, maximise revenue, or pursue other objectives.

### Shut-Down Condition

- **Short run:** Shut down if **P < AVC** (cannot cover variable costs)
- **Long run:** Exit market if **P < AC** (cannot cover total costs)

## 2.5 Market Structures

### Perfect Competition

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Number of firms | Many |
| Product | Homogeneous (identical) |
| Barriers to entry | None |
| Information | Perfect |
| Price control | None (price taker) |

**Equilibrium:**
- Short run: Can make supernormal profit or loss
- Long run: Only normal profit (P = minimum AC)

**[DIAGRAM: Perfect competition showing firm as price taker, with horizontal demand curve at market price]**

### Monopoly

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Number of firms | One |
| Product | Unique, no close substitutes |
| Barriers to entry | High (legal, natural, technological) |
| Price control | Significant (price maker) |

**Equilibrium:**
- MR < Demand (AR)
- Profit max: MC = MR
- P > MC (allocative inefficiency)
- P > minimum AC (productive inefficiency)
- Can make supernormal profit in long run

**[DIAGRAM: Monopoly showing profit-maximising output where MC=MR, with price read off demand curve above]**

### Monopolistic Competition

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Number of firms | Many |
| Product | Differentiated |
| Barriers to entry | Low |
| Price control | Some (brand loyalty) |

**Equilibrium:**
- Short run: Can make supernormal profit
- Long run: Only normal profit (due to entry of new firms)
- Excess capacity (not producing at minimum AC)

### Oligopoly

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Number of firms | Few |
| Product | Homogeneous or differentiated |
| Barriers to entry | High |
| Price control | Significant (interdependent) |

**Key Characteristics:**
- **Interdependence:** Each firm's decisions affect rivals
- **Game theory:** Strategic behaviour (prisoner's dilemma)
- **Kinked demand curve:** Rivals match price cuts but not price rises
- **Collusion:** Explicit (cartels) or tacit (price leadership)

**[DIAGRAM: Kinked demand curve showing asymmetric reactions to price changes]**

### Comparison of Market Structures

| Feature | Perfect Competition | Monopolistic Competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---------|--------------------|--------------------------|-----------|----------|
| Firms | Many | Many | Few | One |
| Product | Homogeneous | Differentiated | Homogeneous/Differentiated | Unique |
| Barriers | None | Low | High | Very high |
| Long-run profit | Normal only | Normal only | Possible supernormal | Possible supernormal |
| Efficiency | Allocative & productive | Neither | Neither | Neither |
| Examples | Agriculture | Restaurants | Cars, phones | Utilities, patents |

## 2.6 Market Failure

### Types of Market Failure

#### 1. Externalities

**Negative externalities (external costs):**
- Social cost > Private cost
- Overproduction from society's perspective
- Examples: Pollution, traffic congestion, smoking

**Positive externalities (external benefits):**
- Social benefit > Private benefit
- Underproduction from society's perspective
- Examples: Education, vaccination, R&D

**[DIAGRAM: Negative externality showing MSC > MPC, with overproduction; Positive externality showing MSB > MPB, with underproduction]**

#### 2. Public Goods

| Characteristic | Meaning |
|---------------|---------|
| **Non-excludable** | Cannot prevent anyone from consuming |
| **Non-rivalrous** | One person's consumption doesn't reduce availability |

**Examples:** National defence, street lighting, lighthouses

**Free-rider problem:** People benefit without paying → private firms won't provide → government must provide

#### 3. Merit and Demerit Goods

| | Merit Goods | Demerit Goods |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Socially desirable; underconsumed | Socially undesirable; overconsumed |
| **Problem** | Information failure (people don't realise benefits) | Information failure (people don't realise harms) |
| **Examples** | Education, healthcare, insurance | Cigarettes, alcohol, drugs, gambling |
| **Government response** | Subsidies, provision, information | Taxes, bans, regulations, information |

#### 4. Market Power

- **Monopoly power:** P > MC → underproduction
- **Monopsony power:** Single buyer pushes price below competitive level

#### 5. Inequality

- Market distributes according to purchasing power, not need
- May result in unacceptable inequality

### Government Responses to Market Failure

| Policy | When Used | How It Works |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| **Taxes (Pigouvian)** | Negative externalities | Internalises externality; raises private cost to social cost |
| **Subsidies** | Positive externalities, merit goods | Lowers private cost/benefit to encourage consumption/production |
| **Regulation** | Public goods, demerit goods | Direct control (bans, standards, quotas) |
| **Provision** | Public goods, merit goods | Government directly provides (education, healthcare) |
| **Tradable permits** | Pollution | Market-based solution to control total emissions |
| **Information** | Information failure | Educate consumers about true costs/benefits |

## 3.1 Maximum and Minimum Prices

### Price Ceilings (Maximum Prices)

- Set **below** market equilibrium
- Aim: Make essential goods affordable
- Result: Excess demand (shortage), queues, black markets
- Examples: Rent controls, price controls during crises

**[DIAGRAM: Price ceiling below equilibrium showing shortage]**

### Price Floors (Minimum Prices)

- Set **above** market equilibrium
- Aim: Protect producers' incomes
- Result: Excess supply (surplus)
- Examples: Minimum wage, agricultural price supports, EU Common Agricultural Policy

**[DIAGRAM: Price floor above equilibrium showing surplus]**

---

## 3.2 Taxes and Subsidies

### Tax Incidence

**Who bears the burden of a tax depends on elasticity:**

| PED vs PES | Burden Falls On... |
|-----------|-------------------|
| PED < PES (demand more inelastic) | Consumers |
| PED > PES (supply more inelastic) | Producers |
| PED = PES | Shared equally |

**[DIAGRAM: Tax incidence showing how elasticity determines burden distribution]**

### Ad Valorem vs Specific Tax

| Specific Tax | Ad Valorem Tax |
|-------------|---------------|
| Fixed amount per unit | Percentage of price |
| Shifts supply curve up parallel | Rotates supply curve |
| Example: $1 per litre of petrol | Example: 20% VAT |

### Subsidy Effects

- Shifts supply curve down/right
- Benefits shared between consumers (lower price) and producers (higher effective price)
- Distribution depends on elasticity

## 3.5 Regulation and Competition Policy

### Reasons for Regulation

- Prevent abuse of monopoly power
- Protect consumers
- Ensure quality/safety standards
- Correct information asymmetries
- Protect the environment

### Types of Regulation

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Price regulation** | Cap prices (RPI-X formula for utilities) |
| **Quality standards** | Minimum requirements (safety, hygiene) |
| **Quantity restrictions** | Limits on output/pollution |
| **Information requirements** | Mandatory disclosure |

### Competition Policy

| Policy | Purpose |
|--------|---------|
| **Anti-trust laws** | Prevent monopolies and cartels |
| **Merger control** | Block anti-competitive mergers |
| **Price fixing prohibition** | Prevent collusion |

### Privatisation and Nationalisation

| Privatisation | Nationalisation |
|--------------|-----------------|
| State → Private ownership | Private → State ownership |

## 4.1 National Income Accounting

### GDP and Related Measures

| Measure | Definition | Formula |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| **GDP (Gross Domestic Product)** | Total value of goods/services produced within a country's borders | C + I + G + (X − M) |
| **GNP (Gross National Product)** | Total value produced by a country's citizens, wherever located | GDP + Net property income from abroad |
| **NNP (Net National Product)** | GNP minus depreciation | GNP − Depreciation |
| **Real GDP** | GDP adjusted for inflation | Nominal GDP / Price Index × 100 |
| **Nominal GDP** | GDP at current prices | — |
| **GDP per capita** | GDP divided by population | GDP / Population |

### Methods of Measuring GDP

1. **Expenditure method:** C + I + G + (X − M)
2. **Income method:** Sum of all incomes (wages + rent + interest + profit)
3. **Output method:** Sum of value added at each stage of production

### Circular Flow of Income

**[DIAGRAM: Circular flow showing households, firms, government, and foreign sector with flows of goods, services, money, and leakages/injections]**

**Leakages (withdrawals):** Savings (S), Taxes (T), Imports (M)
**Injections:** Investment (I), Government spending (G), Exports (X)

**Equilibrium condition:** Leakages = Injections (S + T + M = I + G + X)

---

## 4.2 Aggregate Demand and Aggregate Supply

### Aggregate Demand (AD)

```
AD = C + I + G + (X − M)
```

| Component | Determinants |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Consumption (C)** | Income, wealth, interest rates, expectations, distribution of income |
| **Investment (I)** | Interest rates, business confidence, technology, capacity utilisation |
| **Government spending (G)** | Political decisions, automatic stabilisers |
| **Net exports (X − M)** | Exchange rates, foreign income, trade barriers, competitiveness |

**[DIAGRAM: Downward-sloping AD curve showing negative relationship between price level and real output]**

### Aggregate Supply

**Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS):**
- Upward sloping
- Input prices (especially wages) are sticky
- Higher price level → higher profits → more output

**Long-Run Aggregate Supply (LRAS):**
- Vertical at full employment output (Yf)
- Determined by factors of production and technology
- Changes in price level do not affect long-run output

**[DIAGRAM: SRAS upward sloping, LRAS vertical, showing short-run and long-run equilibrium]**

### Shifts in AD and AS

**AD shifts right (increases):**
- Expansionary fiscal policy (tax cuts, spending rises)
- Expansionary monetary policy (interest rate cuts, quantitative easing)
- Increased consumer/business confidence
- Currency depreciation (boosts exports)
- Economic growth in trading partners

**AD shifts left (decreases):**
- Contractionary policies
- Recession in trading partners
- Currency appreciation
- Fall in confidence

**SRAS shifts right (increases):**
- Lower input prices (especially oil)
- Lower wages
- Improved productivity
- Supply-side policies

**LRAS shifts right:**
- More resources
- Better technology
- Improved labour productivity
- Increase in working population

## 4.3 Economic Growth

### Types of Growth

| Type | Description | Cause |
|------|-------------|-------|
| **Actual growth** | Increase in real GDP | AD increases |
| **Potential growth** | Increase in productive capacity | LRAS shifts right |

### Costs and Benefits of Growth

**Benefits:**
- Higher living standards
- More employment
- Higher tax revenue
- Ability to fund public services
- Investment in innovation

**Costs:**
- Inflation (if demand grows faster than supply)
- Current account deficit (if growth is consumption-led)
- Environmental degradation
- Inequality (if benefits are unevenly distributed)
- Loss of leisure time

### Sources of Growth

- **Capital accumulation:** Investment in physical capital
- **Labour force growth:** More workers
- **Human capital improvement:** Education and training
- **Technological progress:** Innovation and R&D

## 4.4 Unemployment

### Types of Unemployment

| Type | Cause | Solution |
|------|-------|----------|
| **Frictional** | Time between jobs | Better job matching, information |
| **Structural** | Skills/location mismatch | Retraining, relocation incentives |
| **Cyclical (demand-deficient)** | Insufficient AD | Demand management policies |
| **Seasonal** | Seasonal industries | Diversification, off-season training |
| **Technological** | Automation replaces labour | Retraining, education |

### Natural Rate of Unemployment

```
Natural Rate = Frictional + Structural
```
- The rate that exists when labour market is in equilibrium
- Cannot be reduced by demand management (only supply-side policies)

### Costs of Unemployment

| To Individual | To Economy | To Government |
|--------------|-----------|--------------|
| Lost income | Lost output (GDP below potential) | Benefit payments |
| Skills atrophy | Lower tax revenue | Lost tax revenue |
| Psychological harm | Social costs (crime, health) | Social problems |

---

## 4.5 Inflation

### Measuring Inflation

```
Inflation Rate = [(Price Index_t − Price Index_t-1) / Price Index_t-1] × 100
```

**Price indices:**
- **Consumer Price Index (CPI):** Basket of consumer goods
- **Producer Price Index (PPI):** Input prices for producers
- **GDP Deflator:** All goods/services in GDP

### Types of Inflation

| Type | Cause | Description |
|------|-------|-------------|
| **Demand-pull** | AD > AS at full employment | "Too much money chasing too few goods" |
| **Cost-push** | Increase in costs of production | Higher wages, oil prices, taxes |
| **Built-in** | Expectations of future inflation | Wage-price spiral |
| **Monetary** | Excessive money supply growth | "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon" (Friedman) |

### Costs of Inflation

| Cost | Explanation |
|------|-------------|
| **Menu costs** | Cost of changing prices |
| **Shoe-leather costs** | Cost of holding less cash |
| **Redistribution** | Fixed-income losers, debtors gain |
| **Uncertainty** | Makes planning difficult |
| **International competitiveness** | If domestic inflation > trading partners |
| **Fiscal drag** | Tax brackets not adjusted → real tax burden rises |

### Deflation

- Sustained fall in general price level
- Can be more harmful than moderate inflation
- Increases real debt burden
- Delays consumption (expect further price falls)

## 4.6 Balance of Payments

### Current Account

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Trade in goods (visibles)** | Exports − Imports of physical goods |
| **Trade in services (invisibles)** | Exports − Imports of services |
| **Primary income** | Income from investments, wages |
| **Secondary income** | Transfers (aid, remittances) |

```
Current Account = (X − M) + Net Income + Net Transfers
```

### Capital Account

- Capital transfers
- Acquisition/disposal of non-produced, non-financial assets

### Financial Account

- Direct investment
- Portfolio investment
- Other investment
- Reserve assets

```
Current Account + Capital Account + Financial Account = 0
```

### Causes of Current Account Deficits

- Loss of competitiveness
- Strong currency
- High inflation relative to trading partners
- Low savings, high consumption
- Economic boom (imports rise faster than exports)

### Consequences of Current Account Deficits

| Short-term | Long-term |
|-----------|-----------|
| Currency depreciation | Foreign debt accumulation |
| Higher interest rates | Loss of confidence |
 | Foreign financing needed | Potential crisis |

### Policies to Correct Current Account Deficit

| Policy | Mechanism |
|--------|-----------|
| **Expenditure-reducing** | Contractionary fiscal/monetary policy reduces AD and imports |
| **Expenditure-switching** | Depreciation, tariffs, quotas switch demand to domestic goods |
| **Supply-side** | Improve competitiveness through productivity gains |

## 4.7 Exchange Rates

### Exchange Rate Systems

| System | Description | Examples |
|--------|-------------|----------|
| **Fixed** | Pegged to another currency or basket | Hong Kong (USD peg), Gulf currencies |
| **Floating** | Determined by market forces | US dollar, Euro, Yen |
| **Managed float** | Mostly market-determined with occasional intervention | Most countries |

### Factors Affecting Exchange Rates

| Factor | Effect on Currency |
|--------|-------------------|
| **Interest rates** | Higher rates → capital inflows → appreciation |

## 5.5 International Trade and Development

### Comparative Advantage

A country has comparative advantage in producing a good if it can produce it at a **lower opportunity cost** than other countries.

**Example:**

| | Country A | Country B |
|---|---|---|

- **Country B has comparative advantage in cloth**

## DERIVATION 1: Why MR = MC Maximises Profit

### Mathematical Proof

Profit (π) = Total Revenue (TR) − Total Cost (TC)

To maximise profit, we take the derivative with respect to quantity (Q) and set it equal to zero:

dπ/dQ = d(TR)/dQ − d(TC)/dQ = 0

Since d(TR)/dQ = Marginal Revenue (MR)
and d(TC)/dQ = Marginal Cost (MC):

MR − MC = 0
**Therefore: MR = MC**

### Why MC Must Be Rising

For MR = MC to be a **maximum** (not minimum), the second derivative must be negative:

d²π/dQ² = d(MR)/dQ − d(MC)/dQ < 0

Since demand curves slope downward, MR is falling (d(MR)/dQ < 0).
For the inequality to hold, MC must be rising (d(MC)/dQ > 0).

**Economic intuition:** If MC were falling, producing more would continually reduce cost per unit, and the firm would expand indefinitely — no profit-maximising output would exist.

---

## DERIVATION 2: The Multiplier Effect

### Basic Multiplier

When autonomous spending increases (e.g., government spending), it creates a chain reaction:

Round 1: Initial injection = ΔG = $100m
Round 2: Recipients spend MPC × $100m = $80m (if MPC = 0.8)
Round 3: Next recipients spend MPC × $80m = $64m
Round 4: MPC × $64m = $51.2m
...

Total increase in GDP = $100m + $80m + $64m + $51.2m + ...

This is a geometric series with first term a = $100m and common ratio r = MPC = 0.8

Sum = a / (1 − r) = $100m / (1 − 0.8) = $100m / 0.2 = **$500m**

Multiplier (k) = 1 / (1 − MPC) = 1 / MPS = **5**

### Full Multiplier with Leakages

In reality, there are three leakages: savings (MPS), taxes (MPT), and imports (MPM)

k = 1 / (MPS + MPT + MPM)

Example: MPC = 0.6, MPT = 0.2, MPM = 0.1
MPS = 1 − 0.6 = 0.4

k = 1 / (0.4 + 0.2 + 0.1) = 1 / 0.7 = **1.43**

This shows why the multiplier is smaller in open economies with high tax rates.

## WORKED EXAMPLE 1: PED Calculation with Data

### Scenario
The price of Starbucks coffee increases from $4.00 to $5.00. Weekly demand falls from 10,000 cups to 8,000 cups.

### Step-by-Step Calculation

**Using the correct formula (point elasticity):**

PED = (% Change in Quantity Demanded) / (% Change in Price)

% Change in Quantity = (8,000 − 10,000) / 10,000 × 100 = −20%
% Change in Price = ($5.00 − $4.00) / $4.00 × 100 = +25%

PED = −20% / 25% = **−0.8**

Using absolute value: **PED = 0.8**

### Interpretation

Since PED < 1, demand is **price inelastic**. This means:
- A 1% price increase leads to only 0.8% fall in quantity demanded
- Total revenue increases when price rises
- Old revenue: $4.00 × 10,000 = $40,000
- New revenue: $5.00 × 8,000 = $40,000

Wait — revenue stayed the same? Let's check:
Actually: Old = $40,000, New = $40,000. Revenue unchanged.

This is because PED = 0.8 is close to unitary. With exact unitary elasticity (PED = 1), revenue would be maximised and unchanged.

### Using Midpoint (Arc) Elasticity

For more accuracy when price changes are large:

PED = [(Q2 − Q1) / ((Q1 + Q2)/2)] / [(P2 − P1) / ((P1 + P2)/2)]

= [(8,000 − 10,000) / 9,000] / [($5 − $4) / $4.50]
= (−2,000/9,000) / ($1/$4.50)
= (−0.222) / (0.222)
= **−1.0**

Using arc elasticity, demand is **unitary elastic** — revenue is maximised at this price point.

### Finding Profit-Maximising Output

At P = $10 (perfectly competitive firm):

Profit maximisation where **MC = MR**
Since P = MR in perfect competition, find where MC = $10

Looking at the table:
- At Q = 5: MC = $8 (less than MR = $10)
- At Q = 6: MC = $12 (greater than MR = $10)

Since MC rises from $8 to $12 as output increases from 5 to 6, and MR = $10 lies between these, the profit-maximising output is **Q = 5 units** (producing the last unit where MC ≤ MR).

At Q = 5: Profit = −$84 (minimum loss)

Wait — this firm is making losses at all output levels! Let's verify:
At Q = 5: TR = $50, TC = $134, Profit = −$84
At Q = 0: Loss = TFC = $100

**Shutdown condition:** If P < AVC at all output levels, shut down.
At Q = 5: AVC = $6.80, P = $10. Since P > AVC, continue producing.

## WORKED EXAMPLE 3: Market Structure Comparison with Numbers

### Four Firms in Different Markets

All face the same cost structure but different demand conditions:

| | Perfect Competition | Monopolistic Competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Number of firms** | Thousands | Many | Few | One |
| **Product** | Homogeneous | Differentiated | Homogeneous or differentiated | Unique |
| **Price control** | None (price taker) | Some (brand loyalty) | Significant (interdependent) | Complete |
| **Barriers to entry** | None | Low | High | Very high |
| **Long-run profit** | Normal only | Normal only | Possible supernormal | Possible supernormal |
| **Example** | Wheat farming | Restaurants | Airlines | Singapore Power |

### Numerical Example: Equilibrium Comparison

Assume all have TC = 50 + 5Q + 0.5Q²

**Perfect Competition (P = MR = $15):**
Profit max: MC = MR
MC = d(TC)/dQ = 5 + Q
Set 5 + Q = 15 → Q = 10
Profit = TR − TC = ($15 × 10) − (50 + 50 + 50) = $150 − $150 = **$0** ✓ (normal profit)

**Monopoly (Demand: P = 25 − Q):**
TR = P × Q = 25Q − Q²
MR = d(TR)/dQ = 25 − 2Q
Set MR = MC: 25 − 2Q = 5 + Q
20 = 3Q → Q = 6.67
P = 25 − 6.67 = $18.33
Profit = ($18.33 × 6.67) − (50 + 33.35 + 22.22) = $122.26 − $105.57 = **+$16.69** ✓ (supernormal profit)

This demonstrates that monopoly produces **less output** (6.67 vs 10) at a **higher price** ($18.33 vs $15) and earns **supernormal profit** — the classic monopoly welfare loss.

## WORKED EXAMPLE 4: AD-AS Analysis with Numerical Shifts

### Initial Equilibrium

AD = 2,000 − 100P
SRAS = 500 + 100P
LRAS = 1,500 (vertical at full employment output)

**Short-run equilibrium (AD = SRAS):**
2,000 − 100P = 500 + 100P
1,500 = 200P
P = 7.5, Y = 1,250

Since Y (1,250) < LRAS (1,500), there is a **recessionary gap** of 250.

### Scenario: Government Spending Increases by $200

Multiplier = 2
Total AD shift = $200 × 2 = $400

New AD = (2,000 + 400) − 100P = 2,400 − 100P

**New short-run equilibrium:**
2,400 − 100P = 500 + 100P
1,900 = 200P
P = 9.5, Y = 1,450

**Result:**
- Output increased from 1,250 to 1,450 (+200)
- Price level increased from 7.5 to 9.5 (+2)
- Gap to full employment reduced from 250 to 50

**Long-run adjustment:**
As output exceeds LRAS temporarily, wages rise, shifting SRAS left:
New SRAS = 500 + 100P + wage adjustment
Eventually returns to Y = 1,500 at a higher price level.

## CASE STUDY 1: Singapore MAS Exchange Rate Policy

### Background
Singapore uses exchange rate policy (not interest rates) as its primary monetary tool because:
1. Singapore is a small, open economy (trade is 300%+ of GDP)
2. Free capital flows mean interest rates follow global trends
3. Most goods are imported, so exchange rate affects prices directly

### How MAS Policy Works

**Policy Band:**
- SGD managed against undisclosed trade-weighted basket
- MAS sets a "policy band" — allows SGD to fluctuate within a range
- Band can be shifted up (appreciation), down (depreciation), or steepened

**2022-2023 Inflation Response:**
- Global inflation surged due to supply chain disruptions, energy prices
- MAS tightened policy THREE times in 2022:
  1. January: Re-centred band upward
  2. April: Steepened slope of appreciation
  3. October: Further steepened slope
- This allowed faster SGD appreciation, making imports cheaper

### Impact Analysis

| Stakeholder | Impact of SGD Appreciation |
|------------|---------------------------|
| Consumers | Cheaper imports → lower inflation (helps purchasing power) |
| Exporters | More expensive overseas → reduced competitiveness (hurts) |
| Importers | Cheaper costs → higher profit margins or lower prices (helps) |
| Tourists | More expensive to visit Singapore → fewer tourists (hurts hospitality) |
| Investors | Stronger currency signals confidence → may attract investment |

### Evaluation
MAS policy has been effective in controlling imported inflation — Singapore's inflation peaked at ~7% in 2022 but was lower than many peers. However, export-oriented sectors (electronics, chemicals) faced margin pressure. The policy assumes that inflation is primarily imported rather than domestically generated. If domestic demand surges, exchange rate policy alone may be insufficient — fiscal tightening might also be needed.

## ESSAY MODEL ANSWER (25 marks)

**"Evaluate the extent to which a government should intervene to correct market failure."**

### Introduction
Market failure occurs when the free market allocates resources inefficiently, resulting in welfare loss. Common causes include externalities, public goods, merit/demerit goods, and market power. While government intervention can potentially correct these failures, the extent of intervention justified depends on the type and severity of failure, the effectiveness of policy tools, and the risk of government failure.

### Point 1: Arguments FOR Intervention
Government intervention is justified when market failures are significant. First, negative externalities (e.g., pollution) lead to overproduction because private costs < social costs. Without intervention, firms produce where MPC = MPB, ignoring external costs. A Pigouvian tax internalises the externality by shifting MPC upward, reducing output to the socially optimal level. For example, Singapore's carbon tax ($5/tonne, rising to $25 by 2030) aims to reduce emissions by making polluters pay.

Second, public goods (non-excludable, non-rivalrous) like national defence and street lighting will not be provided by the market due to the free-rider problem. Government provision through taxation is the only solution.

Third, information asymmetries (e.g., in healthcare and financial markets) lead to adverse selection and moral hazard. Regulation — such as mandatory disclosure requirements and licensing — can protect consumers who lack expertise.

### Point 2: Arguments AGAINST Excessive Intervention
However, government intervention carries risks. First, government failure may occur if politicians prioritise electoral gains over economic efficiency (regulatory capture, pork-barrel spending). Second, information problems affect governments too — central planners lack the local knowledge that markets aggregate through prices. Hayek argued that dispersed knowledge makes central planning inherently inefficient.

Third, taxation creates deadweight losses — even when correcting externalities, the tax itself distorts behaviour. Fourth, unintended consequences abound. Rent controls intended to help tenants reduce housing supply as landlords exit the market, worsening shortages. Finally, enforcement costs are significant — monitoring compliance, prosecuting violations, and administering programmes requires substantial resources.

### Point 3: Contextual Factors
The optimal level of intervention depends on context. In developed economies with strong institutions (Singapore, Scandinavia), government intervention tends to be more effective due to competent bureaucracy and low corruption. In developing economies with weak institutions, government failure may be worse than market failure. The type of market failure also matters — externalities with clearly measurable costs (carbon emissions) are easier to tax than vague externalities (education spillovers). Political economy factors — lobbying power, voter preferences, ideological commitments — also influence intervention choices.

### Conclusion
Government should intervene to correct market failure when the welfare gains from correction exceed the costs of intervention and risk of government failure. The optimal approach combines market mechanisms with targeted regulation: carbon pricing rather than command-and-control regulation, public-private partnerships for infrastructure, and nudges rather than mandates where possible. Singapore's approach — using market-based tools (carbon tax, ERP pricing) within a strong regulatory framework — offers a model that balances intervention with efficiency.
