### 1.1 Enterprise & Business Creation

#### Key Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Enterprise** | The skill / willingness of an individual to take risks and combine resources to produce goods/services for profit |
| **Entrepreneur** | An individual who organises resources and takes risks to start/run a business |
| **Business Plan** | A document setting out business objectives and strategies to achieve them |
| **Opportunity Cost** | The value of the next best alternative foregone when a decision is made |
| **Sole Trader** | A business owned and controlled by one person |
| **Partnership** | 2–20 people own and run a business together (unlimited liability) |
| **Incorporation** | Legal process of forming a limited company; creates separate legal identity |
| **Unlimited Liability** | Owners are personally responsible for all business debts |
| **Limited Liability** | Owners' liability restricted to the amount invested in shares |

#### Why Start a Business?

**Push factors:** Redundancy, desire for independence, unemployment, dissatisfaction with current role
**Pull factors:** Market gap identified, profit motive, passion/hobby, flexibility, wealth creation

#### The Business Plan

| Section | Purpose |
|---------|---------|
| Executive Summary | Overview of the whole plan |
| Business Description | What the business does, mission, vision |
| Market Analysis | Target market, competition, trends |
| Marketing Strategy | Pricing, promotion, distribution |
| Operations Plan | Location, suppliers, equipment |
| Financial Projections | Cash flow, profit/loss, break-even |
| Management Team | Skills and experience of founders |

> **Exam Tip:** Always link business plan sections to reducing risk for lenders/investors. A strong plan increases chance of securing finance.

#### Sources of Support for Entrepreneurs

- **Government grants & loans** (e.g., startup loans)
- **Business incubators** – shared workspace + mentoring
- **Enterprise agencies** – advice, training, networking
- **Venture capital / angel investors** – equity finance for high-growth startups
- **Crowdfunding** – raising small amounts from many people (reward-based or equity-based)

> **Exam Tip:** In evaluation, compare the cost of finance (interest, loss of control) against the risk of the venture.

### 1.2 Business Structure, Size, and Objectives

#### Types of Business Structure

| Feature | Sole Trader | Partnership | Private Limited Company (Ltd) | Public Limited Company (Plc) |
|---------|-------------|-------------|-------------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Ownership** | 1 person | 2–20 partners | Shareholders (max 50 if Pvt) | Shareholders (unlimited) |
| **Liability** | Unlimited | Unlimited (unless LLP) | Limited | Limited |
| **Legal Identity** | No separate legal identity | No separate legal identity | Separate legal identity | Separate legal identity |
| **Capital Raised** | Limited | Limited | More than sole/partnership | Most – via stock exchange |
| **Control** | Full control | Shared | Directors manage; shareholders own | Directors manage; shareholders own |
| **Accounts** | Not public | Not public | Filed at Companies House (private) | Filed publicly; audited |
| **Regulation** | Minimal | Partnership agreement recommended | More legal formalities | Most heavily regulated |
| **Continuity** | Ends if owner dies | Ends if partner leaves/dies | Perpetual succession | Perpetual succession |

> **Exam Tip:** Evaluation questions often ask you to recommend the best structure. Consider: size, need for capital, desire for control, risk tolerance, and regulatory burden.

#### Measuring Business Size

| Method | Explanation |
|--------|-------------|
| **Number of employees** | Simple but doesn't account for capital intensity |
| **Capital employed / value of output** | Better for capital-intensive firms |
| **Market share** | Percentage of total market sales |
| **Turnover (Revenue)** | Total sales value |

#### Business Objectives

| Objective | Description | When Most Important |
|-----------|-------------|---------------------|
| **Profit maximisation** | Produce where MR = MC; highest possible profit | Short-term; owner-managed firms |
| **Sales revenue maximisation** | Maximise total revenue; may sacrifice some profit | Firms seeking market dominance |
| **Satisficing** | Accept 'enough' profit; pursue other goals | Large corporations with complex stakeholder interests |
| **Survival** | Break even / minimal profit | Startups, recessions, crises |
| **Growth** | Increase market share, revenue, or assets | Mature firms; firms in growing markets |
| **Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)** | Balance profit with social/environmental goals | Firms under public scrutiny |
| **Shareholder value maximisation** | Increase share price and dividends | Plcs; financial markets focus |

> **Exam Tip:** Objectives change over the business life cycle. Startups focus on survival; mature firms on growth or profit; public firms on shareholder value.

#### Stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Interests | Potential Conflict |
|-------------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Shareholders** | Dividends, capital growth, ROI | With managers (principal-agent problem) |
| **Employees** | Wages, job security, conditions | With shareholders (cost cutting) |
| **Customers** | Quality, value, service | With profit margins |
| **Suppliers** | Prompt payment, repeat orders | With purchasing power |
| **Government** | Tax revenue, employment, compliance | With tax avoidance |
| **Community** | Environment, local jobs | With cost externalities |
| **Managers** | Salaries, bonuses, empire-building | With shareholders (satisficing vs maximising) |

> **Exam Tip:** Stakeholder conflicts are central to evaluation. Always weigh short-term vs long-term, and consider which stakeholder has the most power.

### 1.3 Business and the External Environment

#### PESTLE Analysis Framework

| Factor | Examples of Impact on Business |
|--------|-------------------------------|
| **Political** | Tax policy, trade restrictions, political stability, corruption |
| **Economic** | Interest rates, exchange rates, inflation, economic growth, unemployment |
| **Social** | Demographics, lifestyle trends, education levels, cultural attitudes |
| **Technological** | Automation, R&D activity, technology transfer, internet infrastructure |
| **Legal** | Consumer protection, employment law, health & safety, competition law |
| **Environmental** | Climate, weather, sustainability expectations, carbon regulations |

> **Exam Tip:** In application questions, link each PESTLE factor to a specific business decision. Don't just list factors — explain the causal chain.

#### Porter's 5 Forces (Industry Analysis)

| Force | High Threat When... | Impact on Profitability |
|-------|--------------------|------------------------|
| **Threat of new entrants** | Low barriers, low capital, weak brands | Prices fall; market share diluted |
| **Bargaining power of suppliers** | Few suppliers, high switching costs, unique inputs | Input costs rise; margins squeezed |
| **Bargaining power of buyers** | Many alternatives, low switching costs, price-sensitive | Prices fall; service expectations rise |
| **Threat of substitutes** | Close alternatives at lower prices | Demand shifts; prices constrained |
| **Competitive rivalry** | Many competitors, slow growth, high exit costs | Price wars; advertising battles |

> **Exam Tip:** Use Porter's 5 Forces to evaluate industry attractiveness. An industry with low threats across all five forces is highly profitable (e.g., pharmaceuticals with patents). An industry with high threats is a "red ocean" (e.g., budget airlines).

### 1.4 Business and the International Economy

#### Globalisation Drivers

- **Trade liberalisation** – WTO agreements reduce tariffs
- **Technological change** – Containerisation, internet, video conferencing
- **FDI flows** – Multinationals expand across borders
- **Deregulation** – Financial markets opened
- **Transport improvements** – Air freight, high-speed logistics

#### Reasons for International Trade

| Theory | Explanation |
|--------|-------------|
| **Comparative advantage** | Countries specialise in goods with lowest opportunity cost |
| **Economies of scale** | Larger markets allow mass production at lower unit cost |
| **Access to resources** | Raw materials, labour, or technology not available domestically |
| **Market seeking** | Saturated domestic markets; growth opportunities abroad |
| **Risk diversification** | Spread operations across countries to reduce exposure |

#### Protectionism Methods

| Method | How It Works | Example |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **Tariffs** | Tax on imports | 25% US tariff on Chinese steel |
| **Quotas** | Quantity limit on imports | EU sugar import quotas |
| **Subsidies** | Government aid to domestic producers | EU Common Agricultural Policy |
| **Embargoes** | Complete ban on trade | US embargo on Cuba |
| **Regulatory barriers** | Complex standards that exclude imports | Japanese agricultural standards |

#### Exchange Rates Impact

| Appreciation (Currency Stronger) | Depreciation (Currency Weaker) |
|----------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Exports more expensive → lower demand | Exports cheaper → higher demand |
| Imports cheaper → lower costs for importers | Imports more expensive → higher costs |
| Foreign investment more attractive | Domestic investment more attractive |
| Inflation reduced (cheaper imports) | Inflation increased (dearer imports) |

> **Exam Tip:** Always specify the business context when discussing exchange rates. A strong currency helps importers (e.g., Apple importing components) but hurts exporters (e.g., Singapore Airlines earning foreign revenue).

### 1.5 Business Ethics & Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)

#### Arguments FOR CSR

- Long-term reputation and brand value
- Attracts talent and retains employees
- Meets consumer expectations (especially younger demographics)
- Reduces regulatory risk
- Opens new markets (e.g., green products)
- Long-term profit sustainability
- Investor preference for ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance)

#### Arguments AGAINST CSR

- Distracts from profit maximisation
- Increases costs; reduces competitiveness
- Managers lack democratic mandate to spend shareholders' money
- Hard to measure social impact
- Can be 'greenwashing' / PR exercise only

#### Ethical Issues in Business

| Area | Examples |
|------|----------|
| **Labour** | Sweatshops, child labour, fair wages, working conditions |
| **Environment** | Pollution, carbon emissions, deforestation, waste |
| **Marketing** | False advertising, targeting vulnerable groups, data privacy |
| **Finance** | Tax avoidance, insider trading, executive pay |
| **Supply Chain** | Unethical sourcing, animal testing |

> **Exam Tip:** In evaluation, weigh short-term costs of ethical behaviour against long-term benefits. Consider the industry context – some sectors face more scrutiny.

### 2.1 Human Resource Management (HRM)

#### Key Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **HRM** | The strategic approach to the effective management of people to help the business gain competitive advantage |
| **Workforce Planning** | Process of analysing and forecasting workforce needs |
| **Labour Turnover** | Rate at which employees leave and are replaced |
| **Labour Productivity** | Output per worker per period |
| **Absenteeism** | Rate of unauthorised absence from work |

#### Labour Turnover Formula

$$
\text{Labour Turnover} = \frac{\text{Number of staff leaving in a year}}{\text{Average number of staff}} \times 100
$$

#### Causes & Effects of High Labour Turnover

| Causes | Effects on Business |
|--------|---------------------|
| Low pay / poor benefits | Increased recruitment/training costs |
| Poor management | Loss of tacit knowledge and experience |
| Lack of career progression | Lower morale among remaining staff |
| Stress / overwork | Disruption to operations and customer service |
| Better opportunities elsewhere | Reduced productivity during transition periods |

### 2.2 Motivation in Theory and Practice

#### Taylor (Scientific Management)

- **Core idea:** Workers are motivated primarily by money
- **Method:** Time-and-motion studies to find "one best way" to do each task
- **Pay system:** Piece rate (pay per unit produced)
- **Criticism:** Dehumanising; ignores social/psychological needs; creates resentment

#### Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

| Level | Need | Business Application |
|-------|------|---------------------|
| 1 | Physiological | Salary, working conditions, breaks |
| 2 | Safety | Job security, health insurance, safe workplace |
| 3 | Social | Teamwork, friendly environment, company events |
| 4 | Esteem | Recognition, promotions, job titles |
| 5 | Self-actualisation | Challenging work, autonomy, creativity |

> **Limitation:** Hierarchy may not be universal; cultural differences affect priority of needs.

#### Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory

| Hygiene Factors (prevent dissatisfaction) | Motivators (create satisfaction) |
|------------------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Salary | Achievement |
| Working conditions | Recognition |
| Company policy | Interesting work |
| Supervision | Responsibility |
| Job security | Advancement |
| Interpersonal relations | Personal growth |

**Key insight:** Improving hygiene factors prevents unhappiness but does not motivate. Motivators must be present for job satisfaction.

#### McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y

| Theory X (authoritarian) | Theory Y (participative) |
|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| Workers dislike work and avoid it | Workers view work as natural |
| Must be coerced, controlled, threatened | Self-direction and self-control |
| Prefer to be directed; avoid responsibility | Seek responsibility |
| Security needs dominate | Creativity and ingenuity widely distributed |

> **Management implication:** Theory X leads to micromanagement; Theory Y leads to delegation and empowerment.

#### Motivation in Practice

| Technique | How It Works | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| **Job enrichment** | Add challenge, autonomy, responsibility | Google 20% time for personal projects |
| **Job enlargement** | Increase variety of tasks | Cross-training employees |
| **Job rotation** | Move workers between roles | Management trainee programmes |
| **Empowerment** | Delegate decision-making authority | Zappos customer service autonomy |
| **Flexible working** | Remote work, flexitime, compressed hours | Post-COVID hybrid models |
| **Profit sharing** | Share company profits with employees | John Lewis partnership model |
| **Share ownership** | Employee stock options | Silicon Valley tech companies |

### 2.3 Organisational Structure

#### Types of Structure

| Structure | Description | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|-----------|-------------|------------|---------------|
| **Hierarchical / Tall** | Many layers of management; narrow spans of control | Clear chain of command; tight control | Slow communication; demotivating; expensive |
| **Flat** | Few layers; wide spans of control | Fast communication; empowerment; lower costs | Overloaded managers; less promotion opportunity |
| **Matrix** | Dual reporting (functional + project) | Flexibility; resource sharing; cross-functional | Confusion; conflict; power struggles |
| **Divisional** | Separate divisions by product/region/customer | Focus on specific markets; accountability | Duplication of functions; inter-division conflict |
| **Network / Virtual** | Core functions kept; others outsourced | Flexibility; access to global talent | Loss of control; coordination challenges |

#### Delegation

- **Benefits:** Develops staff; frees senior management; faster decisions; job enrichment
- **Barriers:** Fear of losing control; lack of trust; subordinate inability; preference for doing

#### Centralisation vs Decentralisation

| Centralised | Decentralised |
|-------------|---------------|
| Decisions made at top | Decisions made at lower levels |
| Consistency; control | Responsiveness; motivation |
| Suitable for crises, small firms | Suitable for large, diverse firms |

### 2.4 Leadership & Management

#### Leadership Styles

| Style | Characteristics | When Effective | When Ineffective |
|-------|----------------|----------------|------------------|
| **Autocratic** | Decisions made alone; orders given | Crisis; unskilled workers; tight deadlines | Creative tasks; skilled workers; high morale needed |
| **Democratic** | Consults team; majority vote | Complex decisions; need buy-in | Emergencies; large groups; time pressure |
| **Laissez-faire** | Hands-off; lets team decide | Highly skilled, self-motivated experts | Unskilled workers; need direction; new teams |
| **Paternalistic** | Decides but considers welfare | Traditional cultures; welfare-focused firms | Modern workforce; need for empowerment |
| **Transformational** | Inspires vision; intellectual stimulation | Change management; innovation | Stable environments; routine operations |
| **Transactional** | Clear goals; rewards/punishments | Sales teams; target-based roles | Creative work; long-term development |

#### Management vs Leadership

| Management | Leadership |
|------------|------------|
| Doing things right | Doing the right things |
| Planning, organising, controlling | Vision, inspiration, change |
| Maintains stability | Drives transformation |
| Focus on systems and processes | Focus on people and relationships |

### 2.5 Recruitment, Selection, Training, Appraisal

#### Recruitment Methods

| Internal | External |
|----------|----------|
| Promotion, transfer, job rotation | Job adverts, agencies, headhunters, online platforms |
| Cheaper; motivates staff; knows culture | Wider talent pool; fresh ideas; fills skill gaps |
| Limited pool; no new perspectives | Expensive; risky; longer adjustment |

#### Selection Methods

| Method | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|--------|-----------|------------|
| **Interviews** | Assess communication, personality | Subjective; halo effect; nervous candidates |
| **Assessment centres** | Multiple exercises; observe behaviour | Expensive; time-consuming |
| **Psychometric tests** | Objective; predict performance | Cultural bias; can be gamed |
| **Aptitude tests** | Measure specific abilities | May not reflect on-job performance |
| **References** | Verify claims; check history | May be biased; legal risks |

#### Training Methods

| On-the-Job | Off-the-Job |
|------------|-------------|
| Coaching, mentoring, job rotation, shadowing | Courses, seminars, e-learning, simulations |
| Immediate application; low cost | Broader perspective; expert knowledge |
| Risk of bad habits; disrupts operations | Transfer problem; expensive; time away |

#### Performance Appraisal Methods

- **360-degree feedback:** Peers, subordinates, managers, self
- **Management by Objectives (MBO):** Set and review specific targets
- **Behaviourally Anchored Rating Scales (BARS):** Specific behavioural examples
- **Critical Incident:** Record exceptional positive/negative events

### 3.1 Market Research

#### Primary vs Secondary Research

| Primary Research | Secondary Research |
|------------------|-------------------|
| First-hand data collection | Using existing data |
| Expensive; time-consuming | Cheap; fast; readily available |
| Specific to needs | May not match exact needs |
| Methods: surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation, experiments | Sources: government stats, trade journals, internet, company reports |

#### Sampling Methods

| Method | Description | Best Used When... |
|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Random** | Every member has equal chance | Large, homogeneous population |
| **Stratified** | Population divided into subgroups; random within each | Known subgroups with different characteristics |
| **Quota** | Interviewers select to match proportions | Quick, cost-effective representation |
| **Convenience** | Easily accessible respondents | Exploratory research; limited budget |

#### Qualitative vs Quantitative Data

| Qualitative | Quantitative |
|-------------|--------------|
| Open-ended; opinions, feelings, motivations | Numerical; counts, percentages, averages |
| Small sample; depth | Large sample; generalisability |
| Hard to analyse objectively | Statistical analysis possible |
| Focus groups, in-depth interviews | Surveys, experiments, observation |

### 3.3 Marketing Mix – 4Ps / 7Ps

#### The Extended Marketing Mix (7Ps)

| P | Focus | Key Decisions |
|---|-------|---------------|
| **Product** | What is sold | Design, features, quality, branding, packaging, USP |
| **Price** | What is charged | Pricing strategy, discounts, credit terms, psychological pricing |
| **Place** | How it reaches customers | Distribution channels, coverage, logistics, online/offline |
| **Promotion** | How customers know about it | Advertising, PR, sales promotion, personal selling, digital marketing |
| **People** | Who delivers the service | Recruitment, training, appearance, attitude |
| **Process** | How service is delivered | Systems, flow, efficiency, customer experience |
| **Physical Evidence** | Tangible cues | Environment, signage, website, uniforms, receipts |

> **Note:** 4Ps apply to products; 7Ps add People, Process, Physical Evidence for services.

#### Product

| Concept | Explanation |
|---------|-------------|
| **USP (Unique Selling Proposition)** | Feature that differentiates from competitors |
| **Product differentiation** | Making product appear distinct |
| **Brand** | Name, symbol, design that identifies a seller's product |
| **Brand equity** | Value added by brand name |
| **Brand loyalty** | Repeat purchase commitment |

**Product Portfolio Decisions:**
- **Product line extension:** New flavours/sizes of existing product
- **Product line filling:** Adding items within existing range
- **Product line stretching:** Up-market (premium) or down-market (budget) extensions
- **Diversification:** New products in new markets

#### Price

| Pricing Strategy | When Used | Example |
|-----------------|-----------|---------|
| **Penetration** | Enter market with low price to gain share | Streaming services (Netflix launch) |
| **Skimming** | High initial price for innovators; reduce later | Apple iPhone launches |
| **Competitive** | Match competitors' prices | Petrol stations, supermarkets |
| **Cost-plus** | Add markup to cost | Construction, government contracts |
| **Value-based** | Price based on perceived value | Luxury goods, consultants |
| **Psychological** | $9.99 instead of $10 | Retail, restaurants |
| **Predatory** | Temporarily below cost to eliminate rivals | Illegal in most jurisdictions |
| **Price discrimination** | Different prices for different segments | Student discounts, peak/off-peak |

#### Place (Distribution)

| Channel | Description | Example |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| **Direct** | Producer → Consumer | Dell online, farm shops |
| **Retailer** | Producer → Retailer → Consumer | Most consumer goods |
| **Wholesaler** | Producer → Wholesaler → Retailer → Consumer | Traditional grocery supply |
| **Agent/Broker** | Independent intermediary | Real estate, insurance |

**Intensive vs Selective vs Exclusive Distribution:**
- **Intensive:** Maximize outlets (convenience goods — Coca-Cola)
- **Selective:** Carefully chosen outlets (shopping goods — clothing brands)
- **Exclusive:** Limited outlets (specialty goods — Rolex, Ferrari)

#### Promotion

| Element | Description | Examples |
|---------|-------------|----------|
| **Advertising** | Paid, non-personal communication | TV, radio, online, print |
| **Sales promotion** | Short-term incentives | Discounts, BOGOF, coupons, contests |
| **Public relations** | Managing public image | Press releases, events, sponsorship |
| **Personal selling** | Face-to-face persuasion | B2B sales, car dealerships |
| **Direct marketing** | Targeted individual communication | Email, SMS, telemarketing |
| **Digital marketing** | Online channels | Social media, SEO, influencer, content |

**Above-the-line vs Below-the-line:**
- **ATL:** Mass media advertising (TV, radio, print, online display)
- **BTL:** Targeted, measurable activities (direct mail, sponsorship, social media)

### 3.4 Product Life Cycle & Boston Matrix

#### Product Life Cycle (PLC)

| Stage | Sales | Profit | Characteristics | Strategy |
|-------|-------|--------|-----------------|----------|
| **Introduction** | Low | Negative | High costs; low awareness; innovators | Heavy promotion; skimming/penetration |
| **Growth** | Rapid increase | Rising | Early adopters; competitors enter | Build brand; expand distribution |
| **Maturity** | Peak | High | Mass market; intense competition | Defend share; differentiate; efficiency |
| **Decline** | Falling | Falling | Laggards; substitutes emerge | Harvest; divest; niche |

> **Exam Tip:** Not all products follow the standard PLC. Some skip stages (fad products), some are extended (Coca-Cola), some rejuvenate (Lego after near-bankruptcy).

#### Boston Consulting Group (BCG) Matrix

| | High Market Growth | Low Market Growth |
|---|-------------------|-------------------|
| **High Market Share** | **Star** (invest) | **Cash Cow** (milk) |
| **Low Market Share** | **Question Mark** (evaluate) | **Dog** (divest) |

**Strategies:**
- **Stars:** Heavy investment to maintain/grow market share
- **Cash Cows:** Minimise investment; harvest cash to fund Stars
- **Question Marks:** Invest heavily to become Stars OR divest
- **Dogs:** Liquidate, divest, or reposition

### 4.1 Operations Management

#### Lean Production Methods

| Method | Description | Benefit |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **JIT (Just-in-Time)** | Materials arrive exactly when needed | Reduces inventory holding costs; less waste |
| **Kaizen** | Continuous improvement by all employees | Incremental gains; employee engagement |
| **Kanban** | Visual signalling system for production flow | Reduces overproduction; improves flow |
| **Andon** | Visual/audio alert system for quality issues | Immediate problem visibility |
| **Jidoka** | Automation with human oversight | Stops production when defects detected |
| **Poka-yoke** | Error-proofing mechanisms | Prevents mistakes before they occur |
| **5S** | Sort, Set, Shine, Standardise, Sustain | Organised, efficient workplace |

#### Quality Management

| Approach | Focus | Key Tools |
|----------|-------|-----------|
| **Quality Control (QC)** | Inspect output; reject defects | Sampling, testing, inspection |
| **Quality Assurance (QA)** | Build quality into processes | ISO 9001, process documentation |
| **Total Quality Management (TQM)** | Organisation-wide quality culture | Continuous improvement, customer focus |
| **Six Sigma** | Reduce defects to 3.4 per million | DMAIC (Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control) |

**Quality Costs:**
- **Prevention costs:** Training, process design, supplier evaluation
- **Appraisal costs:** Inspection, testing, audits
- **Internal failure costs:** Rework, scrap, downtime
- **External failure costs:** Returns, warranties, reputational damage

> **Exam Tip:** Prevention is cheapest. Every $1 spent on prevention saves $10 in appraisal and $100 in failure costs (quality cost ratios).

### 4.2 Capacity, Productivity, Efficiency

#### Capacity Utilisation

$$
\text{Capacity Utilisation} = \frac{\text{Actual Output}}{\text{Maximum Possible Output}} \times 100
$$

- **High utilisation (>90%):** Efficient but risky (no buffer for demand spikes)
- **Low utilisation (<70%):** Wasteful; high fixed cost per unit; may indicate overcapacity

#### Labour Productivity

$$
\text{Labour Productivity} = \frac{\text{Output per Period}}{\text{Number of Employees}}
$$

#### Methods to Improve Productivity

- Automation and technology investment
- Training and skill development
- Better motivation and job design
- Lean production techniques
- Improved logistics and supply chain

### 4.4 Scale & Economies of Scale

#### Types of Economies of Scale

| Type | Explanation | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Purchasing** | Bulk-buy discounts | Supermarkets negotiating with suppliers |
| **Technical** | Large-scale machinery more efficient per unit | Car manufacturing robots |
| **Financial** | Lower interest rates; better credit terms | Large corporations borrowing |
| **Marketing** | Spreading ad costs over more units | Global brands (Coca-Cola) |
| **Managerial** | Specialisation of management functions | Divisional specialists |
| **Risk-bearing** | Diversification across products/markets | Conglomerates |

#### Diseconomies of Scale

- **Communication breakdown** — Too many layers; slow decisions
- **Coordination problems** — Difficult to manage large, complex organisations
- **Alienation** — Employees feel disconnected; motivation falls
- **Bureaucracy** — Excessive rules and procedures

**Minimum Efficient Scale (MES):** The smallest output at which LRAC is minimised. Industries with high MES (e.g., car manufacturing) have few firms and high barriers to entry.

### 4.5 Project Management

#### Critical Path Analysis (CPA)

**Steps:**
1. List all activities and their durations
2. Identify dependencies (which activities must precede others)
3. Draw network diagram (activity-on-node or activity-on-arrow)
4. Calculate earliest start/finish times (forward pass)
5. Calculate latest start/finish times (backward pass)
6. Identify float (slack) for each activity
7. Identify critical path (zero float activities)

**Key Terms:**

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Earliest Start Time (EST)** | Earliest an activity can begin |

| **Float/Slack** | LFT − Duration − EST (spare time) |
| **Critical Path** | Longest path through network; zero float; determines minimum project duration |
| **Dummy activity** | Zero-duration activity showing logical dependency |

### 5.2 Investment Appraisal

#### Key Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Investment Appraisal** | Evaluating the financial viability of capital expenditure projects |
| **Cash Flow** | Movement of cash in and out of the business |
| **Time Value of Money** | Money today is worth more than the same amount in the future |

#### Methods of Investment Appraisal

**1. Payback Period**

$$
\text{Payback} = \text{Years before full recovery} + \frac{\text{Unrecovered cost at start of year}}{\text{Cash flow during the year}}
$$

- **Decision rule:** Accept if payback < target period
- **Advantages:** Simple; favours liquidity; useful in risky environments
- **Disadvantages:** Ignores cash flows after payback; ignores time value of money

**2. Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)**

$$
\text{ARR} = \frac{\text{Average Annual Profit}}{\text{Average Investment}} \times 100
$$

- **Decision rule:** Accept if ARR > target rate
- **Advantages:** Uses familiar profit concept; easy to calculate
- **Disadvantages:** Uses accounting profit (not cash flow); ignores timing; subjective target rate

**3. Net Present Value (NPV)**

$$
\text{NPV} = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t}
$$

Where $C_t$ = cash flow at time t, r = discount rate

- **Decision rule:** Accept if NPV > 0
- **Advantages:** Considers time value of money; absolute measure of value added; considers all cash flows
- **Disadvantages:** Requires accurate forecasts; sensitive to discount rate; ignores non-financial factors

**4. Internal Rate of Return (IRR)**

$$
\text{NPV} = 0 = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{C_t}{(1+\text{IRR})^t}
$$

- **Decision rule:** Accept if IRR > cost of capital
- **Advantages:** Considers time value; percentage is intuitive; no need to specify discount rate
- **Disadvantages:** Complex to calculate; multiple IRRs possible; reinvestment assumption may be unrealistic

#### NPV vs IRR Conflict

When projects are mutually exclusive and different scale, NPV and IRR may give different rankings. **NPV should be preferred** because it measures absolute value added to the firm.

### 5.5 Working Capital Management

#### Components of Working Capital

$$
\text{Working Capital} = \text{Current Assets} − \text{Current Liabilities}
$$

**Current Assets:** Inventory, receivables, cash, short-term investments
**Current Liabilities:** Payables, overdraft, short-term loans, accrued expenses

#### Working Capital Cycle

$$
\text{Cycle (days)} = \text{Inventory Days} + \text{Receivables Days} − \text{Payables Days}
$$

- **Shorter cycle = less cash tied up = better liquidity**
- **Too short = risk of stockouts and damaged supplier relations**

#### Strategies to Improve Working Capital

| Problem | Solution |
|---------|----------|
| Excess inventory | JIT; better forecasting; reduce SKU complexity |
| Slow collections | Early payment discounts; factoring; credit checks |
| Fast payments to suppliers | Negotiate longer terms; use trade credit strategically |
| Cash shortages | Overdraft facility; rolling cash forecast; reduce cycle |

### 6.2 Strategic Choice

#### Decision Trees

**Components:**
- **Decision nodes:** Squares; points where a choice is made
- **Chance nodes:** Circles; points where outcomes are uncertain
- **Branches:** Lines showing options or outcomes
- **Expected Values:** Calculated at chance nodes

$$
\text{Expected Value (EV)} = \sum (\text{Probability} \times \text{Payoff})
$$

**Process:**
1. Draw tree left to right
2. Assign probabilities to chance branches
3. Calculate payoffs at end points
4. Roll back: calculate EV at each chance node
5. Choose decision with highest EV

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|------------|---------------|
| Visual; logical structure | Probabilities may be subjective |
| Forces consideration of outcomes | Ignores non-financial factors |
| Quantifies risk | May be over-complex |

> **Exam Tip:** Decision trees don't capture risk attitude. A risk-averse manager may reject a high-EV option with a chance of catastrophic loss.

## WORKED EXAMPLE 1: NPV Investment Appraisal

### Scenario
A manufacturing company is considering purchasing a new machine for $200,000. The machine has a 4-year life and zero scrap value. The cost of capital is 10%. Expected cash flows:

| Year | Cash Flow |
|------|-----------|
| 0 | ($200,000) |
| 1 | $70,000 |
| 2 | $80,000 |
| 3 | $90,000 |
| 4 | $60,000 |

### Step-by-Step NPV Calculation

**Year 1:**
Discount factor = 1 / (1.10)¹ = 0.909
PV = $70,000 × 0.909 = $63,630

**Year 2:**
Discount factor = 1 / (1.10)² = 0.826
PV = $80,000 × 0.826 = $66,080

**Year 3:**
Discount factor = 1 / (1.10)³ = 0.751
PV = $90,000 × 0.751 = $67,590

**Year 4:**
Discount factor = 1 / (1.10)⁴ = 0.683
PV = $60,000 × 0.683 = $40,980

**Total NPV:**
= ($200,000) + $63,630 + $66,080 + $67,590 + $40,980
= $38,280

### Decision
Since NPV is **positive ($38,280)**, the investment should be accepted. It adds value to the business.

### Evaluation
NPV is the theoretically superior method because it:
- Accounts for time value of money
- Gives an absolute measure of value added
- Considers all cash flows over the project life

However, limitations include:
- Requires accurate cash flow forecasts (difficult in practice)
- Sensitive to the discount rate used
- Ignores non-financial factors (employee morale, environmental impact, strategic fit)
- Assumes cash flows are reinvested at the cost of capital

In reality, a company might reject this project if it has capital rationing — even with positive NPV, there may be better alternative investments.

## WORKED EXAMPLE 2: Break-Even Analysis

### Scenario
A café plans to sell coffee at $4 per cup. Variable costs are $1.50 per cup (coffee beans, milk, cup). Fixed costs are $10,000 per month (rent, salaries, utilities).

### Calculations

**Contribution per unit** = Selling Price − Variable Cost
= $4.00 − $1.50
= $2.50 per cup

**Break-even output** = Fixed Costs / Contribution per unit
= $10,000 / $2.50
= 4,000 cups per month

**Break-even revenue** = Break-even output × Selling Price
= 4,000 × $4.00
= $16,000 per month

**Margin of Safety (if actual sales = 6,000 cups):**
= (Actual Sales − Break-even Sales) / Actual Sales × 100
= (6,000 − 4,000) / 6,000 × 100
= 33.3%

**Profit at 6,000 cups:**
= Total Contribution − Fixed Costs
= (6,000 × $2.50) − $10,000
= $15,000 − $10,000
= $5,000

### What-If Analysis

| Scenario | Impact on Break-even |
|----------|---------------------|
| Rent increases by $2,000/month | Break-even rises to 4,800 cups |
| Coffee bean prices rise; VC becomes $2.00 | Contribution = $2.00; Break-even = 5,000 cups |
| Price increases to $5.00 | Contribution = $3.50; Break-even = 2,857 cups |

### Evaluation
Break-even analysis is useful for:
- Assessing risk before starting a business
- Evaluating the impact of cost/price changes
- Setting sales targets

Limitations:
- Assumes all output is sold (no inventory changes)
- Assumes constant variable cost per unit (volume discounts ignored)
- Assumes single product (more complex for multi-product firms)
- Ignores cash flow timing

## WORKED EXAMPLE 3: Ratio Analysis with Interpretation

### Company Data

| Item | 2023 | 2022 |
|------|------|------|
| Revenue | $500,000 | $450,000 |
| Gross Profit | $200,000 | $180,000 |
| Net Profit | $50,000 | $45,000 |
| Total Assets | $400,000 | $380,000 |
| Current Assets | $150,000 | $140,000 |
| Inventory | $50,000 | $45,000 |
| Receivables | $60,000 | $55,000 |
| Current Liabilities | $100,000 | $90,000 |
| Non-Current Liabilities | $150,000 | $160,000 |
| Equity | $150,000 | $130,000 |

### Calculations

**Profitability Ratios:**

Gross Profit Margin = (Gross Profit / Revenue) × 100
= ($200,000 / $500,000) × 100 = **40%** (2022: 40%)

Net Profit Margin = (Net Profit / Revenue) × 100
= ($50,000 / $500,000) × 100 = **10%** (2022: 10%)

ROCE = (Net Profit / Capital Employed) × 100
Capital Employed = Equity + NCL = $150,000 + $150,000 = $300,000
= ($50,000 / $300,000) × 100 = **16.7%** (2022: $45,000/$290,000 = 15.5%)

**Liquidity Ratios:**

Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities
= $150,000 / $100,000 = **1.5:1** (2022: 1.56:1)

Acid Test = (Current Assets − Inventory) / Current Liabilities
= ($150,000 − $50,000) / $100,000 = **1.0:1** (2022: 1.06:1)

**Efficiency Ratios:**

Inventory Days = (Inventory / COS) × 365
COS = $500,000 − $200,000 = $300,000
= ($50,000 / $300,000) × 365 = **60.8 days** (2022: 56.4 days)

Receivables Days = (Receivables / Revenue) × 365
= ($60,000 / $500,000) × 365 = **43.8 days** (2022: 44.6 days)

**Gearing:**

Gearing Ratio = (NCL / Capital Employed) × 100
= ($150,000 / $300,000) × 100 = **50%** (2022: 55.2%)

### Interpretation Paragraphs

**Profitability:** The company maintained stable gross and net profit margins at 40% and 10% respectively between 2022 and 2023. This suggests consistent pricing power and cost control. However, ROCE improved from 15.5% to 16.7%, indicating more efficient use of capital. While positive, a 16.7% ROCE should be compared to the industry average and the company's cost of capital. If the cost of capital is 12%, the company is creating shareholder value. If it's 18%, the company is destroying value despite positive accounting profit.

**Liquidity:** The current ratio of 1.5:1 is generally considered adequate (ideal range 1.5-2.0). The acid test of exactly 1.0:1 is on the lower side of the recommended 1:1 minimum. This means the company relies on selling inventory to meet short-term obligations. If inventory becomes obsolete or demand falls, the company could face liquidity problems. The slight decline from 2022 suggests working capital management needs attention.

**Efficiency:** Inventory holding period increased from 56.4 to 60.8 days. This is concerning — inventory is sitting longer, tying up cash and increasing holding costs (storage, insurance, obsolescence risk). Possible causes: overstocking, declining sales, or supply chain issues. Receivables days improved slightly from 44.6 to 43.8 days, suggesting marginally better credit control. However, 43.8 days is relatively long — if credit terms are 30 days, the company is allowing customers to pay late, which strains cash flow.

**Gearing:** The gearing ratio improved from 55.2% to 50%, indicating reduced financial risk. Lower gearing means less interest obligation and reduced vulnerability to interest rate rises. However, some gearing is beneficial due to tax relief on interest (debt is cheaper than equity). The optimal gearing level depends on industry norms, interest rate environment, and the company's cash flow stability. A 50% gearing ratio might be appropriate for a stable manufacturing firm but risky for a cyclical business.

**Overall Assessment:** The company shows stable profitability and improving capital efficiency, but liquidity and inventory management need attention. The reduction in gearing is positive for risk management. Stakeholders should monitor inventory levels and receivables collection closely.

## DETAILED CASE STUDY 1: Apple Inc. – Product Portfolio & BCG Matrix

### Background
Apple Inc. is one of the world's most valuable companies, with a product portfolio spanning iPhones, MacBooks, iPads, Apple Watch, AirPods, and services (Apple Music, iCloud, App Store).

### Applying the BCG Matrix to Apple (2024)

| Product | Category | Evidence | Strategic Implication |
|---------|----------|----------|----------------------|
| **iPhone** | Cash Cow | Mature smartphone market; ~50% of Apple's revenue; high market share in premium segment; generates massive cash flows | Defend market share; incremental innovation; fund R&D for new products |
| **Services (App Store, iCloud, Apple Music)** | Star | High growth (15-20% annually); increasing market share in digital services; high profit margins | Invest heavily; expand ecosystem; lock-in customers |
| **Apple Vision Pro** | Question Mark | New product category (spatial computing); uncertain market size; high development costs ($3,499 price point) | Either invest massively to build market or divest if adoption is slow |
| **iPod (discontinued 2022)** | Dog (was Cash Cow) | Declining MP3 market; overtaken by streaming; minimal contribution to revenue | Correctly divested; resources reallocated to growing segments |

### Analysis: Why Apple's Portfolio Works
Apple's portfolio demonstrates **balanced diversification**. The iPhone (Cash Cow) generates the cash needed to fund Stars (Services) and Question Marks (Vision Pro). This is textbook BCG strategy — using mature products to fund future growth.

However, critics argue Apple is over-dependent on the iPhone (~50% of revenue). If smartphone sales decline (as seen in 2023 with global slowdown), Apple's entire ecosystem weakens. This is a risk the BCG Matrix highlights — over-reliance on one Cash Cow creates vulnerability.

**Exam Evaluation Point:** The BCG Matrix is useful for portfolio analysis but has limitations. It oversimplifies by using only two variables (market growth and relative market share). In reality, products like the Apple Watch started as Question Marks but created their own sub-markets — the Matrix doesn't capture this dynamic evolution well.

## DETAILED CASE STUDY 2: McDonald's – Franchising Strategy & Global Expansion

### Background
McDonald's operates ~40,000 restaurants in 100+ countries. 93% are franchised — one of the highest franchise ratios in the fast-food industry.

### Why McDonald's Uses Franchising (Theory Applied)

**1. Rapid Expansion Without Capital Constraints**
- Each restaurant costs ~$2-3 million to build
- With 93% franchised, McDonald's Corporation only owns ~2,800 restaurants
- Franchisees provide the capital, allowing McDonald's to grow much faster than if it funded everything internally
- In 2023, McDonald's opened 1,500+ new restaurants globally — impossible with wholly-owned expansion

**2. Local Market Knowledge**
- Franchisees understand local tastes, regulations, and labour markets
- Example: McDonald's India offers McAloo Tikki (potato burger) — developed by local franchisees who understood vegetarian preferences
- Example: In Japan, McDonald's serves Teriyaki McBurger and Seaweed Shaker Fries

**3. Motivated Owner-Operators**
- Franchisees invest their own money, creating stronger incentive to maximise profit than salaried managers
- This addresses the **principal-agent problem** — the franchisee (agent) has aligned incentives with McDonald's (principal)

### Drawbacks of McDonald's Franchising Model

| Issue | Evidence | Impact |
|-------|----------|--------|
| Quality control | 2014 Chinese supplier scandal (expired meat) | Reputational damage; difficult to monitor 37,000+ franchisees |
| Profit sharing | McDonald's takes 4-5% royalty + rent | Lower per-unit profit than company-owned stores |
| Contract disputes | Franchisee associations frequently lobby corporate | Tensions over pricing, remodel requirements, technology fees |

### Evaluation
Franchising has been the correct strategy for McDonald's given its global scale ambitions. However, the 2014 scandal shows that **control mechanisms** (audits, mystery shoppers, supply chain oversight) are essential. In markets where quality is paramount (e.g., Japan), McDonald's actually owns more stores to maintain tighter control — demonstrating that the optimal strategy depends on market context.

## DETAILED CASE STUDY 3: Tesla – Vertical Integration vs Outsourcing

### Background
Tesla is unusually vertically integrated for a car manufacturer. It designs its own batteries, chips (Full Self-Driving computer), and software. Most automakers outsource these components.

### Tesla's Vertical Integration Strategy

**1. Battery Production (Gigafactories)**
- Tesla partnered with Panasonic but increasingly produces its own 4680 battery cells
- Reason: Battery costs are ~30-40% of an EV's total cost. Controlling this reduces costs and secures supply.
- In 2023, Tesla's battery cost per kWh fell below $100 — industry-leading

**2. Software & Chips**
- Tesla develops its own FSD (Full Self-Driving) chips instead of using NVIDIA or Mobileye
- This gives Tesla full control over the autonomous driving stack
- Competitors like VW and GM rely on third-party suppliers, creating dependency

**3. Direct Sales (No Dealerships)**
- Tesla sells directly to consumers, bypassing traditional dealer networks
- This captures the dealer margin (~8-10% of vehicle price) and controls customer experience

### Comparison: Tesla vs Traditional Automakers

| Aspect | Tesla (Vertically Integrated) | Toyota (More Outsourced) |
|--------|------------------------------|--------------------------|
| Battery | In-house + partners | Buys from CATL, BYD, Panasonic |
| Chips | Custom-designed (FSD) | Uses NVIDIA, Mobileye |
| Sales | Direct online + showrooms | Franchise dealer network |
| Software | Fully integrated | Fragmented (supplier-dependent) |
| Advantage | Cost control, speed of innovation, data ownership | Flexibility, lower capital intensity, proven reliability |

### Evaluation
Tesla's vertical integration has been a competitive advantage — it enabled rapid innovation and cost reduction. However, it requires massive capital expenditure (Gigafactories cost $5-10 billion each) and carries risk if technology shifts (e.g., solid-state batteries could make Tesla's 4680 investment obsolete). Toyota's more outsourced approach is lower-risk but slower to adapt. The optimal degree of vertical integration depends on industry maturity, capital availability, and competitive pressure.

**"Evaluate the view that profit maximisation is the most important objective for all businesses."**

### Introduction
Profit maximisation — producing where marginal revenue equals marginal cost — is traditionally viewed as the primary business objective. However, whether it is the MOST important objective depends on factors including business size, ownership structure, market conditions, and stakeholder pressures. This essay will argue that while profit is essential for survival, it is not always the most important objective.

### Point 1: Arguments FOR Profit Maximisation
Profit maximisation is crucial for several reasons. First, profit provides the return on investment that justifies risk-taking by entrepreneurs and shareholders. Without the prospect of profit, capital would not flow to businesses, and economic activity would stall. Second, retained profit is the cheapest source of finance for expansion — firms with strong profits can fund growth without resorting to expensive debt or equity dilution. Third, in competitive markets, firms that do not maximise profit risk being outcompeted and eventually driven out of business. For example, budget airlines like Ryanair operate on razor-thin margins and must constantly minimise costs to survive. For publicly-listed companies, failure to deliver profits leads to share price declines, activist investor intervention, and potential takeover.

### Point 2: Arguments AGAINST Profit Maximisation
However, profit maximisation has significant limitations. First, the theory assumes perfect information and ignores the complexity of modern corporations. In reality, managers (who run the firm) may pursue satisficing rather than maximising, as they prioritise job security, empire-building, or work-life balance over shareholder returns — the principal-agent problem. Second, many businesses prioritise other objectives. Social enterprises explicitly prioritise social impact over profit. Start-ups often prioritise growth and market share over immediate profitability (Amazon was unprofitable for years while building scale). Third, excessive focus on short-term profit can damage long-term value. Cutting R&D or employee training boosts current profits but erodes future competitiveness. Finally, stakeholder theory argues that businesses have responsibilities to employees, customers, suppliers, and communities — not just shareholders. During COVID-19, many firms prioritised employee retention over profit, recognising that their workforce was a long-term asset.

### Point 3: "It Depends" — Contextual Factors
Whether profit maximisation is the most important objective depends on context. For small owner-managed firms, profit is likely paramount — the owner's income depends directly on it. For large public companies, shareholder value maximisation (which includes long-term share price growth, not just current profit) may be more appropriate. For firms in highly competitive markets, survival (breaking even) may take priority. For firms in monopoly positions, profit maximisation is more achievable but may attract regulatory intervention. The time horizon also matters: in a recession, short-term survival trumps profit; in a growth phase, market share may be prioritised.

### Conclusion
In conclusion, profit maximisation remains an important objective — without profit, businesses cannot survive or grow. However, it is not universally the MOST important objective. Modern business theory and practice recognise that stakeholder interests, long-term sustainability, and contextual factors all influence objective-setting. The most successful businesses often balance profit with purpose, recognising that long-term value creation requires investment in people, innovation, and relationships — not just short-term profit extraction.

## SINGAPORE CONTEXT: MAS Exchange Rate Policy

### Background
Unlike most central banks that use interest rates, the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) manages monetary policy through exchange rate adjustments. The SGD is managed against a trade-weighted basket of currencies.

### How MAS Policy Works

**Policy Band:**
- SGD managed against undisclosed trade-weighted basket
- MAS sets a "policy band" — allows SGD to fluctuate within a range
- Band can be shifted up (appreciation), down (depreciation), or steepened

**2022-2023 Inflation Response:**
- Global inflation surged due to supply chain disruptions, energy prices
- MAS tightened policy THREE times in 2022:
  1. January: Re-centred band upward
  2. April: Steepened slope of appreciation
  3. October: Further steepened slope
- This allowed faster SGD appreciation, making imports cheaper

### Impact on Businesses

| Stakeholder | Impact of SGD Appreciation |
|------------|---------------------------|
| Consumers | Cheaper imports → lower inflation (helps purchasing power) |
| Exporters | More expensive overseas → reduced competitiveness (hurts) |
| Importers | Cheaper costs → higher profit margins or lower prices (helps) |
| Tourism | Stronger SGD makes Singapore more expensive for tourists (hurts hospitality) |
| Investors | Stronger currency signals confidence → may attract investment |

### Evaluation
MAS policy has been effective in controlling imported inflation — Singapore's inflation peaked at ~7% in 2022 but was lower than many peers. However, export-oriented sectors (electronics, chemicals) faced margin pressure. The policy assumes that inflation is primarily imported rather than domestically generated. If domestic demand surges, exchange rate policy alone may be insufficient — fiscal tightening might also be needed.
