IELTS Speaking Band 9 Guide

Step-by-step model answers with annotations for Part 1, 2 & 3

Part 1 Part 2 Part 3 Marking Criteria

Part 1: Introduction & Interview 4-5 minutes

The examiner asks 2-3 sets of questions on familiar topics. Answers should be 2-4 sentences — not too short, not a monologue.

Topic: Work / Studies

Q1: Do you work or are you a student?

Q2: What do you enjoy most about your job/studies?

Q3: Would you like to change your job/course in the future?

Strategy for Band 9 Part 1

  1. Answer directly first — don't waffle
  2. Add one piece of detail or explanation
  3. Include a personal feeling or opinion
  4. Use natural fillers ("I suppose," "to be honest") — but sparingly
  5. Show some range: past, present, future, conditional

Band 9 Model Answers

Q1: I'm currently working as a software developer for a fintech startup here in Singapore. I've been with the company for just over two years now, and it's been an incredibly steep learning curve.

Q2: What I find most rewarding is the problem-solving aspect — there's something genuinely satisfying about writing code that streamlines complex financial processes. That said, the collaborative environment is equally important to me; bouncing ideas off my teammates often leads to solutions I wouldn't have reached independently.

Q3: That's an interesting question. I don't have any immediate plans to switch careers, but I'm certainly open to specialising in artificial intelligence further down the line. I reckon the skills I'm acquiring now would transfer quite naturally into that field, so it's very much a possibility rather than a certainty.

Why This Scores Band 9 — Fluency & Coherence

  • Answers flow naturally with no unnatural pauses or hesitations
  • Ideas are connected logically: "That said," "equally important," "so it's"
  • Each answer is extended without being memorised or robotic
  • Direct answer first, then elaboration — clear structure

Why This Scores Band 9 — Lexical Resource

  • steep learning curve — natural collocation
  • rewarding — precise emotional vocabulary
  • streamlines complex financial processes — topic-specific, sophisticated
  • bouncing ideas off — natural phrasal verb
  • further down the line — natural idiom for future
  • transfer quite naturally into — sophisticated collocation
  • Variety: "incredibly," "genuinely," "equally," "certainly" — adverbs used precisely

Why This Scores Band 9 — Grammatical Range

  • Present continuous: "I'm currently working"
  • Present perfect: "I've been with," "I've acquired"
  • Relative clause: "code that streamlines"
  • Conditional: "would transfer," "wouldn't have reached"
  • Passive meaning: "processes that are streamlined"
  • Complex noun phrases: "the problem-solving aspect," "the collaborative environment"

Part 2: Individual Long Turn 3-4 minutes

You receive a cue card, have 1 minute to prepare notes, then speak for 1-2 minutes.

Cue Card

Describe a time when you had to make a difficult decision.

You should say:

  • what the decision was
  • when and where you made it
  • why it was difficult
  • and explain how you felt after making the decision.

Step 1: Note-Taking Strategy (1 minute)

Don't write full sentences. Use keywords and connectors:

  • Decision: resign from stable job → startup
  • When/Where: March 2023, apartment, midnight
  • Why difficult: financial security vs passion, family expectations
  • Feelings after: initially terrified, then liberated, no regrets

Step 2: Structure Your Talk (PEEP)

  1. Point — State the decision clearly
  2. Explain — Give context and background
  3. Example — Specific details (when, where, who)
  4. Personal — Your feelings and reflection

Band 9 Model Answer (1 minute 45 seconds spoken)

I'd like to talk about a decision I made roughly two years ago, which was whether to resign from my well-established position at a multinational corporation to join a fledgling startup.

I found myself grappling with this dilemma in March 2023. I distinctly remember pacing around my apartment at around midnight, pros and cons list in hand, knowing that the deadline for giving notice was the following morning. The setting itself was rather surreal — the city was dead silent, yet my mind was racing at a hundred miles an hour.

What made this decision particularly agonising was the sheer contrast between the two paths. On one hand, my corporate job offered financial security, a clear promotion trajectory, and the approval of my family, who had always valued stability above all else. On the other hand, the startup represented everything I was genuinely passionate about — innovative technology, creative autonomy, and the possibility of building something from the ground up. It felt as though I was being torn between prudence and ambition.

Ultimately, I took the leap. The immediate aftermath was a peculiar mixture of terror and exhilaration. For the first fortnight, I woke up every morning with a knot in my stomach, questioning whether I'd made an enormous mistake. However, as the weeks passed and I began contributing meaningfully to the startup's growth, that anxiety gradually transformed into a profound sense of fulfilment. Looking back now, I can say with absolute conviction that it was the most liberating decision I've ever made, and I haven't regretted it for a single moment.

Why This Scores Band 9 — Fluency & Coherence

  • Continuous speech for nearly 2 minutes with no unnatural pauses
  • Clear chronological structure: setup → moment of decision → difficulty → aftermath
  • Signposting: "I'd like to talk about," "What made this particularly agonising," "Ultimately," "However," "Looking back now"
  • Each bullet point from the cue card is addressed fully and naturally

Why This Scores Band 9 — Lexical Resource

  • fledgling startup — precise adjective
  • grappling with this dilemma — sophisticated verb phrase
  • pros and cons list — natural collocation
  • racing at a hundred miles an hour — idiomatic expression
  • agonising — precise emotional adjective
  • prudence and ambition — sophisticated abstract nouns
  • profound sense of fulfilment — natural collocation
  • absolute conviction — strong, natural phrase
  • Variety: "torn between," "took the leap," "knot in my stomach" — idiomatic richness

Why This Scores Band 9 — Grammatical Range

  • Past continuous: "was pacing," "was racing"
  • Past perfect: "had always valued"
  • Passive: "was being torn"
  • Conditional (implied): "whether I'd made"
  • Participle clause: "knowing that the deadline"
  • Cleft structure: "What made this... was"
  • Comparative: "the most liberating decision"
  • Time clauses: "as the weeks passed," "Looking back now"

Part 3: Two-Way Discussion 4-5 minutes

Abstract, analytical questions related to Part 2 topic. This is where Band 9 candidates shine through critical thinking and sophisticated language.

Questions (following Part 2 about decisions)

Q1: Why do some people find it harder than others to make decisions?

Q2: Do you think it's better to make decisions quickly or take time to consider all options?

Q3: How has the internet changed the way people make decisions?

Strategy for Band 9 Part 3

  1. Answer the question directly but with nuance — avoid absolute statements
  2. Provide a general reason, then a specific example or scenario
  3. Use hedging language: "tend to," "generally speaking," "in most cases"
  4. Contrast different perspectives: "On the one hand... on the other"
  5. Speculate about future trends or broader implications

Band 9 Model Answers

Q1: I suppose it largely boils down to a combination of personality traits and upbringing. Individuals who've been sheltered from failure throughout childhood often develop what psychologists call "decision paralysis" — they simply haven't built the resilience to cope with suboptimal outcomes. Conversely, people raised in environments where autonomy was encouraged from an early age tend to trust their judgment more readily. That said, I think the magnitude of the decision also plays a pivotal role; someone might breeze through minor choices yet crumble when the stakes are genuinely high.

Q2: That's a nuanced issue. On balance, I'd argue that the optimal approach depends entirely on the nature and consequences of the decision at hand. For trivial matters — say, choosing what to have for lunch — deliberation is arguably a waste of cognitive resources. However, when we're dealing with life-altering choices, such as career transitions or major investments, a methodical evaluation process is absolutely paramount. Interestingly, research suggests that excessive deliberation can actually degrade decision quality due to something called "analysis paralysis," where overthinking leads to missed opportunities.

Q3: The internet has fundamentally transformed decision-making, though not always for the better, in my view. On the positive side, unprecedented access to information empowers consumers to compare products, read reviews, and make evidence-based choices that would have been impossible two decades ago. Yet there's a darker undercurrent: the algorithmic curation of content often traps people in echo chambers, reinforcing existing biases rather than exposing them to diverse perspectives. Moreover, the sheer volume of available information can be overwhelming, leading some individuals to outsource their decisions entirely to influencers or AI recommendations, which I find rather alarming.

Why This Scores Band 9 — Fluency & Coherence

  • Each answer is 4-6 sentences of continuous, coherent speech
  • Clear paragraph-like structure within spoken answers
  • Natural signposting: "On the positive side," "Yet there's a darker undercurrent," "Moreover"
  • Ideas build on each other: general → specific → counterpoint → conclusion

Why This Scores Band 9 — Lexical Resource

  • decision paralysis — technical term used naturally
  • suboptimal outcomes — sophisticated academic phrase
  • cognitive resources — precise academic collocation
  • analysis paralysis — natural advanced term
  • echo chambers — contemporary, relevant vocabulary
  • algorithmic curation — topic-specific, advanced
  • outsourcing decisions — metaphorical use
  • Hedging language: "largely," "tend to," "arguably," "in my view" — sophisticated tentativeness

Why This Scores Band 9 — Grammatical Range

  • Passive voice: "been sheltered," "been encouraged," "traps people"
  • Noun clauses: "what psychologists call," "that excessive deliberation can"
  • Conditionals: "might breeze through," "would have been impossible"
  • Participles: "choosing what to have," "overthinking leads to"
  • Comparative structures: "harder than others," "more readily"
  • Cleft sentence: "it's largely... that"
  • Complex embedding without loss of clarity
