IBEN1: Language & Literature

Overview

Readers, Writers and Texts (Topic 1) is the conceptual foundation of IB English Literature and Language & Literature. It explores how meaning is constructed through the interaction between authorial intention, textual features, and reader response. This topic underpins Paper 1 (unseen analysis), the Individual Oral (IO), and the Higher Level Essay (HLE).

1.1 Textual Analysis Fundamentals

IB English assessment rewards close reading — the detailed examination of how language and structure create meaning.

Levels of Analysis

  • Word level: diction, connotation, denotation, register, jargon, neologism.
  • Sentence level: syntax, sentence length, punctuation effects, parallelism, antithesis.
  • Textual level: narrative perspective, structure, genre conventions, intertextuality.

1.2 Audience & Purpose

  • Purpose: to inform, persuade, entertain, reflect, challenge, celebrate.
  • Audience: intended readership affects tone, register, and assumed knowledge.
  • Context: historical, cultural, and situational circumstances shaping production and reception.

Command Terms for Analysis

Analyse: break down into components and examine relationships.
Evaluate: assess strengths and limitations with evidence.
Compare: identify similarities and differences.

1.3 Stylistic Features

Literary Devices

  • Imagery: visual, auditory, olfactory, gustatory, tactile, kinaesthetic.
  • Metaphor & simile: figurative comparisons — metaphor is direct; simile uses "like" or "as."
  • Symbolism: an object or action representing a deeper meaning.
  • Irony: verbal (saying the opposite), dramatic (audience knows more), situational (outcome differs from expectation).
  • Pathetic fallacy: nature reflecting human emotions.

Narrative Techniques

  • First person: subjective, immediate, limited perspective.
  • Third person limited: follows one character's thoughts.
  • Third person omniscient: all-knowing narrator.
  • Unreliable narrator: credibility is questionable — creates dramatic irony.
  • Stream of consciousness: interior monologue mimicking thought processes.

1.4 Intertextuality

Texts do not exist in isolation — they draw on, allude to, and reshape prior texts and cultural knowledge.

  • Allusion: reference to another text, event, or figure (e.g., biblical, classical, historical).
  • Pastiche/parody: imitation for homage or mockery.
  • Intertextual rewriting: retelling stories from new perspectives (e.g., Wide Sargasso Sea / Jane Eyre).

1.5 Paper 1 Skills (Unseen Analysis)

  • Read the text twice — first for overall impression, second for detail.
  • Annotate: underline key phrases, circle literary devices, note shifts in tone.
  • Structure your response: introduction (thesis), body paragraphs (point-evidence-analysis), conclusion.
  • Always link technique to meaning — do not simply list devices.

Common Misconceptions

  • Technique spotting: Identifying a simile is not analysis — explaining its effect on meaning is.
  • Authorial intention: Focus on what the text does, not what the author "meant."
  • Reader response: Personal reactions must be grounded in textual evidence, not unsupported opinion.
  • Context: Historical context enhances analysis but should not dominate — the text itself is primary.

Practice Questions

  • Analyse how a writer uses setting to reflect a character's internal state.
  • Compare the effects of first-person and third-person narration in two texts you have studied.
  • Explain how intertextual references enrich the meaning of a literary work.
  • Evaluate the significance of an unreliable narrator in shaping reader response.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Close reading: word, sentence, and textual levels.
  • Always link technique to meaning — avoid technique spotting.
  • Audience, purpose, and context shape textual production and reception.
  • Key devices: imagery, metaphor, symbolism, irony, pathetic fallacy.
  • Narrative perspective determines what the reader knows and feels.
  • Intertextuality: texts connect to other texts and cultural knowledge.

Exam Tips

  • Read unseen texts twice before writing.
  • Plan your response — 5 minutes of planning saves 15 minutes of rewriting.
  • Use PEEL structure: Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link.
  • Integrate quotations smoothly — avoid dropped quotes.
  • In evaluation, weigh strengths against limitations with textual support.
