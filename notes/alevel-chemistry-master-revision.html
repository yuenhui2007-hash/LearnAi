C1: Atomic Structure

IGCSE Content

The Structure of the Atom

An atom consists of a central nucleus containing protons and neutrons, with electrons arranged in shells orbiting the nucleus. The key subatomic particles have the following properties:

  • Proton (p+): Relative mass = 1, Relative charge = +1, Located in nucleus
  • Neutron (n0): Relative mass = 1, Relative charge = 0, Located in nucleus
  • Electron (e-): Relative mass = 1/1840 (negligible), Relative charge = -1, Located in shells

Atomic Number and Mass Number

Atomic Number (Z) = Number of protons = Number of electrons (in neutral atom)
Mass Number (A) = Number of protons + Number of neutrons
Number of neutrons = A - Z

Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. They have the same atomic number but different mass numbers. Example: Carbon-12 (6p, 6n), Carbon-13 (6p, 7n), Carbon-14 (6p, 8n).

Relative Atomic Mass (Ar) is the weighted average mass of an atom of an element compared to 1/12 the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

Ar = Σ(isotope mass × relative abundance) / 100

Electronic Configuration

Electrons fill shells according to rules:

  • First shell: maximum 2 electrons
  • Second shell: maximum 8 electrons
  • Third shell: maximum 8 electrons (IGCSE level) or 18 (full capacity)
  • Fourth shell: maximum 18 electrons (full capacity)

Examples: Na (2.8.1), Mg (2.8.2), Cl (2.8.7), Ar (2.8.8)

Ions

Ions are charged particles formed when atoms gain or lose electrons. Metals lose electrons to form positive ions (cations). Non-metals gain electrons to form negative ions (anions).

📌 Exam Tip

Remember: Group number = number of electrons in outer shell for main group elements. The group number also tells you the charge of the ion formed (with sign reversed for non-metals).

AS Level Content

Atomic Orbitals and Quantum Mechanics

At AS level, electrons are described as occupying orbitals rather than shells. An orbital is a region in space where there is a high probability of finding an electron.

  • s orbital: spherical shape, holds max 2 electrons
  • p orbitals: dumbbell shape, 3 orientations (px, py, pz), hold max 6 electrons total
  • d orbitals: complex shapes, 5 orientations, hold max 10 electrons total

Subshells and Energy Levels

Each principal energy level (shell) n contains subshells:

  • n = 1: 1s only
  • n = 2: 2s, 2p
  • n = 3: 3s, 3p, 3d
  • n = 4: 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f

Aufbau Principle and Filling Order

Electrons fill orbitals in order of increasing energy:

1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s → 4f → 5d → 6p

Remember: 4s fills before 3d due to lower energy. This is crucial for transition metal configurations.

Hund's Rule and Pauli Exclusion Principle

  • Pauli Exclusion Principle: Each orbital can hold a maximum of 2 electrons with opposite spins (↑↓)
  • Hund's Rule: Electrons occupy degenerate orbitals (same energy) singly with parallel spins before pairing

Ionisation Energy

First Ionisation Energy is the energy required to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.

X(g) → X+(g) + e-

Factors affecting ionisation energy:

  • Nuclear charge: Higher nuclear charge → higher IE
  • Distance from nucleus: Greater distance → lower IE
  • Shielding: More inner electrons → more shielding → lower IE

Trends in Ionisation Energy

Across a period (left to right): IE generally increases due to increasing nuclear charge and same shielding.

Down a group: IE decreases due to increased distance and shielding.

Exceptions to note:

  • Group 2 to Group 3 (e.g., Mg to Al): IE drops slightly as electron removed from higher energy p orbital
  • Group 5 to Group 6 (e.g., N to O): IE drops due to electron-electron repulsion in paired p orbital

Evidence for Shell Structure from Successive Ionisation Energies

Large jumps in successive ionisation energies indicate removal of an electron from a new, inner shell. This provides evidence for the shell model of the atom.

Mass Spectrometry

A mass spectrometer determines relative atomic masses and is used to:

  • Calculate Ar from isotope data
  • Identify elements in a sample
  • Determine molecular masses (at A2)

Stages: Vaporisation → Ionisation → Acceleration → Deflection → Detection

The degree of deflection depends on mass-to-charge ratio (m/z). Lighter ions are deflected more.

A2 Level Content

Electronic Configurations of Transition Metal Ions

Transition metals lose 4s electrons before 3d electrons when forming ions:

  • Fe: [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s² → Fe²⁺: [Ar] 3d⁶ → Fe³⁺: [Ar] 3d⁵
  • Mn: [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s² → Mn²⁺: [Ar] 3d⁵
  • Cr: [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ (exception for stability of half-filled d subshell)
  • Cu: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ (exception for stability of full d subshell)

Relative Molecular Mass and Fragmentation

Mass spectrometry of organic compounds gives:

  • Molecular ion peak (M+): m/z = Mr of the compound
  • Fragmentation peaks: characteristic fragments that help identify structure

Common fragment ions:

CH₃⁺ (m/z = 15)   CH₂=OH⁺ (m/z = 31)   C₆H₅⁺ (m/z = 77)
CH₃CO⁺ (m/z = 43)   COOH⁺ (m/z = 45)

High Resolution Mass Spectrometry

High resolution MS can distinguish between ions with the same nominal mass but different exact masses (based on precise atomic masses). For example, CO (28.0101), N₂ (28.0134), and C₂H₄ (28.0313) can be distinguished.

  • n = mass / Mᵣ
  • n = concentration × volume (dm³)
  • 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³
  • % yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100

Last-Minute Summary

🎯 Key Points to Remember

  • Protons = atomic number; neutrons = mass number - atomic number
  • Isotopes: same proton number, different neutron number
  • Electron configuration order: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p...
  • Cr = [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu = [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ (exceptions)
  • IE increases across period, decreases down group
  • Drops at Group 2→3 and Group 5→6 due to subshell change and pairing repulsion
  • Successive IE jumps indicate new shell being broken into
  • Mass spec: molecular ion = Mr; fragmentation helps identify structure
  • Transition metal ions lose 4s before 3d electrons

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Confusing atomic number and mass number
  • Writing 3d before 4s in electron configurations
  • Forgetting Hund's rule when filling p orbitals
  • Not explaining shielding AND distance when comparing IE
  • Forgetting that 4s fills before 3d but empties before 3d in ions

C2: Chemical Bonding

IGCSE Content

Ionic Bonding

Ionic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. It typically occurs between metals and non-metals. Metals lose electrons to form cations (+); non-metals gain electrons to form anions (-).

Dot-and-cross diagrams: Show outer shell electrons only. Electrons from one atom are shown as dots, from the other as crosses.

Examples: NaCl, MgCl₂, CaO, Na₂O

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻
Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl (ionic lattice)

Covalent Bonding

Covalent bonding involves the sharing of electron pairs between atoms. It typically occurs between non-metal atoms. A single covalent bond = 1 shared pair; double bond = 2 shared pairs; triple bond = 3 shared pairs.

Examples: H₂ (single), O₂ (double), N₂ (triple), CO₂ (double), H₂O, CH₄, NH₃

Coordinate (dative) covalent bond: Both electrons in the shared pair come from the same atom. Examples: NH₄⁺, H₃O⁺, Al₂Cl₆

Metallic Bonding

Metallic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and a "sea" of delocalised electrons. This explains why metals:

  • Conduct electricity (delocalised electrons are mobile)
  • Are malleable and ductile (ions can slide past each other)
  • Have high melting points (strong electrostatic forces)

Diamond: Each carbon bonded to 4 others in tetrahedral arrangement. Very hard, high mp, non-conducting.

Graphite: Layers of hexagonal rings. Each carbon bonded to 3 others. Layered structure, conducts electricity (delocalised electrons between layers), soft/slippery.

Graphene: Single layer of graphite. Exceptional strength and conductivity.

AS Level Content

Bond Polarity and Electronegativity

Electronegativity is the ability of an atom to attract the bonding electrons in a covalent bond. Values range from 0.7 (Fr) to 4.0 (F) on the Pauling scale.

Bond polarity: If two atoms have different electronegativities, the bond is polar. The more electronegative atom carries a partial negative charge (δ-), the other δ+.

Bond polarity = |χ₁ - χ₂|
0.0 - 0.4: non-polar covalent
0.4 - 1.7: polar covalent
> 1.7: ionic

Polar Molecules vs Polar Bonds

A molecule can have polar bonds but be non-polar overall if the bond dipoles cancel due to symmetry. Examples:

  • CO₂: polar bonds but linear → non-polar molecule
  • H₂O: polar bonds, bent shape → polar molecule
  • CCl₄: polar bonds, tetrahedral → non-polar molecule
  • NH₃: polar bonds, trigonal pyramidal → polar molecule

Intermolecular Forces

Van der Waals forces (London dispersion forces): Temporary induced dipoles. Present in all molecules. Strength increases with number of electrons (molecular size).

Permanent dipole-dipole forces: Between polar molecules. Additional to VdW.

Hydrogen bonding: Special strong dipole-dipole interaction. Occurs when H is bonded to N, O, or F and interacts with lone pairs on N, O, or F of another molecule.

Order of strength: Hydrogen bond > Permanent dipole-dipole > Van der Waals

Effect of Hydrogen Bonding on Physical Properties

  • Higher boiling points than expected (H₂O, NH₃, HF anomalously high)
  • Ice is less dense than water (hydrogen bonds hold molecules in open lattice)
  • Water has high surface tension and specific heat capacity

Shapes of Molecules (VSEPR Theory)

Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion theory states that electron pairs around a central atom arrange themselves to minimise repulsion.

  • 2 bond pairs: linear (180°) e.g., CO₂, BeCl₂
  • 3 bond pairs: trigonal planar (120°) e.g., BF₃, AlCl₃
  • 4 bond pairs: tetrahedral (109.5°) e.g., CH₄, CCl₄
  • 3 bond pairs + 1 lone pair: trigonal pyramidal (~107°) e.g., NH₃
  • 2 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs: bent (~104.5°) e.g., H₂O
  • 5 bond pairs: trigonal bipyramidal (90°, 120°) e.g., PCl₅
  • 6 bond pairs: octahedral (90°) e.g., SF₆

Lone pair-bond pair repulsion > bond pair-bond pair repulsion, so angles decrease when lone pairs present.

Bond Energy and Bond Length

Bond energy is the energy required to break one mole of a particular bond in gaseous molecules. Stronger bonds have higher bond energies and shorter bond lengths.

Trends: triple > double > single bond in strength. Shorter bonds are stronger.

A2 Level Content

Hybridisation

Hybridisation explains molecular geometry by mixing atomic orbitals:

  • sp: linear (e.g., C₂H₂, BeCl₂) — 1 s + 1 p orbital
  • sp²: trigonal planar (e.g., C₂H₄, BF₃) — 1 s + 2 p orbitals
  • sp³: tetrahedral (e.g., CH₄, NH₃, H₂O) — 1 s + 3 p orbitals

In ethene, the C=C double bond consists of one σ (sigma) bond from sp² overlap and one π (pi) bond from unhybridised p orbital overlap.

Sigma and Pi Bonds

  • Sigma (σ) bonds: formed by head-on overlap of orbitals. All single bonds are σ. Present in all multiple bonds too.
  • Pi (π) bonds: formed by sideways overlap of p orbitals. One π in a double bond, two π in a triple bond.

σ bonds are stronger than π bonds. π bonds prevent rotation about double bonds, leading to cis-trans isomerism.

Delocalisation and Resonance

In molecules like benzene (C₆H₆), electrons are delocalised over all six carbon atoms in a ring. This gives:

  • Greater stability (delocalisation energy)
  • Equal C-C bond lengths (intermediate between single and double)
  • Resistance to addition reactions (unlike alkenes)

Resonance structures are different Lewis representations of the same delocalised system.

Last-Minute Summary

🎯 Key Points to Remember

  • Ionic: metal + non-metal, electrostatic attraction, giant lattice
  • Covalent: non-metal + non-metal, electron sharing, can be simple molecular or giant covalent
  • Metallic: metal cations + delocalised electrons
  • Electronegativity increases across period, decreases down group
  • H-bonding requires H bonded to N, O, or F interacting with N, O, or F
  • VSEPR: lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs, reducing bond angles
  • sp = linear, sp² = trigonal planar, sp³ = tetrahedral
  • Every bond has 1 σ; double bond = 1σ + 1π; triple bond = 1σ + 2π
  • Delocalisation in benzene gives stability and equal bond lengths

  • Forgetting that giant covalent structures don't have intermolecular forces
  • Saying NH₃ or H₂O is tetrahedral (they're trigonal pyramidal / bent)
  • Not mentioning both electronegativity difference AND molecular symmetry for polarity
  • Confusing bond energy with atomisation energy

C3: States of Matter

IGCSE Content

The Mole Concept

The mole is the amount of substance containing the same number of particles (atoms, molecules, ions) as there are atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12. This number is Avogadro's constant: 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

n = mass / Mr    or    mass = n × Mr
n = number of particles / NA    where NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹

Empirical and Molecular Formulae

Empirical formula = simplest whole number ratio of atoms in a compound.

Molecular formula = actual number of atoms of each element in a molecule.

To find empirical formula from percentage composition:

  1. Assume 100 g sample → mass of each element = percentage value
  2. Convert mass to moles (divide by Ar)
  3. Divide by smallest number of moles
  4. Multiply to get whole numbers if needed

Reacting Mass Calculations

Using balanced equations and mole ratios:

  1. Write balanced equation
  2. Calculate moles of known substance
  3. Use mole ratio from equation to find moles of unknown
  4. Convert to required quantity (mass, volume, etc.)

Concentration Calculations

Concentration (mol/dm³) = n / V (in dm³)
Concentration (g/dm³) = mass / V (in dm³)
1 dm³ = 1000 cm³

Molar Gas Volume

At room temperature and pressure (RTP, 20°C, 1 atm): 1 mole = 24 dm³

At standard temperature and pressure (STP, 0°C, 1 atm): 1 mole = 22.4 dm³

n = V / 24 (at RTP)    or    n = V / 22.4 (at STP)

Percentage Yield and Purity

% Yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100
% Purity = (mass of pure substance / mass of sample) × 100

AS Level Content

Ideal Gas Equation

PV = nRT
P = pressure (Pa), V = volume (m³), n = moles, R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, T = temperature (K)
T(K) = T(°C) + 273

Assumptions of ideal gases:

  • Particles have negligible volume compared to container
  • No intermolecular forces
  • Collisions are perfectly elastic
  • Particles move in random motion

Real gases deviate from ideal behaviour at high pressure and low temperature.

Avogadro's Law and Gas Volumes

Equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules. This allows direct use of reacting gas volumes from equation coefficients.

H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g)
1 vol + 1 vol → 2 vol

Standard Enthalpy Changes of Combustion and Formation

These are defined per mole and used in Hess's law calculations. (See C5 for more detail.)

Acid-Base Titrations

At equivalence point: moles of H⁺ = moles of OH⁻ (for monoprotic acids and monobasic bases)

For monoprotic acid + monobasic base: MaVa = MbVb
For polyprotic: n₁M₁V₁ = n₂M₂V₂ where n = number of replaceable H⁺/OH⁻

Back Titrations

Used when direct titration is difficult:

  1. Add excess of one reagent to react with analyte
  2. Titrate the excess reagent
  3. Calculate amount reacted with analyte by difference

Redox Titrations

Common examples:

  • Manganate(VII) with iron(II): MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O
  • Iodine-thiosulfate: I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻

A2 Level Content

Partition Coefficient

When a solute is distributed between two immiscible solvents at equilibrium:

Kpc = [solute] in solvent A / [solute] in solvent B (at constant T)

The partition coefficient is constant at a given temperature and independent of total concentration. Used in solvent extraction and chromatography.

Equilibrium Constants in Terms of Concentration and Pressure

Kc = [products] / [reactants] (coefficients as powers)
Kp = (partial pressures of products) / (partial pressures of reactants)

(See C7 Equilibria for full details.)

Born-Haber Cycles and Lattice Energy Calculations

Lattice energy calculations involve stoichiometric amounts and enthalpy changes per mole. (See C5 for more detail.)

  • Empirical formula: % → mass → moles → ratio → simplest integers

C4: Chemical Energetics

IGCSE Content

Electrolysis Basics

Electrolysis is the decomposition of a compound into its elements by an electric current. It requires:

  • An electrolyte (molten or aqueous ionic compound)
  • Two electrodes (anode = positive, cathode = negative)
  • A direct current supply

Key rule: Cations (positive) move to cathode (negative), anions (negative) move to anode (positive).

Molten Electrolysis

At cathode: Metal ions gain electrons (reduction) → metal deposited

At anode: Non-metal ions lose electrons (oxidation) → non-metal produced

Example: Molten NaCl
Cathode: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na
Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻

Aqueous Electrolysis

Water complicates the process. At the cathode, either the metal ion or H⁺ is reduced; at the anode, either the anion or OH⁻ is oxidised.

At cathode:

  • If metal is above hydrogen in reactivity series: H₂ is produced (2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂)
  • If metal is below hydrogen: metal is deposited (e.g., Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu)

At anode:

  • If halide ion present (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻): halogen produced (e.g., 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻)
  • If OH⁻ or oxyanion (SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻): O₂ produced (4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻)

Electrolysis of Dilute vs Concentrated Solutions

Dilute NaCl: Cathode: H₂; Anode: O₂

Concentrated NaCl (brine): Cathode: H₂; Anode: Cl₂

This is the basis of the chlor-alkali industry.

Electrolysis Reactions Summary

Electrolysis of CuSO₄(aq) with inert electrodes:
Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (pink deposit)
Anode: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ (bubbles)

Electrolysis of CuSO₄(aq) with copper electrodes:
Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
Anode: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ (copper dissolves)

Electroplating

The object to be plated is the cathode. The plating metal is the anode. The electrolyte contains ions of the plating metal. This is used to prevent corrosion and improve appearance.

AS Level Content

Standard Electrode Potentials

The standard electrode potential (E°) is the potential difference between a half-cell and a standard hydrogen electrode under standard conditions (298 K, 1 mol/dm³, 1 atm).

Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE): 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂(g)    E° = 0.00 V

Calculating Cell EMF

E°cell = E°reduction (cathode) - E°reduction (anode)
or
E°cell = E°(more positive) - E°(less positive)

A positive E°cell means the reaction is feasible (spontaneous) under standard conditions.

Predicting Reactions Using E° Values

  • Species with more positive E° are better oxidising agents (get reduced)
  • Species with more negative E° are better reducing agents (get oxidised)
  • The half-cell with more positive E° will undergo reduction
  • The half-cell with less positive E° will undergo oxidation

Standard Electrode Potential Values to Know

K⁺/K: -2.93 V   Na⁺/Na: -2.71 V   Mg²⁺/Mg: -2.37 V
Zn²⁺/Zn: -0.76 V   Fe²⁺/Fe: -0.44 V   Pb²⁺/Pb: -0.13 V
2H⁺/H₂: 0.00 V   Cu²⁺/Cu: +0.34 V   Ag⁺/Ag: +0.80 V
Cl₂/Cl⁻: +1.36 V   F₂/F⁻: +2.87 V

Factors Affecting Electrode Potential

Under non-standard conditions, use the Nernst equation (not required at AS but conceptually):

  • Concentration: higher [ion] shifts equilibrium, affecting potential
  • Temperature: affects position of equilibrium
  • Pressure: for gas electrodes

Redox Equations

To combine half-equations:

  1. Write both half-equations with equal electrons
  2. Add them together, cancelling electrons
MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O    E° = +1.52 V
Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻
Multiply second by 5, then add:
MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O

Electrolysis Quantitative Calculations

Q = I × t (charge = current × time in seconds)
n = Q / F (moles = charge / Faraday constant)
F = 96500 C mol⁻¹

A2 Level Content

Equilibrium Constant Kc and Electrode Potentials

There is a relationship between Gibbs free energy, equilibrium constant, and electrode potential:

ΔG° = -nFE°cell = -RT ln K
At 298 K: E°cell = (0.059 / n) × log K

Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions with Concentration Effects

At A2, you need to explain which ion is preferentially discharged based on:

  • Standard electrode potentials
  • Concentration of ions
  • Overpotential effects (e.g., high overpotential for H₂ on some electrodes)

Fuel Cells

Hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell:

Anode: H₂ + 2OH⁻ → 2H₂O + 2e⁻ (alkaline)
Cathode: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻
Overall: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Advantages: only product is water, efficient energy conversion. Disadvantages: H₂ storage, expensive catalysts.

Storage Cells and Batteries

  • Lead-acid battery: Rechargeable, used in cars
  • Lithium-ion batteries: High energy density, used in electronics

  • More positive E° = better oxidising agent; more negative = better reducing agent
  • Q = It; n = Q/F; F = 96500 C mol⁻¹
  • Fuel cells: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O; alkaline or acidic conditions

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Confusing anode and cathode (anode = oxidation, not always positive in electrolysis)
  • Not reversing the sign when using E°(reduction) for oxidation half-cells
  • Forgetting to balance electrons when combining half-equations
  • Using time in minutes instead of seconds in Q = It
  • Not considering concentration effects in aqueous electrolysis

C5: Electrochemistry

IGCSE Content

Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions

Exothermic: Heat energy given out to surroundings. Products have less energy than reactants. ΔH is negative. Examples: combustion, neutralisation, respiration.

Endothermic: Heat energy taken in from surroundings. Products have more energy than reactants. ΔH is positive. Examples: thermal decomposition, photosynthesis, dissolving ammonium nitrate in water.

Activation Energy

The minimum energy required for a reaction to occur. Even exothermic reactions need activation energy to get started. Represented as Ea on energy profile diagrams.

Energy Profile Diagrams

  • Exothermic: products lower than reactants; ΔH negative
  • Endothermic: products higher than reactants; ΔH positive
  • Activation energy (Ea) is the energy barrier from reactants to transition state
  • Catalyst lowers Ea by providing an alternative reaction pathway

Bond Energy Calculations

ΔH = Σ(bond energies of bonds broken) - Σ(bond energies of bonds formed)
Bonds broken = endothermic (+), Bonds formed = exothermic (-)

AS Level Content

Standard Enthalpy Change Definitions

  • Standard enthalpy change of reaction (ΔH°r): enthalpy change when reaction occurs in molar quantities stated in equation, under standard conditions
  • Standard enthalpy change of formation (ΔH°f): enthalpy change when 1 mole of compound is formed from its elements in standard states
  • Standard enthalpy change of combustion (ΔH°c): enthalpy change when 1 mole of substance is completely burned in oxygen under standard conditions
  • Standard enthalpy change of neutralisation (ΔH°neut): enthalpy change when 1 mole of water is formed from neutralisation of acid and base

Standard conditions: 298 K (25°C), 1 atm pressure, 1 mol/dm³ concentration.

Hess's Law

The enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the route taken. Used to calculate enthalpy changes that cannot be measured directly.

ΔH = ΣΔH°f(products) - ΣΔH°f(reactants)
ΔH = ΣΔH°c(reactants) - ΣΔH°c(products)

Calorimetry

q = mcΔT
q = heat energy (J), m = mass of solution (g), c = specific heat capacity (4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ for water), ΔT = temperature change (K or °C)

ΔH = q / n (in kJ/mol, so divide by 1000 and by moles)

Sources of error in calorimetry:

  • Heat loss to surroundings
  • Assuming solution has same density and SHC as water
  • Incomplete combustion
  • Heat capacity of calorimeter not accounted for

Bond Enthalpies

Mean bond enthalpy: average value across different molecules. Bond enthalpy calculations use gas-phase species only and give approximate values because mean bond enthalpies are averages.

Enthalpy Changes of Solution and Hydration

  • Lattice energy (ΔH°latt): enthalpy change when 1 mole of ionic solid is formed from gaseous ions (exothermic)
  • Enthalpy change of solution (ΔH°sol): enthalpy change when 1 mole of solute dissolves in solvent to form infinitely dilute solution
  • Enthalpy change of hydration (ΔH°hyd): enthalpy change when 1 mole of gaseous ions is hydrated
ΔH°sol = ΔH°latt + ΣΔH°hyd(ions)

A2 Level Content

Born-Haber Cycles

A thermochemical cycle used to calculate lattice energy. Steps for MX:

  1. Atomisation of metal: M(s) → M(g)
  2. Atomisation of non-metal: ½X₂(g) → X(g)
  3. Ionisation of metal: M(g) → M⁺(g) + e⁻
  4. Electron affinity of non-metal: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g)
  5. Formation: M(s) + ½X₂(g) → MX(s)
  6. Lattice formation: M⁺(g) + X⁻(g) → MX(s)
ΔH°f = ΔH°at(M) + ΔH°at(X) + IE(M) + EA(X) + ΔH°latt

Entropy and Gibbs Free Energy

Entropy (S) is a measure of disorder. Standard entropy S° is measured in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

ΔS° = ΣS°(products) - ΣS°(reactants)

Factors increasing entropy:

  • More moles of gas on product side
  • Change from solid → liquid → gas
  • Increase in complexity/molar mass

Gibbs Free Energy:

ΔG = ΔH - TΔS
ΔG < 0: spontaneous reaction
ΔG = 0: equilibrium
ΔG > 0: non-spontaneous

Temperature Dependence of Spontaneity

ΔHΔSSpontaneity
-+Spontaneous at all T
+-Non-spontaneous at all T
--Spontaneous at low T only
++Spontaneous at high T only

🧪 Worked Example

Question: Calculate the mass of NaOH needed to make 500 cm³ of 0.2 mol/dm³ solution. (Mᵣ NaOH = 40)

Solution:
n = c × V = 0.2 × (500/1000) = 0.1 mol
mass = n × Mᵣ = 0.1 × 40 = 4.0 g
Answer: 4.0 g
Forgetting to convert cm³ to dm³ (÷1000) when using n = c × V. Always check your units!
In a balanced equation, mole ratios are the stoichiometric coefficients. Use these to convert between substances.

⚡ Quick Recall

  • n = mass / Mᵣ
  • n = concentration × volume (dm³)
  • 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³
  • % yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100

Last-Minute Summary

🎯 Key Points to Remember

  • Exothermic: ΔH < 0; Endothermic: ΔH > 0
  • ΔH = ΣBE(broken) - ΣBE(formed)
  • Hess's Law: ΔH = ΣΔHf(products) - ΣΔHf(reactants)
  • q = mcΔT; ΔH = q/n
  • Born-Haber cycle: ΔHf = Σ(atomisation + IE + EA) + ΔHlatt
  • ΔS = ΣS(products) - ΣS(reactants)
  • ΔG = ΔH - TΔS; if ΔG < 0, reaction is spontaneous
  • Units: ΔH in kJ/mol; ΔS in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹; convert before subtracting!

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Using the wrong Hess's Law formula (products-reactants for formation, reactants-products for combustion)
  • Forgetting to convert J to kJ (or vice versa) when combining ΔH and TΔS
  • Not including coefficients in cycle calculations
  • Assuming all lattice energies are exothermic (they are, but check signs)
  • Forgetting that entropy is in J, not kJ

C6: Equilibria

IGCSE Content

Rate of Reaction

Rate of reaction is the change in concentration of reactants or products per unit time. It can be measured by:

  • Mass loss (gas produced)
  • Volume of gas evolved
  • Change in colour intensity
  • Change in pH
  • Turbidity (precipitate formation)
Rate = Δconcentration / Δtime
Units: mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹

Collision Theory

For a reaction to occur, particles must:

  1. Collide with each other
  2. Collide with sufficient energy (≥ activation energy)
  3. Collide with correct orientation

Factors Affecting Rate of Reaction

  • Concentration: More particles per unit volume → more frequent collisions → faster rate
  • Temperature: Higher T → particles move faster → more energetic collisions → more collisions exceed Ea
  • Surface area: More exposed surface → more collision sites → faster rate
  • Catalyst: Provides alternative pathway with lower Ea → more molecules have sufficient energy

Catalysts

A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being chemically changed. It works by lowering the activation energy. It is not used up. Enzymes are biological catalysts. Transition metals often act as catalysts.

AS Level Content

Rate Equations

For a reaction: aA + bB → products, the rate equation is:

Rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ
Where m and n are orders with respect to A and B, found experimentally (not from stoichiometry)

Order of Reaction

  • Zero order: Rate independent of [reactant]. Rate = k. Graph of [A] vs time is straight line.
  • First order: Rate ∝ [reactant]. Rate = k[A]. Half-life is constant.
  • Second order: Rate ∝ [reactant]². Rate = k[A]².

Overall order = m + n

Units of Rate Constant k

Overall OrderUnits of k
0mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
1s⁻¹
2dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹
3dm⁶ mol⁻² s⁻¹

Methods for Determining Order

Initial rates method: Measure initial rate at different initial concentrations. Compare how rate changes when [reactant] is changed.

Graphical methods:

  • Plot [A] vs time: straight line = zero order; curve = first or second
  • Plot ln[A] vs time: straight line = first order; gradient = -k
  • Plot 1/[A] vs time: straight line = second order; gradient = k

Half-Life

For first-order reactions only:

t½ = ln(2) / k = 0.693 / k
Half-life is independent of initial concentration

Arrhenius Equation

k = A e^(-Ea/RT)
k = rate constant, A = frequency factor, Ea = activation energy, R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, T = temperature (K)

A plot of ln k vs 1/T gives a straight line with gradient = -Ea/R.

A2 Level Content

Rate-Determining Step

In a multi-step reaction mechanism, the slowest step determines the overall rate. The rate equation reflects only the species involved in (or before) the rate-determining step.

Example:
Step 1 (slow): NO₂ + NO₂ → NO₃ + NO (rate-determining)
Step 2 (fast): NO₃ + CO → NO₂ + CO₂
Overall: NO₂ + CO → NO + CO₂
Rate = k[NO₂]² (CO does not appear in rate equation)

Pre-Equilibrium

When a fast equilibrium precedes the rate-determining step, the concentration of intermediates can be expressed in terms of reactants using the equilibrium constant.

Activation Energy and Catalysis

Catalysts provide an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy. They do not change ΔH of the reaction. Homogeneous catalysts are in the same phase as reactants; heterogeneous catalysts are in a different phase (usually solid catalyst, gas/liquid reactants).

Enzyme Kinetics (Michaelis-Menten)

Enzymes are highly specific biological catalysts. The Michaelis constant (Km) relates to enzyme-substrate affinity. At high [substrate], the rate becomes independent of [substrate] (zero order with respect to substrate) as all active sites are occupied.

  • n = mass / Mᵣ
  • n = concentration × volume (dm³)
  • 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³
  • % yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100

Last-Minute Summary

🎯 Key Points to Remember

  • Collision theory: collide + sufficient energy + correct orientation
  • Rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ; orders found experimentally
  • Zero order: rate constant; First order: t½ = 0.693/k; Second order: 1/[A] vs time is linear
  • Units of k depend on overall order
  • Arrhenius: ln k vs 1/T; gradient = -Ea/R
  • Rate-determining step controls overall rate
  • Catalysts lower Ea; do not change ΔH or equilibrium position
  • Temperature increases rate by increasing proportion of molecules with E ≥ Ea (Boltzmann distribution)

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Using stoichiometric coefficients as orders in rate equations
  • Confusing rate with rate constant
  • Using minutes instead of seconds for time units
  • Forgetting that catalysts don't affect equilibrium position or ΔH
  • Not converting temperature to Kelvin in Arrhenius calculations

C7: Reaction Kinetics

IGCSE Content

Reversible Reactions

Some reactions can go in both directions. At equilibrium, the forward and backward reactions occur at the same rate, so concentrations remain constant. Equilibrium is dynamic, not static.

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)

Le Chatelier's Principle

If a dynamic equilibrium is disturbed by changing conditions, the position of equilibrium moves to counteract the change.

Concentration: Increasing reactant concentration shifts equilibrium to products (right).

Pressure (gases only): Increasing pressure shifts equilibrium to side with fewer gas molecules.

Temperature: Increasing temperature shifts equilibrium in endothermic direction.

Catalyst: Speeds up both forward and backward reactions equally. No effect on equilibrium position; reduces time to reach equilibrium.

Haber Process

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)    ΔH = -92 kJ/mol
Conditions: 450°C, 200 atm, iron catalyst
Compromise temperature: higher T would favour reactants (exothermic), but lower T is too slow

Contact Process

2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g)    ΔH = -98 kJ/mol
Conditions: 450°C, 1-2 atm, V₂O₅ catalyst

AS Level Content

Equilibrium Constant Kc

For a general reaction: aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD

Kc = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ / [A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ
Units depend on stoichiometry; pure solids and liquids are omitted

Kc is constant at a given temperature. Only temperature changes Kc.

Equilibrium Constant Kp

For gaseous equilibria, partial pressures are used:

Kp = (pC)ᶜ(pD)ᵈ / (pA)ᵃ(pB)ᵇ
Partial pressure = mole fraction × total pressure
Mole fraction = moles of gas / total moles of gas

Relationship Between Kp and Kc

Kp = Kc(RT)^Δn
Δn = change in moles of gas (products - reactants)
R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, T in Kelvin

Calculating Equilibrium Constants

Use ICE tables (Initial, Change, Equilibrium):

  1. Write balanced equation
  2. Set up table with initial amounts
  3. Define change in terms of x
  4. Write equilibrium expressions
  5. Substitute into Kc/Kp expression and solve

A2 Level Content

Acid-Base Equilibria

Brønsted-Lowry theory: Acid = proton donor, Base = proton acceptor. Conjugate acid-base pairs differ by one proton.

HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻
Acid 1 + Base 2 ⇌ Acid 2 + Base 1

Acid Dissociation Constant Ka

Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA]
pKa = -log₁₀Ka
Stronger acid → larger Ka → smaller pKa

pH Calculations

pH = -log₁₀[H⁺]
[H⁺] = 10^(-pH)
Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C
pH + pOH = 14

Strong and Weak Acids/Bases

  • Strong acid: Fully dissociated. [H⁺] = acid concentration for monoprotic acids.
  • Weak acid: Partially dissociated. [H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA]) for dilute solutions.

Buffer Solutions

A buffer resists pH change when small amounts of acid or base are added. Contains a weak acid and its conjugate base (salt).

[H⁺] = Ka × [acid] / [salt]
pH = pKa + log₁₀([salt]/[acid]) (Henderson-Hasselbalch equation)

Salt Hydrolysis

  • Salt of strong acid + strong base: pH = 7 (neutral)
  • Salt of weak acid + strong base: pH > 7 (basic, anion hydrolyses)
  • Salt of strong acid + weak base: pH < 7 (acidic, cation hydrolyses)

Solubility Product Ksp

For AₓBᵧ(s) ⇌ xAʸ⁺(aq) + yBˣ⁻(aq):
Ksp = [A]ˣ[B]ʸ
Ionic product > Ksp → precipitation occurs

⚡ Quick Recall

  • n = mass / Mᵣ
  • n = concentration × volume (dm³)
  • 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³
  • % yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100

Last-Minute Summary

🎯 Key Points to Remember

  • Le Chatelier: equilibrium shifts to oppose change
  • Temperature increase favours endothermic direction; only factor changing Kc
  • Pressure increase favours side with fewer gas moles
  • Catalyst: no effect on equilibrium position or Kc
  • Kc = [products]/[reactants] with coefficients as powers
  • Kp uses partial pressures; Kp = Kc(RT)^Δn
  • pH = -log[H⁺]; pH < 7 acidic, pH > 7 alkaline
  • Weak acid: [H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA])
  • Buffer: weak acid + its salt; pH = pKa + log([salt]/[acid])

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting to omit solids and pure liquids from Kc expressions
  • Confusing Kp units with Kc units
  • Not squaring/cubing coefficients in equilibrium expressions
  • Using concentration instead of partial pressure in Kp
  • Forgetting that pH calculations need [H⁺] in mol/dm³

Introduction to Organic Chemistry

Organic chemistry is the study of carbon compounds (excluding CO, CO₂, carbonates, cyanides). Carbon forms stable covalent bonds with itself and other elements, allowing vast diversity of compounds.

Homologous Series

A homologous series is a family of compounds with the same functional group and similar chemical properties. Each member differs from the next by CH₂.

SeriesFunctional GroupGeneral Formula
AlkanesC-C single bonds onlyCnH₂n₊₂
AlkenesC=C double bondCnH₂n
Alcohols-OHCnH₂n₊₁OH

IUPAC Nomenclature Rules

  1. Identify longest carbon chain containing the principal functional group
  2. Number from end nearest to functional group (lowest locant)
  3. Name substituents with positions as prefixes
  4. Alphabetise substituent names (ignore di-, tri- prefixes for alphabetising)

Isomerism

Structural isomerism: Same molecular formula, different structural arrangement.

  • Chain isomerism: different carbon skeleton (branched vs straight)
  • Position isomerism: functional group in different positions
  • Functional group isomerism: different functional groups

AS Level Content

Stereoisomerism

Geometric (cis-trans/E-Z) isomerism: Occurs when there is restricted rotation (e.g., C=C double bond) and each carbon of the double bond has two different groups attached.

E isomer: higher priority groups on opposite sides
Z isomer: higher priority groups on same side
Priority: atomic number of directly attached atom (higher = higher priority)

Optical Isomerism

Occurs in molecules with a chiral centre (carbon attached to four different groups). These non-superimposable mirror images are called enantiomers.

  • Rotate plane-polarised light in equal but opposite directions
  • racemic mixture: equal mixture of both enantiomers; optically inactive
  • Optical activity measured using a polarimeter

Types of Organic Reactions

  • Substitution: One atom/group replaced by another
  • Addition: Two molecules combine to form one product
  • Elimination: Removal of atoms/groups to form a double bond
  • Redox: Gain/loss of oxygen/hydrogen or electrons
  • Hydrolysis: Breaking bonds by reaction with water
  • Condensation: Joining molecules with loss of small molecule (e.g., H₂O)

Reaction Mechanisms

Curly arrows: Show movement of electron pairs. Full-headed arrows = movement of 2 electrons; half-headed = 1 electron (radicals).

Electrophiles: Electron pair acceptors (e.g., H⁺, NO₂⁺, Br₂)

Nucleophiles: Electron pair donors (e.g., OH⁻, NH₃, H₂O, CN⁻)

A2 Level Content

Advanced Stereochemistry

Diastereomers: Stereoisomers that are not mirror images (e.g., cis-trans isomers).

Meso compounds: Molecules with chiral centres but an internal plane of symmetry; optically inactive.

Reaction Mechanisms in Detail

SN1 (unimolecular nucleophilic substitution):

  • Two steps: rate-determining formation of carbocation, then nucleophilic attack
  • Rate = k[halogenoalkane]
  • Favoured by tertiary halogenoalkanes, polar protic solvents
  • Racemisation at chiral centres

SN2 (bimolecular nucleophilic substitution):

  • One step: nucleophile attacks from opposite side to leaving group
  • Rate = k[halogenoalkane][nucleophile]
  • Favoured by primary halogenoalkanes, polar aprotic solvents
  • Inversion of configuration at chiral centres

Analytical Techniques

Infrared (IR) Spectroscopy:

  • O-H stretch: 3200-3600 cm⁻¹ (broad for H-bonded)
  • C-H stretch: 2850-3100 cm⁻¹
  • C=O stretch: 1680-1750 cm⁻¹
  • C-O stretch: 1000-1300 cm⁻¹
  • N-H stretch: 3300-3500 cm⁻¹

Mass Spectrometry: Molecular ion gives Mr; fragmentation gives structural information.

NMR Spectroscopy:

  • Chemical shift (δ) indicates environment of proton
  • Integration tells relative number of protons
  • Splitting (n+1 rule) indicates number of adjacent protons
  • TMS reference at δ = 0

  • Homologous series: same functional group, differ by CH₂, similar chemical properties
  • Structural isomers: chain, position, functional group
  • E-Z isomerism: restricted rotation + two different groups on each C of C=C

C9: Group 2 & 17

IGCSE Content

Alkanes

Saturated hydrocarbons with general formula CnH₂n₊₂. They contain only C-C single bonds and C-H bonds. Alkanes are relatively unreactive but undergo:

  • Combustion: Complete combustion produces CO₂ + H₂O; incomplete produces CO or C (soot)
  • Substitution with halogens: UV light required. Free radical mechanism.
  • Cracking: Breaking long-chain alkanes into shorter alkanes + alkenes
CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O (complete combustion)
C₈H₁₈ → C₄H₁₀ + C₄H₈ (cracking)

Alkenes

Unsaturated hydrocarbons with general formula CnH₂n. They contain a C=C double bond and are more reactive than alkanes.

  • Combustion: Burns with sooty flame (higher C:H ratio)
  • Addition reactions: Characteristic reaction of alkenes
Addition of H₂ (hydrogenation): C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆ (Ni catalyst, 150°C)
Addition of Br₂ (bromination): C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂ (red-brown → colourless)
Addition of HBr: C₂H₄ + HBr → C₂H₅Br (Markovnikov: H to C with more H)
Addition of H₂O (hydration): C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH (H₃PO₄ catalyst, 300°C, 60 atm)

Tests for Unsaturation

Bromine water test: red-brown bromine water decolourises with alkenes (addition reaction). Alkanes do not react (no change, or very slow under UV).

Fossil Fuels and Environmental Impact

  • Crude oil is separated by fractional distillation
  • Combustion of fossil fuels produces CO₂ (greenhouse gas) and SO₂ (acid rain)
  • Catalytic converters remove CO, NOₓ, and unburnt hydrocarbons from exhaust

AS Level Content

Free Radical Substitution Mechanism

Mechanism for reaction of methane with chlorine (UV light):

Initiation: Cl₂ → 2Cl• (homolytic fission by UV)
Propagation: CH₄ + Cl• → CH₃• + HCl
                CH₃• + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•
Termination: Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂
                CH₃• + Cl• → CH₃Cl
                CH₃• + CH₃• → C₂H₆

Further substitution can occur: CH₃Cl → CH₂Cl₂ → CHCl₃ → CCl₄

Electrophilic Addition to Alkenes

The electron-rich C=C double bond attacks electrophiles.

Mechanism (bromination):
1. Br₂ polarised by C=C; Brδ+ attacked by π electrons → cyclic bromonium ion
2. Br⁻ attacks from opposite side (anti-addition) → dibromoalkane

Markovnikov's Rule

In addition of HX to unsymmetrical alkenes, H attaches to the carbon with more H atoms already (forming more stable carbocation).

CH₃CH=CH₂ + HBr → CH₃CHBrCH₃ (major product)
Not CH₃CH₂CH₂Br (minor product)

Ozonolysis of Alkenes

Alkenes react with ozone, then reductive workup (Zn/H₂O) gives carbonyl compounds. Used to locate position of double bond.

A2 Level Content

Arene Chemistry - Benzene

Benzene (C₆H₆) has a planar hexagonal structure with delocalised π electrons. It undergoes electrophilic substitution (not addition) because addition would destroy the stable aromatic system.

Electrophilic Substitution of Benzene

General mechanism:
1. Electrophile (E⁺) generated by catalyst
2. E⁺ attacked by π electrons of benzene → carbocation intermediate (arenium ion)
3. Loss of H⁺ restores aromaticity → substituted benzene

Nitration

Reagents: concentrated HNO₃ + concentrated H₂SO₄ (catalyst), 50°C
Electrophile: NO₂⁺ (nitronium ion)
C₆H₆ + HNO₃ → C₆H₅NO₂ + H₂O

Halogenation

Reagents: Br₂ + FeBr₃ catalyst (or AlBr₃)
Electrophile: Br⁺
C₆H₆ + Br₂ → C₆H₅Br + HBr

Chlorination uses Cl₂ + FeCl₃/AlCl₃

Friedel-Crafts Reactions

Alkylation: C₆H₆ + RCl → C₆H₅R + HCl (AlCl₃ catalyst)
Acylation: C₆H₆ + RCOCl → C₆H₅COR + HCl (AlCl₃ catalyst)

Directing Effects of Substituents

  • 2,4-directing (activating): -OH, -NH₂, -NHR, -NR₂, -R, -X — donate electrons, activate ring
  • 3-directing (deactivating): -NO₂, -COOH, -CHO, -COR, -CN, -SO₃H — withdraw electrons, deactivate ring

Phenol

Phenol (C₆H₅OH) has an -OH group directly attached to benzene. The lone pairs on oxygen delocalise into the ring, making it more reactive than benzene:

  • Reacts with bromine water without catalyst → white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol
  • Weakly acidic (pKa ~10) — more acidic than alcohols, less than carboxylic acids

Methylbenzene (Toluene)

Methyl group is 2,4-directing and activating. Methylbenzene is more reactive than benzene and undergoes substitution more readily.

C10: Transition Metals

IGCSE Content

Alcohols

General formula CnH₂n₊₁OH. Classified by carbon attached to -OH:

  • Primary (1°): -OH on carbon attached to 1 other carbon
  • Secondary (2°): -OH on carbon attached to 2 other carbons
  • Tertiary (3°): -OH on carbon attached to 3 other carbons

Combustion: Alcohols burn in oxygen to form CO₂ and H₂O

Oxidation: Primary alcohols oxidise to aldehydes, then carboxylic acids. Secondary alcohols oxidise to ketones. Tertiary alcohols resist oxidation.

Aldehydes and Ketones

Both contain C=O (carbonyl group). Aldehydes have C=O at end of chain; ketones have C=O in middle.

Fehling's/Benedict's test: Aldehydes reduce Cu²⁺ (blue) to Cu₂O (brick-red precipitate). Ketones do not react.

Tollens' reagent: Aldehydes reduce [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ to Ag (silver mirror). Ketones do not react.

Carboxylic Acids

General formula CnH₂n₊₁COOH. Weak acids that partially dissociate:

CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺
React with carbonates: 2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂

Esters

Formed from condensation reaction between carboxylic acid and alcohol:

CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O (conc H₂SO₄ catalyst, heat)

Esters have fruity smells and are used in flavourings and perfumes.

AS Level Content

Oxidation of Alcohols

Reagents: Acidified potassium dichromate(VI) (K₂Cr₂O₇/H⁺) — orange to green. Or acidified potassium manganate(VII) (KMnO₄/H⁺) — purple to colourless.

Primary alcohol → aldehyde (distill off product): CH₃CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃CHO + H₂O
Primary alcohol → carboxylic acid (reflux): CH₃CH₂OH + 2[O] → CH₃COOH + H₂O
Secondary alcohol → ketone (reflux): CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ + [O] → CH₃COCH₃ + H₂O
Tertiary alcohol: no reaction with common oxidising agents

Reduction of Carbonyls

Aldehyde → primary alcohol: CH₃CHO + 2[H] → CH₃CH₂OH
Ketone → secondary alcohol: CH₃COCH₃ + 2[H] → CH₃CH(OH)CH₃
Reagent: NaBH₄ (sodium borohydride) in aqueous/ethanolic solution

Nucleophilic Addition to Carbonyls

Mechanism with HCN:

1. CN⁻ attacks C=O carbon (nucleophilic)
2. C=O π bond breaks, O becomes O⁻
3. O⁻ protonated to form hydroxynitrile
Catalyst: CN⁻ or OH⁻ (nucleophile generated from KCN + acid)

Iodoform Reaction

Methyl ketones (CH₃COR) and ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) give yellow precipitate of CHI₃ with I₂/NaOH. Used as a test and in synthesis (shortens carbon chain by one).

A2 Level Content

Acyl Chlorides

More reactive than carboxylic acids. React with:

  • Water → carboxylic acid + HCl
  • Alcohol → ester + HCl
  • Ammonia → amide + HCl
  • Primary amine → substituted amide + HCl
CH₃COCl + H₂O → CH₃COOH + HCl
CH₃COCl + CH₃OH → CH₃COOCH₃ + HCl
CH₃COCl + NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + HCl

Carboxylic Acid Derivatives

Amides: RCONH₂. Formed from acyl chlorides + ammonia/amine. Less reactive than acyl chlorides. Hydrolyse to carboxylic acid + ammonia/amine (acid or alkali conditions).

Grignard Reagents

RMgX (where X = halogen). React with carbonyl compounds to form alcohols with new C-C bonds:

CH₃CHO + CH₃MgBr → CH₃CH(OMgBr)CH₃ → CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ (after H₃O⁺ workup)
With aldehyde → secondary alcohol
With ketone → tertiary alcohol
With CO₂ → carboxylic acid (after acid workup)

Optical Activity in Carbonyl Chemistry

Nucleophilic addition to planar carbonyl can occur from either side. If the carbonyl carbon becomes a new chiral centre, a racemic mixture is formed.

C11: Organic Chemistry

📗 AS Level Content

Amines

Amines are derivatives of ammonia where one or more H atoms are replaced by alkyl/aryl groups:

  • Primary amine: RNH₂ (e.g., methylamine CH₃NH₂)
  • Secondary amine: R₂NH (e.g., dimethylamine (CH₃)₂NH)
  • Tertiary amine: R₃N (e.g., trimethylamine (CH₃)₃N)

Amines are weak bases (lone pair on N accepts protons):

CH₃NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃NH₃⁺ + OH⁻
Basicity order: tertiary < secondary < primary < NH₃ (in gas phase) / tertiary > secondary > primary (in aqueous, due to solvation)

Mechanisms & Pathways

AS Chemistry requires understanding reaction mechanisms in detail:

Preparation of Amines

  • Reduction of nitriles: CH₃CN + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂NH₂ (LiAlH₄ or H₂/Ni)
  • Reduction of nitro compounds: C₆H₅NO₂ + 6[H] → C₆H₅NH₂ (Sn/HCl or H₂/Ni)
  • Hofmann degradation of amides: RCONH₂ + Br₂ + 4NaOH → RNH₂ + Na₂CO₃ + 2NaBr + 2H₂O

Reactions of Amines

  • With acids: form salts (e.g., CH₃NH₂ + HCl → CH₃NH₃⁺Cl⁻)
  • With acyl chlorides: form amides
  • With halogenoalkanes: further substitution to give 2°, 3° amines, then quaternary salts

Nitriles

Contain -C≡N group. Prepared from halogenoalkanes + KCN (in ethanol):

CH₃CH₂Br + KCN → CH₃CH₂CN + KBr
CH₃CH₂CN + 2H₂O + H⁺ → CH₃CH₂COOH + NH₄⁺ (hydrolysis)

C12: Hydrocarbons

IGCSE Content

Addition Polymerisation

Monomers with C=C double bonds join together without losing any atoms. The double bond opens to form single bonds between repeating units.

n CH₂=CH₂ → -(CH₂-CH₂)n- (polyethene)
n CH₂=CHCl → -(CH₂-CHCl)n- (poly(chloroethene), PVC)
n CH₂=CH(C₆H₅) → -(CH₂-CH(C₆H₅))n- (polystyrene)

Properties of addition polymers: Non-biodegradable, chemically inert, thermoplastic (can be remelted).

Condensation Polymerisation

Monomers join together with the loss of a small molecule (usually water). Requires two different functional groups that can react.

Dicarboxylic acid + diol → polyester + water
HOOC-C₆H₄-COOH + HO-CH₂-CH₂-OH → -(CO-C₆H₄-CO-O-CH₂-CH₂-O-)n- + H₂O (Terylene)

Natural Polymers

Proteins: Polymers of amino acids joined by peptide (amide) bonds.

Carbohydrates: Starch and cellulose are polymers of glucose.

DNA: Polymer of nucleotides.

AS Level Content

Polyesters

Formed from dicarboxylic acids and diols. Contains ester linkages (-COO-). Examples: Terylene, PET.

n HOOC-R-COOH + n HO-R'-OH → -(CO-R-CO-O-R'-O-)n- + 2n H₂O

Polyamides

Formed from dicarboxylic acids and diamines (or amino acids). Contains amide linkages (-CONH-). Examples: Nylon-6,6, proteins.

n HOOC-(CH₂)₄-COOH + n H₂N-(CH₂)₆-NH₂ → -(CO-(CH₂)₄-CO-NH-(CH₂)₆-NH-)n- + 2n H₂O (Nylon-6,6)

Hydrolysis of Polymers

Polyesters and polyamides can be hydrolysed:

  • Acid hydrolysis of polyester → dicarboxylic acid + diol
  • Alkali hydrolysis of polyester → dicarboxylate salt + diol
  • Acid hydrolysis of polyamide → dicarboxylic acid + diamine salt
  • Alkali hydrolysis of polyamide → dicarboxylate salt + diamine

A2 Level Content

Environmental Impact of Polymers

Non-biodegradable plastics: Persist in environment, cause pollution, harm wildlife. Solutions include recycling, incineration (energy recovery), and biodegradable alternatives.

Biodegradable polymers: Poly(lactic acid) (PLA) from corn starch; polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) from bacteria. Break down by microbial action.

Conducting Polymers

Some polymers with conjugated double bonds can conduct electricity when doped (e.g., polyacetylene, polyaniline). Used in OLEDs, solar cells, and sensors.

Polymer Structures and Properties

  • Thermoplastic: Linear chains with weak intermolecular forces. Can be remelted and reshaped.
  • Thermosetting: Cross-linked network. Rigid, cannot be remelted.
  • Elastomers: Cross-linked but with flexible chains. Can stretch and return to shape (e.g., vulcanised rubber).

C13: Carbonyl Compounds

📙 A2 Level Content

Definition and Properties

Transition elements are d-block elements that form at least one stable ion with a partially filled d subshell. Key properties:

  • Variable oxidation states
  • Coloured compounds
  • Catalytic activity
  • Formation of complex ions
  • Formation of paramagnetic compounds

Electronic Configurations

Transition metals lose 4s electrons before 3d electrons when forming ions:

Sc: [Ar] 3d¹ 4s² → Sc³⁺: [Ar]
Ti: [Ar] 3d² 4s² → Ti²⁺: [Ar] 3d² → Ti³⁺: [Ar] 3d¹ → Ti⁴⁺: [Ar]
Cr: [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ (exception) → Cr³⁺: [Ar] 3d³
Mn: [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s² → Mn²⁺: [Ar] 3d⁵
Fe: [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s² → Fe²⁺: [Ar] 3d⁶ → Fe³⁺: [Ar] 3d⁵
Cu: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ (exception) → Cu⁺: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ → Cu²⁺: [Ar] 3d⁹
Zn: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² → Zn²⁺: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ (not a transition element by ion definition)

Complex Ions

A central metal ion surrounded by ligands (molecules/ions with lone pairs). Ligands donate electron pairs to form coordinate bonds.

  • Monodentate: One donor atom (e.g., H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻, CN⁻)
  • Bidentate: Two donor atoms (e.g., ethanedioate C₂O₄²⁻, 1,2-diaminoethane en)
  • Hexadentate: Six donor atoms (e.g., EDTA⁴⁻)

Coordination number = number of donor atoms bonded to metal.

Shapes of Complex Ions

  • Coordination number 6: octahedral (e.g., [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺, [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻)
  • Coordination number 4: tetrahedral (e.g., [CuCl₄]²⁻) or square planar
  • Coordination number 2: linear (e.g., [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺)

Colour in Transition Metal Compounds

Colour arises from d-d transitions:

  1. Ligands cause splitting of d orbitals into two energy levels
  2. Electron absorbs visible light and jumps from lower to higher d orbital
  3. Complementary colour is transmitted/reflected

Colour depends on:

  • Oxidation state of metal
  • Identity of metal
  • Type of ligand (spectrochemical series)

Spectrochemical series (increasing field strength): I⁻ < Br⁻ < Cl⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < H₂O < NH₃ < en < CN⁻ < CO

Stronger field ligands cause larger splitting → absorb higher energy (shorter wavelength) light.

Catalytic Activity

Transition metals and their compounds are excellent catalysts because:

  • Variable oxidation states allow redox cycles
  • Ability to adsorb reactants on surface (heterogeneous)
  • Formation of intermediate complexes (homogeneous)

Examples:

  • Fe in Haber process
  • V₂O₅ in Contact process
  • Ni in hydrogenation of alkenes
  • MnO₂ in decomposition of H₂O₂
  • Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ in S₂O₈²⁻/I⁻ reaction

Ligand Substitution Reactions

[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺(pale blue) + 4Cl⁻ → [CuCl₄]²⁻(yellow) + 6H₂O
[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺(deep blue) + 4H₂O
[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺(pink) + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CoCl₄]²⁻(blue) + 6H₂O (endothermic, blue at high T)

Precipitation Reactions

[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂(s)(blue) + 6H₂O
[Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₂(s)(green) + 6H₂O
[Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃(s)(brown) + 6H₂O

Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O    E° = +1.33 V (orange → green)

IGCSE Content

Group 2 (Alkaline Earth Metals)

Elements: Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra. Have 2 electrons in outer shell. Form 2+ ions.

Trend down the group:

  • Atomic radius increases (more electron shells)
  • Reactivity increases (outer electrons further from nucleus, more shielding)
  • First and second ionisation energies decrease

Reactions of Group 2 Metals

With water: Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂ (more vigorous down group)
With oxygen: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (bright white flame)
With acids: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂

Group 2 Oxides and Hydroxides

Basic oxides and hydroxides. Solubility of hydroxides increases down group. Solubility of sulfates decreases down group.

Mg(OH)₂: sparingly soluble (milk of magnesia, antacid)
Ca(OH)₂: slightly soluble (limewater, test for CO₂)
Ba(OH)₂: soluble (strong base)

Group 17 (Halogens)

Elements: F, Cl, Br, I, At. Have 7 electrons in outer shell. Form 1- ions.

Trend down the group:

  • Colour darkens: F₂ (pale yellow gas), Cl₂ (greenish-yellow gas), Br₂ (red-brown liquid), I₂ (grey-black solid)
  • Melting and boiling points increase (more electrons, stronger VdW)
  • Reactivity decreases (harder to gain electron, atomic radius increases)

Reactions of Halogens

Displacement: Cl₂ + 2NaBr → 2NaCl + Br₂ (more reactive displaces less reactive)
With metals: 2Fe + 3Cl₂ → 2FeCl₃
With hydrogen: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl (explosive in sunlight)

Tests for Halide Ions

Add dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solution:

  • Cl⁻: white precipitate of AgCl (soluble in dilute NH₃)
  • Br⁻: cream precipitate of AgBr (soluble in concentrated NH₃)
  • I⁻: yellow precipitate of AgI (insoluble in NH₃)

AS Level Content

Thermal Stability of Group 2 Compounds

Thermal stability of carbonates and nitrates increases down the group because the cation is larger and polarises the anion less.

MgCO₃ → MgO + CO₂ (decomposes easily)
BaCO₃ → very difficult to decompose
2Ca(NO₃)₂ → 2CaO + 4NO₂ + O₂
2Mg(NO₃)₂ decomposes more easily than Ba(NO₃)₂

Flame Tests

  • Li: crimson red
  • Na: yellow/orange
  • K: lilac
  • Ca: brick red
  • Sr: crimson
  • Ba: apple green

Industrial Uses

  • CaO (quicklime): making steel, neutralising acidic soil, drying agent
  • Ca(OH)₂ (slaked lime): neutralising acidic soil, water treatment
  • CaCO₃ (limestone): building material, making cement, neutralising acid

Chlorine and Water Treatment

Chlorine is used to kill bacteria in drinking water. It reacts with water:

Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HClO (hypochlorous acid, kills bacteria)
Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O (bleach)

Reaction of Chlorine with Cold vs Hot NaOH

Cold dilute: Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O (disproportionation)
Hot concentrated: 3Cl₂ + 6NaOH → 5NaCl + NaClO₃ + 3H₂O

A2 Level Content

Enthalpy Changes in Group 2

Thermodynamic cycles can explain trends in thermal decomposition. The lattice energy and enthalpy changes of formation determine decomposition temperature.

Anomalous Properties of Beryllium and Fluorine

Beryllium: Due to small size and high charge density:

  • Forms covalent compounds (e.g., BeCl₂)
  • Amphoteric oxide and hydroxide
  • Does not react with water

Fluorine: Due to small size and weak F-F bond:

  • Exceptionally high oxidising power
  • Can oxidise water: 2F₂ + 2H₂O → 4HF + O₂
  • HF is a weak acid (strong H-F bond)

Interhalogen Compounds

Compounds between different halogens: ClF, BrF₃, ICl. More reactive than individual halogens.

Industrial Production of Chlorine

Membrane cell electrolysis of brine:
Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻
Cathode: 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻
Overall: 2NaCl + 2H₂O → Cl₂ + H₂ + 2NaOH

🧪 Worked Example

Question: Calculate the mass of NaOH needed to make 500 cm³ of 0.2 mol/dm³ solution. (Mᵣ NaOH = 40)

Solution:
n = c × V = 0.2 × (500/1000) = 0.1 mol
mass = n × Mᵣ = 0.1 × 40 = 4.0 g
Answer: 4.0 g
Forgetting to convert cm³ to dm³ (÷1000) when using n = c × V. Always check your units!
In a balanced equation, mole ratios are the stoichiometric coefficients. Use these to convert between substances.

⚡ Quick Recall

  • n = mass / Mᵣ
  • n = concentration × volume (dm³)
  • 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³
  • % yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100

Last-Minute Summary

🎯 Key Points to Remember

  • Group 2: reactivity increases down group; IE decreases; hydroxide solubility increases; sulfate solubility decreases
  • Group 2 carbonates/nitrates: thermal stability increases down group
  • Group 17: reactivity decreases down group; colour darkens; mp/bp increase
  • Halide test: AgNO₃ + HNO₃; Cl⁻ white, Br⁻ cream, I⁻ yellow
  • Chlorine disproportionates in alkali: cold → ClO⁻; hot → ClO₃⁻
  • Be is anomalous: covalent, amphoteric, unreactive with water
  • F is anomalous: oxidises water, HF is weak acid

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Confusing the trends in solubility of hydroxides vs sulfates
  • Saying halogens become more reactive down the group (they become less reactive)
  • Forgetting to add dilute HNO₃ before AgNO₃ in halide tests
  • Not balancing disproportionation equations correctly
  • Confusing thermal stability trends (increases down group, not decreases)
