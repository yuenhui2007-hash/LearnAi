🌱 Unit 1 — Basic Economic Ideas

▼

🔑 Scarcity, Choice & Opportunity Cost

Human wants are unlimited, resources are limited. This forces choices. Opportunity cost is the next best alternative you give up. It's not just "what you lose" — it's specifically the best alternative forgone.

📐 Production Possibility Curve (PPC)

Shows maximum output combinations of two goods. Concave (bowed out) = increasing opportunity cost (resources aren't perfectly transferable). Points inside = inefficient. Points outside = unattainable with current resources. An outward shift = economic growth.

Exam tip: Movement along = reallocation. Shift = growth/decline.

⚖️ Economic Systems

Free market: Price signals allocate resources. Efficient but creates inequality. Planned: Government decides. Equal but inefficient. Mixed: Most real economies — markets dominate, government intervenes to fix failures.

💡
Did you know? The concept of opportunity cost was formalised by Friedrich von Wieser in the 1880s, but the idea traces back to Adam Smith and the French Physiocrats. Every time you scroll TikTok instead of studying, you're experiencing it firsthand.

⚖️ Unit 2 — The Price System & Theory of the Firm

▼

📉 Demand & Supply

Law of demand: Price falls → quantity demanded rises (income effect + substitution effect). Law of supply: Price rises → quantity supplied rises (profit motive). Equilibrium where Qd = Qs. Shifts: income, tastes, input prices, technology, taxes.

📊 Elasticities

PED: How responsive is demand to price changes? >1 = elastic (luxuries). <1 = inelastic (necessities). YED: Normal goods (>0) vs inferior goods (<0). XED: Positive = substitutes, negative = complements. PES: How responsive is supply? More time = more elastic.

Key determinant: Availability of substitutes

🏢 Theory of the Firm

Profit maximisation: MC = MR (and MC rising). Shut-down: In short run, continue if P > AVC. In long run, exit if P < AC. Costs: TFC, TVC, TC, AFC, AVC, AC, MC. Revenue: TR, AR (= price), MR.

🏭 Market Structures

Perfect competition: Many firms, identical products, no barriers, normal profit in long run. Monopoly: One firm, barriers to entry, supernormal profit, inefficiency (P > MC). Oligopoly: Few firms, interdependence, game theory, kinked demand curve. Monopolistic competition: Many firms, differentiated products, excess capacity.

🍎 Case Study: Apple's Pricing Power

Apple has inelastic demand for iPhones (loyal customers, ecosystem lock-in). This means they can raise prices without losing many customers — explaining why iPhone prices rose from $499 (2007) to $1,099+ today while maintaining market share.

⚠️ Market Failure

Negative externalities: Social cost > Private cost (pollution, congestion). Positive externalities: Social benefit > Private benefit (education, vaccines). Public goods: Non-excludable, non-rivalrous (defence, street lights). Merit/demerit goods: Information failure leads to under/over-consumption.

🏛️ Unit 3 — Government Microeconomic Intervention

▼

💰 Taxes & Subsidies

Specific tax: Fixed amount per unit (shifts supply up parallel). Ad valorem tax: Percentage of price (rotates supply curve). Incidence: Who bears the burden? Depends on elasticity — more inelastic side bears more. Subsidies lower costs; distribution also depends on elasticity.

📋 Price Controls

Maximum price (ceiling): Below equilibrium → shortage, queues, black markets. Used for rent control, essential goods. Minimum price (floor): Above equilibrium → surplus. Used for minimum wage, agricultural price supports. Both create deadweight loss.

Eval tip: Price controls help consumers/producers short-term but distort markets long-term

🛡️ Regulation & Competition Policy

Governments regulate to prevent abuse of monopoly power, protect consumers, ensure quality, and correct information asymmetries. Competition policy: Anti-trust laws, merger control, price-fixing prohibition. Privatisation: State → private ownership. Arguments for (efficiency, competition) and against (job losses, profit over service).

🏠 Case Study: Rent Control in Berlin

Berlin introduced rent caps in 2020. Result: housing supply fell as landlords converted rentals to sales or Airbnb. Courts eventually ruled it unconstitutional. Classic example of maximum price causing shortages and unintended consequences.

🌍 Unit 4 — The Macroeconomy

▼

📊 National Income & Circular Flow

GDP = C + I + G + (X − M). Measured by expenditure, income, or output methods. Real GDP adjusts for inflation. Circular flow: Injections (I, G, X) must equal leakages (S, T, M) for equilibrium.

📈 Aggregate Demand & Supply

AD = total spending in the economy. Shifts right with expansionary fiscal/monetary policy, confidence, export growth. SRAS is upward sloping (sticky wages). LRAS is vertical at full employment output. Only supply-side policies shift LRAS.

💼 Unemployment

Frictional: Between jobs (inevitable, short-term). Structural: Skills/location mismatch (needs retraining). Cyclical: Insufficient AD (recession). Natural rate = frictional + structural. Only supply-side policies reduce natural rate.

📉 Inflation

Demand-pull: "Too much money chasing too few goods." Cost-push: Rising input costs (oil, wages). Monetary: Excessive money supply growth (Friedman). Costs: menu costs, shoe-leather costs, redistribution, uncertainty, fiscal drag.

💱 Balance of Payments & Exchange Rates

Current account deficits caused by loss of competitiveness, strong currency, low savings. Exchange rate systems: Fixed (peg), floating (market-determined), managed float (most common). J-curve: Depreciation worsens deficit short-term before improving it.

💡
Did you know? Venezuela's inflation hit 1,000,000% in 2018. The cause? The government printed money to finance deficits while output collapsed. This is hyperinflation — when money becomes literally worthless. People weighed cash instead of counting it.

🎯 Unit 5 — Government Macroeconomic Intervention

▼

💷 Fiscal Policy

Government spending and taxation. Expansionary: Cut taxes, increase spending → boosts AD. Contractionary: Raise taxes, cut spending → reduces AD. Automatic stabilisers: Welfare spending rises and tax revenue falls during recessions without government action.

🏦 Monetary Policy

Central bank controls interest rates and money supply. Transmission mechanism: Interest rate ↓ → borrowing cheaper → investment and consumption ↑ → AD ↑. Quantitative easing: Central bank buys assets to inject money when interest rates are near zero.

Limitation: Liquidity trap — cutting rates doesn't work if people hoard cash

📐 Supply-Side Policy

Aims to shift LRAS right. Labour market: Education, training, reducing union power. Product markets: Deregulation, competition policy. Capital markets: R&D subsidies, tax incentives for investment, infrastructure. Long time lags but non-inflationary growth.

📉 Phillips Curve

Short-run: Inverse relationship between inflation and unemployment (sticky wages allow trade-off). Long-run: Vertical at natural rate — no permanent trade-off. Expectations adjust. Expectations-augmented: Actual inflation = Expected inflation − α(Unemployment gap).

🌏 International Trade & Development

Comparative advantage: Trade benefits all countries when they specialise in what they produce at lowest opportunity cost. Protectionism: Tariffs, quotas, subsidies protect domestic industries but raise prices and invite retaliation. Development: Measured by HDI (health, education, income). Barriers: low savings, poor infrastructure, weak institutions, debt.

🇸🇬 Case Study: Singapore's Exchange Rate Policy

Unlike most countries, Singapore uses exchange rate policy instead of interest rates. The MAS manages the SGD against a trade-weighted basket. During 2022 inflation, they allowed faster SGD appreciation — making imports cheaper and controlling imported inflation without raising interest rates.
