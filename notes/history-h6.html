International History, 1945–1991

Cambridge 9389 A-Level / 0470 IGCSE

📘 IGCSE Content

The UN and the New World Order

  • UN founded (24 Oct 1945): Security Council (USA, USSR, UK, France, China as permanent members with veto), General Assembly, Secretariat, International Court of Justice.
  • Goals: maintain peace, self-determination, human rights, economic cooperation.
  • Limitations: veto power paralysed Security Council during Cold War; superpowers used UN when useful, ignored it when not (e.g., Korea 1950 — USSR boycott allowed action).

Decolonisation

  • India and Pakistan (1947): Mountbatten Plan; partition; 1 million dead in communal violence.
  • Middle East: Israel declared independence (1948); Arab-Israeli War; 700,000 Palestinian refugees. Suez Crisis (1956): Britain, France, Israel invaded Egypt after Nasser nationalised canal; USA and USSR forced withdrawal — end of British imperial role.
  • Africa: Ghana first sub-Saharan colony to gain independence (1957, Nkrumah). 1960 — "Year of Africa" (17 states independent). Algeria (1962) after brutal war with France.
  • Congo (1960): Patrice Lumumba assassinated (1961); UN intervention; civil war and secession of Katanga.
  • Non-Aligned Movement (1961): Bandung Conference (1955); founding members included Tito, Nasser, Nehru, Sukarno, Nkrumah.

The Arab-Israeli Conflict

  • 1948 War: Israel defeated Arab states; expanded territory beyond UN partition plan.
  • 1956 Suez Crisis: Nasser nationalised canal; British/French/Israeli invasion; superpower pressure forced retreat.
  • Six-Day War (1967): Israel launched pre-emptive strike; destroyed Egyptian air force; captured Sinai, Gaza, West Bank, East Jerusalem, Golan Heights. UN Resolution 242 called for withdrawal and peace.
  • Yom Kippur War (1973): Egypt and Syria attacked on Jewish Day of Atonement. Initial Arab gains reversed by Israeli counter-offensive. Oil crisis — OPEC embargo.
  • Camp David Accords (1978): Sadat (Egypt) and Begin (Israel) peace treaty; Sinai returned to Egypt. Sadat assassinated 1981.
  • Palestinian issue: PLO founded 1964 (Arafat); Intifadas (1987–93, 2000–05); Oslo Accords (1993).

The Cuban Revolution and Missile Crisis

  • 1959: Fidel Castro overthrew Batista. US-owned property nationalised; alliance with USSR.
  • Bay of Pigs (April 1961): CIA-backed invasion failed; embarrassed Kennedy.
  • Cuban Missile Crisis (Oct 1962): USSR placed nuclear missiles in Cuba. US naval blockade ("quarantine"). 13-day standoff. Khrushchev agreed to remove missiles in exchange for US pledge not to invade Cuba and secret removal of Jupiter missiles from Turkey.
  • Consequences: Hotline established (1963); Limited Test Ban Treaty (1963); nuclear diplomacy became more cautious.

The Middle East and Oil

  • Iranian Revolution (1979): Shah overthrown; Ayatollah Khomeini established Islamic Republic. US Embassy hostage crisis (Nov 1979–Jan 1981).
  • Iran-Iraq War (1980–88): Saddam Hussein invaded Iran; stalemate; 1 million dead.
  • Oil shocks (1973, 1979): transformed global economy; shifted power to producer states.

📗 AS Level Content

The Role of the UN: Success and Failure

  • Korean War (1950–53): UN authorised intervention only because USSR boycotted Security Council. Demonstrated UN potential when superpowers agreed; impotence when they disagreed.
  • Congo (1960–64): UN peacekeeping (ONUC) controversial — Lumumba's death, Katanga secession. Led to tighter peacekeeping rules.
  • Suez (1956): UN Emergency Force (first armed peacekeeping force) supervised withdrawal. Success for Hammarskjöld's preventive diplomacy.
  • Cold War paralysis: vetoes by both superpowers. General Assembly resolutions non-binding (e.g., condemning Soviet invasion of Afghanistan).

Superpower Involvement in the Middle East

  • USA: supported Israel (economic/military aid), allied with conservative monarchies (Saudi Arabia, Iran pre-1979), sought to contain Soviet influence.
  • USSR: supported Arab nationalist regimes (Egypt, Syria, Iraq), provided arms and advisors. Lost influence after Sadat's expulsion of Soviet advisors (1972) and Camp David.
  • Oil as leverage: 1973 embargo showed Arab states could influence Western policy. But long-term effect was Western energy diversification.

Decolonisation: Causes and Patterns

  • Immediate causes: WWII weakened European powers; Japanese victories shattered myth of white supremacy; USA and USSR supported self-determination.
  • Varied paths: peaceful transfer (India, Ghana); armed struggle (Algeria, Vietnam, Kenya — Mau Mau); negotiated settlement (most of British Africa).
  • Challenges of independence: artificial borders, ethnic divisions, lack of trained administrators, economic dependence (neocolonialism). Cold War competition often exacerbated instability.

The Cuban Missile Crisis: Decision-Making

  • ExComm deliberations: options ranged from air strike to blockade to diplomatic solution. Kennedy chose blockade — demonstrated resolve while allowing time.
  • Khrushchev's motives: close missile gap (USSR had few ICBMs), defend Cuba from invasion, bargain for concessions on Berlin/Turkey.
  • Resolution: public Soviet withdrawal for public US non-invasion pledge + secret Turkish missile deal. Both sides claimed victory; both made concessions.
  • Legacy: "eyeball to eyeball" mythology (later debunked — more negotiation than brinkmanship). Hotline and arms control followed.

📙 A2 Level Content

The Cold War as a Global System

  • Bipolarity shaped international relations but did not determine everything. Non-aligned states carved out autonomy (Tito, Nasser, India).
  • Dependency theory (André Gunder Frank): decolonisation did not end economic exploitation; former colonies remained in periphery, exporting raw materials to core capitalist states.
  • Modernisation theory (Walt Rostow): all societies progress through stages to industrial modernity; US aid could accelerate development. Critiqued as ethnocentric and ignoring structural inequalities.

The Middle East: Local and International Dimensions

  • Arab-Israeli conflict was not purely Cold War proxy war. Local factors (religion, nationalism, refugees, water) were at least as important as superpower rivalry.
  • Camp David showed USA could broker peace without USSR. But Palestinian issue remained unresolved, leading to continued conflict.
  • Iranian Revolution: anti-Western, anti-communist, Islamist. Confounded both superpowers. Demonstrated limits of Cold War framework.

Arms Control and Détente

  • Limited Test Ban Treaty (1963); NPT (1968); SALT I (1972) — froze ICBM and SLBM numbers; ABM Treaty limited missile defences.
  • SALT II (1979) signed but not ratified due to Soviet invasion of Afghanistan.
  • Détente's limits: regional conflicts continued; human rights issues (Helsinki) exploited by dissidents but ignored by governments.
  • Second Cold War (1979–85): Afghanistan, Euromissiles, Reagan's rhetoric ("evil empire"). Détente declared dead.

Essay Frameworks

  • "The UN failed to keep the peace during the Cold War": Vetoes, superpower bypassing, limited enforcement. Counter: Korean War, Suez, peacekeeping successes, post-Cold War expansion of role.
  • "Decolonisation was driven by the Cold War": Superpowers pressured empires; independence movements gained support. Counter: weakened Europe after WWII was primary cause; nationalism predated Cold War.
  • "The Cuban Missile Crisis was a victory for the USA": Missiles removed, Khrushchev backed down, hotline established. Counter: secret Turkey deal, USA pledged not to invade Cuba, crisis showed dangers of nuclear brinkmanship.

📜 Worked Example

Question: To what extent was Germany responsible for WWI?

Essay Plan:
FOR: Blank cheque, Schlieffen Plan, naval expansion, Fischer thesis
AGAINST: Austrian ultimatum, Russian mobilisation, alliance system, Clark "sleepwalkers"
Conclusion: Significant responsibility but not sole blame
Writing narrative instead of analysis. History essays need CAUSATION — explain WHY and SIGNIFICANCE.
Always use SPECIFIC EVIDENCE — dates, names, statistics. "On 28 June 1914" not "in 1914".

⚡ Quick Recall

  • Long-term: Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism
  • Short-term: Assassination of Franz Ferdinand
  • Treaty of Versailles: War guilt, reparations, territorial losses
  • Cold War: Containment, Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan

Source Analysis Framework

  • Content: What does the source say? What information does it provide?
  • Provenance: Who wrote it? When? Why? What is their perspective?
  • Reliability: Is it trustworthy? What biases might exist?
  • Utility: How useful is it for a specific enquiry?

Essay Structure

  • Introduction: Define key terms, set out argument/line of reasoning
  • Paragraphs: Point → Evidence → Explanation → Link back to question
  • Synthesis: Compare factors, weigh importance, show interconnections
  • Conclusion: Judgment based on evidence, avoid introducing new points
Always answer the EXACT question asked. Underline key words ("To what extent", "How far", "Most important") and address them directly.
