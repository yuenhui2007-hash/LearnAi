Atomic Particles & Nuclear Notation

Every atom consists of a central nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons in energy levels or shells. Understanding the properties of these subatomic particles is fundamental to all of chemistry.

ParticleRelative MassRelative ChargeLocationAbsolute Mass (kg)
Proton1+1Nucleus1.673 × 10⁻²⁷
Neutron10Nucleus1.675 × 10⁻²⁷
Electron1/1836 ≈ 0.0005-1Electron shells9.109 × 10⁻³¹
📘 Key Definitions

Atomic number (Z): The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. It defines the element.

Mass number (A): The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.

Isotopes: Atoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A).

Relative atomic mass (Aᵣ): The weighted average mass of an atom of an element compared to 1/12th the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons
Atomic number (Z) = number of protons = number of electrons (in neutral atom)
Number of neutrons = A − Z
Worked Example: Nuclear Notation

Write the nuclear symbol for an atom with 17 protons and 18 neutrons.

Solution: Z = 17 (chlorine), A = 17 + 18 = 35. Symbol: ³⁵₁₇Cl

Worked Example: Isotopes

Chlorine has two stable isotopes: ³⁵Cl (75.8%) and ³⁷Cl (24.2%). Calculate Aᵣ.

Solution: Aᵣ = (35 × 75.8 + 37 × 24.2) / 100 = (2653 + 895.4) / 100 = 35.5 (to 1 d.p.)

⚠️ Common Misconceptions

• Isotopes do NOT have different chemical properties — only physical properties like mass and density differ because chemistry depends on electrons, not neutrons.

• Relative atomic mass is NOT simply the average of isotope masses — it must be weighted by abundance.

• The mass number is always a whole number; relative atomic mass is usually not.

For a positive ion: electrons = Z − charge. For a negative ion: electrons = Z + |charge|.

Electronic Configuration & Ionisation Energy

Electrons occupy energy levels (shells) around the nucleus. Each shell contains subshells (s, p, d, f) which hold orbitals. An orbital can hold a maximum of 2 electrons with opposite spins.

📘 Key Definitions

Shell (principal quantum number n): Main energy level. n = 1, 2, 3...

Subshell: s (1 orbital, 2e⁻ max), p (3 orbitals, 6e⁻ max), d (5 orbitals, 10e⁻ max), f (7 orbitals, 14e⁻ max).

Orbital: A region of space where an electron may be found. Maximum 2 electrons per orbital (Pauli exclusion principle).

Aufbau Principle — Filling Order
  1. Fill lowest energy orbitals first
  2. Each orbital gets one electron before pairing (Hund's rule)
  3. Maximum 2 electrons per orbital with opposite spins
Filling order: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s → 4f → 5d → 6p...
Worked Example: Electronic Configurations

Fe (Z=26): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁶ (or [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s²)

Fe²⁺: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ (lose 4s electrons first!)

Fe³⁺: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁵ (half-filled d subshell is extra stable)

Exceptions to remember: Cr = [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu = [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹. A half-filled or fully-filled d subshell gives extra stability.

Ionisation Energy

First ionisation energy (IE₁): energy required to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous +1 ions.

X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻

Trends in First Ionisation Energy

Across a period (left to right): Generally increases due to increasing nuclear charge with similar shielding. Smaller atomic radius means electrons are held more tightly.

Down a group: Decreases because outer electrons are further from the nucleus and there is more shielding from inner shells.

Explain the dip at Group 13 (e.g., Al):
Al: [Ne] 3s² 3p¹. The 3p electron is slightly higher in energy than 3s and is partially shielded by the 3s electrons, so it is removed more easily than expected → lower IE.
Explain the dip at Group 16 (e.g., S):
S: [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴. The fourth p electron must pair up in an already-occupied orbital. Electron-electron repulsion in the paired orbital makes it easier to remove → lower IE than P.
Worked Example: Explaining IE Trend

Explain why magnesium has a higher first ionisation energy than aluminium.

Solution: Mg: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s²; Al: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p¹. Al's outer electron is in a 3p orbital which is at a slightly higher energy than 3s and is shielded by the 3s² pair. Less energy is needed to remove it. In Mg, both outer electrons are in the same 3s subshell with no additional shielding, so they are held more tightly.

⚠️ Common Misconceptions

• Do NOT say "the 3p electron is further from the nucleus" — 3s and 3p are at roughly similar distances.

• Always mention nuclear charge, atomic radius, AND shielding when explaining trends.

• Ionisation energy refers to gaseous atoms — state symbols matter!

Mass Spectrometry & Isotopes

Mass spectrometry is an analytical technique used to determine relative atomic masses, molecular masses, and structural information. It is essential in identifying unknown compounds.

Steps in Mass Spectrometry (Electron Impact)
  1. Vaporisation: Sample is heated to become a gas
  2. Ionisation: High-energy electrons bombard sample molecules, knocking off an electron → M⁺ (molecular ion)
  3. Acceleration: Positive ions are accelerated by an electric field
  4. Deflection: Magnetic field deflects ions based on mass-to-charge ratio (m/z). Lighter ions are deflected more.
  5. Detection: Ions hit a detector; signal intensity is proportional to abundance
In a mass spectrum, the peak with the highest m/z value (on the far right) is the molecular ion peak M⁺ and gives the relative molecular mass.
Relative atomic mass = Σ(isotope mass × relative abundance) / 100
Worked Example: Calculating Aᵣ from Mass Spectrum

A sample of boron gives peaks at m/z 10 (abundance 20%) and m/z 11 (abundance 80%).

Solution: Aᵣ = (10 × 20 + 11 × 80) / 100 = (200 + 880) / 100 = 10.8

Worked Example: Identifying an Element

An element has peaks at m/z 63 (69.2%) and m/z 65 (30.8%). Identify the element.

Solution: Aᵣ = (63 × 69.2 + 65 × 30.8) / 100 = 63.6. This is copper (Cu).

Fragmentation Patterns

The molecular ion M⁺ is often unstable and breaks into fragments. These fragments produce peaks at lower m/z values and give structural clues:

  • m/z 15: CH₃⁺
  • m/z 29: CHO⁺ or C₂H₅⁺
  • m/z 43: C₃H₇⁺ or CH₃CO⁺
  • m/z 77: C₆H₅⁺ (phenyl)

💡 Examiner Tips

  • Always show your working when calculating Aᵣ from isotope data
  • The molecular ion peak gives Mr; fragmentation peaks give structural information
  • For Time of Flight (TOF) mass spec: ions with same kinetic energy, lighter ones travel faster
  • Remember: M+1 peak from ¹³C (~1.1% natural abundance)

Exam-Style Questions

📝 Exam Question 1

(a) Define the term first ionisation energy. [2]

(b) Write an equation for the second ionisation energy of magnesium. [1]

(c) Explain why aluminium has a lower first ionisation energy than magnesium. [3]

Mark Scheme:
(a) Energy required to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms (1) to form one mole of gaseous +1 ions (1).
(b) Mg⁺(g) → Mg²⁺(g) + e⁻ (1)
(c) Al has electron in 3p orbital (1) which is higher in energy / more shielded by 3s² (1) so less energy needed to remove it / electron is less strongly held (1).
📝 Exam Question 2

A sample of magnesium contains three isotopes with the following abundances: ²⁴Mg 78.6%, ²⁵Mg 10.1%, ²⁶Mg 11.3%. Calculate the relative atomic mass to 1 decimal place. [2]

Mark Scheme:
Aᵣ = (24 × 78.6 + 25 × 10.1 + 26 × 11.3) / 100 (1) = 24.3 (1)

📝 Quick Summary

  • Protons: mass 1, charge +1; Neutrons: mass 1, charge 0; Electrons: mass negligible, charge -1
  • Mass number = protons + neutrons; Atomic number = protons
  • Isotopes have same Z, different A; same chemical properties, different physical properties
  • Electronic configuration follows Aufbau, Pauli, Hund's rules
  • Cr = [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹; Cu = [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹
  • IE increases across period, decreases down group; dips at Gp 13 and Gp 16
  • Mass spec steps: Vaporise → Ionise → Accelerate → Deflect → Detect
  • Aᵣ = Σ(isotope mass × abundance) / 100

💡 Exam Tips

  • Remember the mass spectrometry steps: Vaporise, Ionise, Accelerate, Deflect, Detect
  • Calculate RAM using isotope abundances — always show working
  • Electronic configuration: 4s fills before 3d, but empties before 3d in ions
  • When explaining IE trends, always mention nuclear charge, atomic radius, and shielding
  • Use state symbols (g) in ionisation energy equations
