Amines and Amides

Amines are organic derivatives of ammonia where one or more H atoms are replaced by alkyl or aryl groups. They are weak bases due to the lone pair on nitrogen.

📘 Key Definitions

Primary amine: One alkyl group bonded to N (RNH₂).

Secondary amine: Two alkyl groups bonded to N (R₂NH).

Tertiary amine: Three alkyl groups bonded to N (R₃N).

Amide: Contains the −CONH₂ group, formed from a carboxylic acid and an amine.

Basicity of Amines

Aliphatic amines are stronger bases than ammonia because alkyl groups donate electrons (+I effect), increasing electron density on nitrogen and making the lone pair more available.

Aromatic amines (e.g., phenylamine) are weaker bases than ammonia because the lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring, making it less available.

CH₃CH₂NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺ + OH⁻     Kb > Kb(NH₃)
C₆H₅NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ C₆H₅NH₃⁺ + OH⁻     Kb < Kb(NH₃)

Preparation of Amines

  1. Reduction of nitriles: CH₃CN + 2[H] → CH₃CH₂NH₂ (LiAlH₄ or H₂/Ni)
  2. Reduction of nitro compounds: C₆H₅NO₂ + 6[H] → C₆H₅NH₂ + 2H₂O (Sn/HCl or H₂/Ni)
  3. Hofmann degradation: RCONH₂ + Br₂ + 4OH⁻ → RNH₂ + CO₃²⁻ + 2Br⁻ + 2H₂O

Amide Formation and Hydrolysis

Formation: CH₃COCl + NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + HCl
Or: CH₃COOH + NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + H₂O (heat, catalyst)

Acid hydrolysis: CH₃CONH₂ + H₂O + H⁺ → CH₃COOH + NH₄⁺
Alkaline hydrolysis: CH₃CONH₂ + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + NH₃

Amino Acids and Proteins

Amino acids contain both an amino group (−NH₂) and a carboxylic acid group (−COOH). They are the building blocks of proteins.

📘 Key Definitions

Amphoteric: Can act as both an acid and a base.

Zwitterion: A dipolar ion with both positive and negative charges.

Isoelectric point: The pH at which an amino acid exists mainly as a zwitterion.

At low pH (acidic): H₃N⁺-CH(R)-COOH
At isoelectric point: H₃N⁺-CH(R)-COO⁻ (zwitterion)
At high pH (alkaline): H₂N-CH(R)-COO⁻

Peptide Bond Formation

Amino acids link together by condensation reactions between the −NH₂ of one amino acid and the −COOH of another, forming a peptide bond (−CONH−).

H₂N-CH(R₁)-COOH + H₂N-CH(R₂)-COOH →
H₂N-CH(R₁)-CO-NH-CH(R₂)-COOH + H₂O
The peptide bond has partial double bond character due to resonance, which restricts rotation and gives the protein backbone its planar structure. This is crucial for protein folding and secondary structure.

Condensation Polymerisation

Condensation polymers are formed when monomers join together with the elimination of a small molecule, usually water.

Polyesters

Dicarboxylic acid + Diol → Polyester + Water

n HOOC-C₆H₄-COOH + n HO-CH₂-CH₂-OH →
−[OC-C₆H₄-COO-CH₂-CH₂-O]ₙ− + 2n H₂O

Terephthalic acid + Ethane-1,2-diol → PET (polyethylene terephthalate)

Polyamides

Dicarboxylic acid + Diamine → Polyamide + Water

n HOOC-(CH₂)₄-COOH + n H₂N-(CH₂)₆-NH₂ →
−[OC-(CH₂)₄-CONH-(CH₂)₆-NH]ₙ− + 2n H₂O

Adipic acid + Hexane-1,6-diamine → Nylon-6,6
Nylon is a polyamide; Terylene/PET is a polyester. Both are condensation polymers. The numbers in Nylon-6,6 refer to the number of carbon atoms in each monomer (6 in adipic acid, 6 in diamine).

Natural Polymers

Proteins

Polymers of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. Essential for structure (collagen, keratin), enzymes (catalysts), transport (haemoglobin), and defence (antibodies).

Carbohydrates

Polysaccharides like starch and cellulose are polymers of glucose.

Starch: α-glucose units joined by α-1,4-glycosidic bonds. Forms helical structure. Energy storage in plants.

Cellulose: β-glucose units joined by β-1,4-glycosidic bonds. Forms straight chains with hydrogen bonding between chains. Structural material in plants.

Humans can digest starch but not cellulose because we lack the enzyme cellulase.

DNA and RNA

Nucleic acids are polymers of nucleotides. Each nucleotide contains:

  • A phosphate group
  • A pentose sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA)
  • A nitrogenous base (A, T, G, C in DNA; A, U, G, C in RNA)
DNA forms a double helix held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs: A-T (2 H-bonds) and G-C (3 H-bonds). The sequence of bases encodes genetic information.

Exam-Style Questions

📝 Exam Question 1

(a) Explain why phenylamine is a weaker base than ethylamine. [2]

(b) Write an equation for the reaction of ethylamine with HCl. [1]

Mark Scheme:
(a) Lone pair on N is delocalised into the benzene ring (1). Less available for protonation / electron density on N is reduced (1).
(b) C₂H₅NH₂ + HCl → C₂H₅NH₃⁺Cl⁻ (1).
📝 Exam Question 2

(a) Draw the structure of the dipeptide formed from glycine and alanine. [2]

(b) Name the type of reaction and the bond formed. [2]

Mark Scheme:
(a) Correct peptide link shown between two amino acids with correct side chains (2). [Deduct 1 for missing atoms]
(b) Condensation reaction (1). Peptide / amide bond (1).

📝 Quick Summary

  • Amines: weak bases; aliphatic > NH₃ > aromatic in basicity
  • Amides: neutral; hydrolysed by acid or alkali
  • Amino acids: amphoteric; form zwitterions at isoelectric pH
  • Peptide bond: −CONH− formed by condensation of two amino acids
  • Protein structure: primary (sequence), secondary (H-bonds), tertiary (various forces), quaternary (multiple chains)
  • Condensation polymers: polyesters (ester linkages), polyamides (amide linkages)
  • PET: polyester; Nylon-6,6: polyamide
  • Starch: α-glucose; Cellulose: β-glucose
  • DNA: double helix; A-T (2 H-bonds), G-C (3 H-bonds)

💡 Exam Tips

  • Always draw peptide bonds correctly: −CO−NH−
  • Remember the difference between addition and condensation polymers
  • For amine basicity, consider both inductive effects and lone pair delocalisation
  • Starch vs cellulose: α vs β glycosidic links make all the difference
  • Condensation polymer repeating units must show the atoms lost as water
