Climate Change

IB DP Geography HL/SL — Option B / Core

Overview

Climate Change examines the causes, impacts, and responses to global climate change. It is one of the most topical and frequently examined areas in IB Geography, appearing in Paper 1, Paper 2, and the IA. Understanding the enhanced greenhouse effect, climate vulnerability, and mitigation/adaptation strategies is essential.

2.1 Causes of Climate Change

Natural Causes

  • Orbital variations (Milankovitch cycles).
  • Solar output changes.
  • Volcanic eruptions (aerosols reflect sunlight).

Anthropogenic (Human) Causes

  • Burning fossil fuels: releases CO₂.
  • Deforestation: reduces carbon sinks.
  • Agriculture: livestock produce methane (CH₄); rice paddies emit methane.
  • Industry: cement production releases CO₂; refrigerants release CFCs/HFCs.

The Enhanced Greenhouse Effect

Human activities increase concentrations of greenhouse gases (GHGs), trapping more infrared radiation and raising global temperatures.

Major GHGs: CO₂ (fossil fuels), CH₄ (agriculture, landfill), N₂O (fertilisers), CFCs/HFCs (industry)
CO₂ is the most significant by volume; CH₄ has much higher global warming potential (GWP) per molecule.

2.2 Impacts of Climate Change

  • Rising temperatures: heatwaves, changed growing seasons.
  • Melting ice: sea level rise, loss of albedo (positive feedback).
  • Extreme weather: more intense hurricanes, droughts, floods.
  • Ocean acidification: CO₂ dissolves in seawater, harming marine ecosystems.
  • Ecosystem disruption: species migration, coral bleaching, biodiversity loss.
  • Human systems: food security threats, water scarcity, health impacts (disease vectors), climate migration.

2.3 Mitigation

Efforts to reduce or prevent emission of greenhouse gases.

  • Renewable energy: solar, wind, hydroelectric, geothermal.
  • Energy efficiency: improved building standards, public transport.
  • Carbon capture and storage (CCS): capturing CO₂ at source.
  • Reforestation/afforestation: increasing carbon sinks.
  • International agreements: Kyoto Protocol (1997), Paris Agreement (2015).

2.4 Adaptation

Adjusting to actual or expected climate change effects.

  • Hard engineering: sea walls, flood barriers, coastal realignment.
  • Soft engineering: beach nourishment, wetland restoration, managed retreat.
  • Agricultural: drought-resistant crops, changing planting dates.
  • Social: early warning systems, climate-resilient infrastructure.

2.5 Vulnerability & Resilience

  • Vulnerability: susceptibility to harm from climate change. Determined by exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity.
  • Resilience: ability to recover from climate shocks.
  • Low-income countries are often more vulnerable due to limited adaptive capacity, despite lower emissions.

Common Misconceptions

  • Weather vs. climate: Weather is short-term; climate is long-term average. A cold day does not disprove climate change.
  • Ozone depletion vs. greenhouse effect: Different issues. CFCs deplete ozone but also contribute to warming.
  • Climate change vs. global warming: Global warming refers to temperature increase; climate change includes all impacts (extreme weather, sea level, etc.).

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: Short-answer questions on greenhouse gases, climate data, and mitigation strategies.
  • Paper 2: Essays on vulnerability, mitigation vs. adaptation, or international cooperation.
  • IA: Climate-related fieldwork (coastal erosion, urban heat island, carbon footprint) is common.

Practice Questions

  • Explain the enhanced greenhouse effect and distinguish it from the natural greenhouse effect.
  • Discuss the impacts of climate change on food security in sub-Saharan Africa.
  • Evaluate the effectiveness of international agreements in addressing climate change.
  • Compare hard and soft engineering approaches to coastal adaptation.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Causes: fossil fuels, deforestation, agriculture, industry → enhanced greenhouse effect.
  • Impacts: temperature rise, sea level rise, extreme weather, ocean acidification, ecosystem loss.
  • Mitigation: reduce emissions (renewables, efficiency, CCS, reforestation).
  • Adaptation: adjust to impacts (hard/soft engineering, agricultural change, early warning).
  • Vulnerability = exposure + sensitivity − adaptive capacity.
  • Low-income countries often most vulnerable despite lowest emissions.

Exam Tips

  • Use specific data (e.g., "global temperatures have risen by ~1.1°C since pre-industrial times").
  • Always distinguish between mitigation and adaptation.
  • Include named case studies (e.g., Bangladesh adaptation, Germany's Energiewende).
  • For evaluation, consider effectiveness, cost, equity, and timescale.
  • Diagrams of the greenhouse effect should show incoming shortwave and outgoing longwave radiation.
