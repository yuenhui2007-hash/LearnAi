Electrolysis Fundamentals

📘 Key Definitions

Electrolysis: The decomposition of a compound into its elements by an electric current.

Electrolyte: A compound that conducts electricity when molten or in aqueous solution, and is decomposed in the process.

Anode: The positive electrode where oxidation occurs (Anode = AnOx = Anode Oxidation).

Cathode: The negative electrode where reduction occurs (Cathode = RedCat = Reduction Cathode).

Cation: A positively charged ion that moves toward the cathode.

Anion: A negatively charged ion that moves toward the anode.

Electrolysis is a non-spontaneous process driven by electrical energy. It is used extensively in industry for extracting reactive metals (aluminium, sodium), producing chlorine and sodium hydroxide, and electroplating.

At the anode (+), oxidation occurs: species lose electrons.
At the cathode (−), reduction occurs: species gain electrons.
Remember: OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).

Selective Discharge at Electrodes

When more than one ion is present, the ion discharged depends on:

  • At the cathode: The cation with the more positive (less negative) E° value is reduced preferentially.
  • At the anode: The anion with the less negative (more positive) E° value is oxidised preferentially. However, concentrated halides are oxidised before OH⁻.

Electrolysis of Molten Compounds

In molten electrolysis, there is only one cation and one anion, so the products are predictable. This method is used to extract very reactive metals that cannot be extracted by carbon reduction.

Worked Example: Electrolysis of Molten NaCl

Cathode (−): Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na(l)     (reduction)

Anode (+): 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂(g) + 2e⁻     (oxidation)

Overall: 2Na⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → 2Na(l) + Cl₂(g)

This is the Downs process for producing sodium metal. The cell operates at ~600°C with a steel cathode and carbon anode.

Worked Example: Electrolysis of Molten Al₂O₃ (Hall-Héroult Process)

Cathode (−): Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al(l)

Anode (+): 2O²⁻ → O₂(g) + 4e⁻

The carbon anodes react with oxygen: C + O₂ → CO₂, so they burn away and must be replaced regularly.

Cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) is added to lower the melting point from 2072°C to ~950°C.

Hall-Héroult Process — Industrial Details
  1. Aluminium ore (bauxite) is purified to Al₂O₃.
  2. Al₂O₃ is dissolved in molten cryolite at ~950°C.
  3. Steel tank lined with carbon acts as the cathode.
  4. Carbon anodes are suspended in the electrolyte.
  5. Liquid aluminium sinks to the bottom and is tapped off.
  6. The process is energy-intensive (~15 MWh per tonne of Al).

Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions

In aqueous solutions, H⁺ and OH⁻ (from water) are also present and may compete with the electrolyte ions for discharge.

Cathode rules:
• If the metal is above hydrogen in the reactivity series (more reactive: K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al), H⁺ is discharged instead: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂(g)
• If the metal is below hydrogen (less reactive: Cu, Ag, Au), the metal ion is discharged: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)

Anode rules:
• If halide ions (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻) are present in high concentration, they are discharged: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻
• If halide concentration is low or no halides present, OH⁻ is discharged: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻
• With inert electrodes (platinum/graphite), these rules apply. With reactive electrodes (copper), the electrode itself may dissolve.
Worked Example: Electrolysis of Concentrated NaCl (Brine)

Cathode: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂(g)    (Na⁺ is too reactive to be discharged)

Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂(g) + 2e⁻    (Cl⁻ in high concentration)

Solution: Na⁺ and OH⁻ remain → NaOH(aq) is the other product

Overall: 2NaCl + 2H₂O → H₂ + Cl₂ + 2NaOH

This is the chlor-alkali process, producing three valuable products.

Worked Example: Electrolysis of Dilute H₂SO₄

Cathode: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂(g)

Anode: 4OH⁻ → O₂(g) + 2H₂O + 4e⁻

Overall: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂

This is essentially the electrolysis of water. The volume ratio of H₂:O₂ is 2:1.

Worked Example: Electrolysis of CuSO₄ with Copper Electrodes

Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)    (copper deposited)

Anode: Cu(s) → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻    (copper dissolves)

Result: The concentration of CuSO₄ remains constant. This is the principle of electroplating and copper refining.

Standard Electrode Potentials

Standard electrode potentials (E°) measure the tendency of a species to gain electrons (be reduced) under standard conditions (1 mol dm⁻³, 1 atm, 298 K).

📘 Key Definition

Standard electrode potential (E°): The potential difference (voltage) measured when a half-cell is connected to a standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) under standard conditions. E° for the SHE is defined as 0.00 V.

Standard hydrogen electrode: 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂(g)     E° = 0.00 V
Conditions: [H⁺] = 1.0 mol dm⁻³, P(H₂) = 1 atm, T = 298 K, Pt electrode.
Half-reactionE° (V)
Li⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Li−3.04
K⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ K−2.93
Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Ca−2.87
Na⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Na−2.71
Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Mg−2.37
Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ ⇌ Al−1.66
Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn−0.76
Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Fe−0.44
Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Pb−0.13
2H⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂0.00
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu+0.34
Ag⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Ag+0.80
Cl₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Cl⁻+1.36
F₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2F⁻+2.87
More negative E° = stronger reducing agent (more easily oxidised).
More positive E° = stronger oxidising agent (more easily reduced).
Li is the strongest reducing agent; F₂ is the strongest oxidising agent.

Electrochemical Cells

An electrochemical (voltaic/galvanic) cell converts chemical energy into electrical energy through a spontaneous redox reaction. It consists of two half-cells connected by a salt bridge and an external circuit.

Components of an Electrochemical Cell
  1. Two half-cells: Each contains an electrode in an electrolyte.
  2. Electrodes: Metal electrodes or inert electrodes (Pt, graphite) for gas/ion half-cells.
  3. Salt bridge: Contains inert electrolyte (e.g., KNO₃) to complete the circuit and maintain electrical neutrality.
  4. External circuit: Allows electrons to flow from anode to cathode.
Cell potential: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode
(Use reduction potentials for both; subtract the anode potential)

For a spontaneous reaction: E°cell > 0
The more positive E° half-cell is the cathode (reduction).
The more negative E° half-cell is the anode (oxidation).
Worked Example: Calculating Cell Potential

Calculate the standard cell potential for: Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s)

Solution:

Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn     E° = −0.76 V

Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu     E° = +0.34 V

Cathode (reduction): Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu     E° = +0.34 V

Anode (oxidation): Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻     E° = −0.76 V

E°cell = +0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V

Worked Example: Predicting Spontaneity

Will chlorine oxidise bromide ions under standard conditions?

Cl₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Cl⁻     E° = +1.36 V

Br₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Br⁻     E° = +1.07 V

E°cell = +1.36 − (+1.07) = +0.29 V > 0

Yes, the reaction is spontaneous: Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂

Cell diagrams: Anode | Anode ion || Cathode ion | Cathode
Single vertical line = phase boundary
Double vertical line = salt bridge
Always write anode on the left, cathode on the right.

Quantitative Electrolysis & Faraday's Laws

📘 Key Definitions

Faraday constant (F): The charge on one mole of electrons. F = 96485 C mol⁻¹ ≈ 96500 C mol⁻¹.

Faraday's First Law: The mass of substance produced at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity passed.

Faraday's Second Law: The masses of different substances produced by the same quantity of electricity are proportional to their equivalent masses (M/z).

Q = I × t     (charge = current × time)
n(e⁻) = Q / F     (moles of electrons = charge / Faraday constant)
n(substance) = Q / (z × F)     (z = number of electrons in half-equation)
mass = n × M     (mass = moles × molar mass)
Worked Example: Calculating Mass Deposited

A current of 2.00 A is passed through molten Al₂O₃ for 30 minutes. Calculate the mass of aluminium produced.

Solution:

Q = I × t = 2.00 × (30 × 60) = 3600 C

Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al, so z = 3

n(Al) = Q / (z × F) = 3600 / (3 × 96500) = 0.01244 mol

mass = 0.01244 × 27.0 = 0.336 g

Worked Example: Finding Current or Time

How long will it take to deposit 1.00 g of copper from CuSO₄ solution using a current of 0.500 A?

Solution:

Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu, so z = 2

n(Cu) = 1.00 / 63.5 = 0.01575 mol

Q = n × z × F = 0.01575 × 2 × 96500 = 3039 C

t = Q / I = 3039 / 0.500 = 6078 s = 101 minutes

Fuel Cells and Batteries

Hydrogen-Oxygen Fuel Cell

Fuel cells convert chemical energy directly into electrical energy with high efficiency (~60%). They are environmentally friendly because water is the only product.

Acidic Fuel Cell

Anode: H₂(g) → 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻

Cathode: ½O₂(g) + 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → H₂O(l)

Overall: H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O

E°cell = +1.23 V

Alkaline Fuel Cell

Anode: H₂(g) + 2OH⁻(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + 2e⁻

Cathode: ½O₂(g) + H₂O(l) + 2e⁻ → 2OH⁻(aq)

Overall: H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O

Advantages of fuel cells: high efficiency, no pollutants, continuous operation with fuel supply.
Disadvantages: expensive catalysts (Pt), hydrogen storage challenges, production of H₂ often involves fossil fuels.

Rechargeable Batteries — Lithium-ion

Li-ion batteries are widely used in portable electronics and electric vehicles.

Discharge:

Anode: Li → Li⁺ + e⁻

Cathode: Li⁺ + CoO₂ + e⁻ → LiCoO₂

Advantages: High energy density, lightweight, no memory effect, low self-discharge.

Exam-Style Questions

📝 Exam Question 1

(a) State the products at each electrode during the electrolysis of concentrated aqueous sodium chloride. [2]

(b) Write half-equations for the reactions at each electrode. [2]

(c) Explain why sodium metal is NOT produced at the cathode. [2]

Mark Scheme:
(a) Cathode: hydrogen (1). Anode: chlorine (1).
(b) Cathode: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ (1). Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ (1).
(c) Na⁺ is more difficult to reduce than H⁺ / H⁺ has a more positive E° / Na⁺ requires more energy to gain an electron / water is more easily reduced (1). Na is very reactive / high in reactivity series (1).
📝 Exam Question 2

A current of 1.50 A is passed through copper(II) sulfate solution for 20.0 minutes. Calculate the mass of copper deposited at the cathode. [3]

Mark Scheme:
Q = I × t = 1.50 × 1200 = 1800 C (1)
n(Cu) = Q / (2F) = 1800 / (2 × 96500) = 0.00933 mol (1)
mass = 0.00933 × 63.5 = 0.592 g (1)
📝 Exam Question 3

(a) Calculate E°cell for the reaction: Fe(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Fe²⁺(aq) + Cu(s) [2]

(b) Predict whether Ag⁺ will oxidise Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ under standard conditions. [2]

Mark Scheme:
(a) E°cell = +0.34 − (−0.44) = +0.78 V (2). [1 mark for correct values, 1 for calculation]
(b) Ag⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Ag    E° = +0.80 V; Fe³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺    E° = +0.77 V (1).
E°cell = +0.80 − (+0.77) = +0.03 V > 0, so yes (1).

📝 Quick Summary

  • Anode = oxidation (+ in electrolysis, − in cells); Cathode = reduction (− in electrolysis, + in cells)
  • Molten electrolysis: only one cation/anion; products are predictable
  • Aqueous electrolysis: H⁺ and OH⁻ compete; use reactivity series and concentration rules
  • E° measures tendency to be reduced; more positive = stronger oxidising agent
  • E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode; must be positive for spontaneity
  • Q = I × t; n(e⁻) = Q/F; n(substance) = Q/(zF)
  • Fuel cells: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O; high efficiency, clean

💡 Exam Tips

  • Remember OIL RIG and AN OX / RED CAT mnemonics
  • In aqueous electrolysis, reactive metals (above H) produce H₂ at cathode
  • Concentrated halides produce halogen at anode; dilute solutions produce O₂
  • Always use reduction potentials when calculating E°cell
  • Check units: current in A, time in s, charge in C
  • Fuel cell equations differ in acidic vs alkaline media — be careful!
