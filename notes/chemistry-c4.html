IGCSE Content

Electrolysis Basics

Electrolysis is the decomposition of a compound into its elements by an electric current. It requires:

  • An electrolyte (molten or aqueous ionic compound)
  • Two electrodes (anode = positive, cathode = negative)
  • A direct current supply

Key rule: Cations (positive) move to cathode (negative), anions (negative) move to anode (positive).

Molten Electrolysis

At cathode: Metal ions gain electrons (reduction) → metal deposited

At anode: Non-metal ions lose electrons (oxidation) → non-metal produced

Example: Molten NaCl
Cathode: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na
Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻

Aqueous Electrolysis

Water complicates the process. At the cathode, either the metal ion or H⁺ is reduced; at the anode, either the anion or OH⁻ is oxidised.

At cathode:

  • If metal is above hydrogen in reactivity series: H₂ is produced (2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂)
  • If metal is below hydrogen: metal is deposited (e.g., Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu)

At anode:

  • If halide ion present (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻): halogen produced (e.g., 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻)
  • If OH⁻ or oxyanion (SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻): O₂ produced (4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻)

Electrolysis of Dilute vs Concentrated Solutions

Dilute NaCl: Cathode: H₂; Anode: O₂

Concentrated NaCl (brine): Cathode: H₂; Anode: Cl₂

This is the basis of the chlor-alkali industry.

Electrolysis Reactions Summary

Electrolysis of CuSO₄(aq) with inert electrodes:
Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (pink deposit)
Anode: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ (bubbles)

Electrolysis of CuSO₄(aq) with copper electrodes:
Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
Anode: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ (copper dissolves)

Electroplating

The object to be plated is the cathode. The plating metal is the anode. The electrolyte contains ions of the plating metal. This is used to prevent corrosion and improve appearance.

AS Level Content

Standard Electrode Potentials

The standard electrode potential (E°) is the potential difference between a half-cell and a standard hydrogen electrode under standard conditions (298 K, 1 mol/dm³, 1 atm).

Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE): 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂(g)    E° = 0.00 V

Calculating Cell EMF

E°cell = E°reduction (cathode) - E°reduction (anode)
or
E°cell = E°(more positive) - E°(less positive)

A positive E°cell means the reaction is feasible (spontaneous) under standard conditions.

Predicting Reactions Using E° Values

  • Species with more positive E° are better oxidising agents (get reduced)
  • Species with more negative E° are better reducing agents (get oxidised)
  • The half-cell with more positive E° will undergo reduction
  • The half-cell with less positive E° will undergo oxidation

Standard Electrode Potential Values to Know

K⁺/K: -2.93 V   Na⁺/Na: -2.71 V   Mg²⁺/Mg: -2.37 V
Zn²⁺/Zn: -0.76 V   Fe²⁺/Fe: -0.44 V   Pb²⁺/Pb: -0.13 V
2H⁺/H₂: 0.00 V   Cu²⁺/Cu: +0.34 V   Ag⁺/Ag: +0.80 V
Cl₂/Cl⁻: +1.36 V   F₂/F⁻: +2.87 V

Factors Affecting Electrode Potential

Under non-standard conditions, use the Nernst equation (not required at AS but conceptually):

  • Concentration: higher [ion] shifts equilibrium, affecting potential
  • Temperature: affects position of equilibrium
  • Pressure: for gas electrodes

Redox Equations

To combine half-equations:

  1. Write both half-equations with equal electrons
  2. Add them together, cancelling electrons
MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O    E° = +1.52 V
Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻
Multiply second by 5, then add:
MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O

Electrolysis Quantitative Calculations

Q = I × t (charge = current × time in seconds)
n = Q / F (moles = charge / Faraday constant)
F = 96500 C mol⁻¹

A2 Level Content

Equilibrium Constant Kc and Electrode Potentials

There is a relationship between Gibbs free energy, equilibrium constant, and electrode potential:

ΔG° = -nFE°cell = -RT ln K
At 298 K: E°cell = (0.059 / n) × log K

Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions with Concentration Effects

At A2, you need to explain which ion is preferentially discharged based on:

  • Standard electrode potentials
  • Concentration of ions
  • Overpotential effects (e.g., high overpotential for H₂ on some electrodes)

Fuel Cells

Hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell:

Anode: H₂ + 2OH⁻ → 2H₂O + 2e⁻ (alkaline)
Cathode: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻
Overall: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Advantages: only product is water, efficient energy conversion. Disadvantages: H₂ storage, expensive catalysts.

Storage Cells and Batteries

  • Lead-acid battery: Rechargeable, used in cars
  • Lithium-ion batteries: High energy density, used in electronics

Last-Minute Summary

🎯 Key Points to Remember

  • Cations → cathode; Anions → anode
  • At cathode: if metal above H in reactivity series → H₂; below → metal deposited
  • At anode: halides → halogen; otherwise → O₂
  • E°cell = E°(cathode) - E°(anode) = E°(more positive) - E°(less positive)
  • Positive E°cell = feasible reaction
  • More positive E° = better oxidising agent; more negative = better reducing agent
  • Q = It; n = Q/F; F = 96500 C mol⁻¹
  • Fuel cells: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O; alkaline or acidic conditions

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Confusing anode and cathode (anode = oxidation, not always positive in electrolysis)
  • Not reversing the sign when using E°(reduction) for oxidation half-cells
  • Forgetting to balance electrons when combining half-equations
  • Using time in minutes instead of seconds in Q = It
  • Not considering concentration effects in aqueous electrolysis
