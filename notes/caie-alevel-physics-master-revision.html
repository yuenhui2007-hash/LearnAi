Physical Quantities

Detailed Notes

1. Physical Quantities

A physical quantity is any quantity that can be measured. Physical quantities are divided into two categories:

  • Base quantities — cannot be defined in terms of other quantities. The seven SI base quantities are: length (m), mass (kg), time (s), electric current (A), temperature (K), amount of substance (mol), luminous intensity (cd).
  • Derived quantities — defined in terms of base quantities. Examples: velocity (m/s), force (kg·m/s²), energy (kg·m²/s²).

2. Units

The SI system (Système International) is the standard system of units used in science:

  • All measurements should be expressed in SI units or appropriate prefixes.
  • Common prefixes: nano (n, 10⁻⁹), micro (μ, 10⁻⁶), milli (m, 10⁻³), centi (c, 10⁻²), kilo (k, 10³), mega (M, 10⁶), giga (G, 10⁹).
Homogeneity of equations: An equation is homogeneous if the units on both sides are the same. This is a useful check but does not prove an equation is correct.

3. Scalars and Vectors

  • Scalar — has magnitude only (e.g., mass, speed, distance, energy, temperature).
  • Vector — has magnitude and direction (e.g., displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum).
  • Vectors are added using the parallelogram law or by resolving into components.

4. Measurement Techniques

  • Precision — refers to the smallest division on the measuring instrument.
  • Accuracy — how close a measurement is to the true value.
  • Uncertainty — the range within which the true value is expected to lie.
  • Percentage uncertainty = (uncertainty / measured value) × 100%.

5. Significant Figures & Decimal Places

  • Significant figures (s.f.): All non-zero digits + zeros between non-zero digits + trailing zeros after decimal
  • Rules for calculations: For multiplication/division, answer should have same s.f. as least precise value
  • Example: 2.34 × 1.2 = 2.8 (2 s.f., not 2.808)
  • Decimal places (d.p.): For addition/subtraction, answer should have same d.p. as least precise value
Always check the number of significant figures required in the question. Method marks are often lost for incorrect rounding.

6. Estimation & Order of Magnitude

  • Order of magnitude: The power of 10 closest to a value (e.g., 5000 ≈ 10⁴)
  • Fermi estimation: Quick approximation using powers of 10 to check reasonableness
  • Common values: Speed of light ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s, Earth's radius ≈ 6.4 × 10⁶ m, proton mass ≈ 1.7 × 10⁻²⁷ kg

Additional Applications

  • Real-world context: Engineering applications, medical physics, environmental monitoring
  • Experimental methods: Common apparatus, measurement techniques, error analysis
  • SI units in practice: Prefix usage in scientific and everyday contexts

5. Dimensions & Dimensional Analysis

  • Dimensions of base quantities: [L] for length, [M] for mass, [T] for time, [I] for current, [Θ] for temperature
  • Force: [MLT⁻²] — mass × acceleration
  • Energy: [ML²T⁻²] — force × distance
  • Power: [ML²T⁻³] — energy / time
  • Dimensional consistency: Both sides of any physical equation must have the same dimensions

📐 Worked Example

Question: The period T of a simple pendulum is given by T = 2π√(l/g). Verify this is dimensionally correct.

[T] = T
[2π√(l/g)] = √(L / LT⁻²) = √(T²) = T ✓
Both sides have dimension [T] — equation is dimensionally consistent.

Kinematics

Detailed Notes

1. Kinematics

Kinematics is the study of motion without considering the forces causing it.

  • Displacement (s) — distance moved in a specified direction (vector).
  • Velocity (v) — rate of change of displacement (vector).
  • Speed — rate of change of distance (scalar).
  • Acceleration (a) — rate of change of velocity (vector).
s = ut + ½at²
v = u + at
v² = u² + 2as
s = ½(u + v)t

These equations apply only for uniform acceleration in a straight line.

2. Graphs of Motion

  • Displacement-time graph: gradient = velocity.
  • Velocity-time graph: gradient = acceleration; area under graph = displacement.
  • Acceleration-time graph: area under graph = change in velocity.

3. Dynamics

Newton's Laws of Motion:

  • First Law: A body remains at rest or moves with constant velocity unless acted upon by a resultant force.
  • Second Law: F = ma. The resultant force is proportional to the rate of change of momentum.
  • Third Law: When body A exerts a force on body B, body B exerts an equal and opposite force on body A.
Momentum: p = mv
Impulse: FΔt = Δ(mv)
Conservation of momentum: total momentum before = total momentum after (in a closed system)

4. Projectile Motion

  • Horizontal motion: constant velocity (aₓ = 0).
  • Vertical motion: constant acceleration due to gravity (aᵧ = g ≈ 9.81 m/s²).
  • Horizontal and vertical motions are independent.

7. Projectile Motion — Full Analysis

  • Horizontal: aₓ = 0, vₓ = u cos θ (constant), sₓ = ut cos θ
  • Vertical: aᵧ = −g, vᵧ = u sin θ − gt, sᵧ = ut sin θ − ½gt²
  • Time of flight: T = 2u sin θ / g (when landing at same height)
  • Max height: H = u² sin²θ / 2g (at t = u sin θ / g)
  • Range: R = u² sin 2θ / g (max at θ = 45°)

Dynamics

Detailed Notes

1. Types of Forces

  • Contact forces: normal reaction, friction, tension, upthrust.
  • Non-contact forces: gravitational, electrostatic, magnetic.
  • Resultant force — the single force that has the same effect as all forces acting on a body.

2. Force Diagrams (Free-Body Diagrams)

Always draw forces acting on the body in question. Label each force clearly.

3. Density and Pressure

Density: ρ = m/V
Pressure: P = F/A
Fluid pressure: P = ρgh

4. Upthrust and Archimedes' Principle

Archimedes' Principle: The upthrust on a body immersed in a fluid is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced.

  • If upthrust > weight, body floats.
  • If upthrust < weight, body sinks.
  • If upthrust = weight, body is in equilibrium (neutral buoyancy).

5. Friction

  • Static friction — acts when there is no relative motion. Maximum static friction = μₛN.
  • Kinetic (sliding) friction — acts when there is relative motion. Fₖ = μₖN.
  • μₛ > μₖ always.

  • Experimental methods: Common apparatus, measurement techniques, error analysis

5. Newton's Laws in Rotational Motion

  • Torque (τ): τ = r × F = rF sin θ, measured in N·m
  • Moment of inertia (I): I = Σmr², depends on mass distribution
  • Angular acceleration (α): α = τ/I (rotational equivalent of F=ma)
  • Rotational kinetic energy: Eₖ = ½Iω²

Last-Minute Summary

  • Contact forces: normal, friction, tension, upthrust; Non-contact: gravitational, electrostatic, magnetic
  • Resultant force = vector sum of all forces
  • Density ρ = m/V; Pressure P = F/A; Fluid pressure P = ρgh
  • Archimedes: upthrust = weight of fluid displaced
  • Float: upthrust > weight; Sink: upthrust < weight
  • Static friction max = μₛN; Kinetic friction = μₖN; μₛ > μₖ

Forces

Detailed Notes

1. Work

Work done by a force is the product of the force and the displacement in the direction of the force.

W = Fs cos θ

Work is a scalar. Unit: joule (J).

2. Energy

  • Kinetic energy: Eₖ = ½mv²
  • Gravitational potential energy: Eₚ = mgh (near Earth's surface)
  • Elastic potential energy: Eₑ = ½kx²

3. Principle of Conservation of Energy

Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed from one form to another. In a closed system, total energy is constant.

4. Power

Power: P = W/t = E/t
Also: P = Fv (when force and velocity are in same direction)

Unit: watt (W) = J/s.

5. Efficiency

Efficiency = (useful power output / total power input) × 100%

No machine is 100% efficient due to energy losses (friction, heat, sound).

Last-Minute Summary

  • Work W = Fs cos θ (J)
  • Kinetic energy Eₖ = ½mv²; GPE Eₚ = mgh; Elastic Eₑ = ½kx²
  • Conservation of energy: total energy constant in closed system
  • Power P = W/t = E/t = Fv (W)

Work, Energy, Power

Detailed Notes

1. Stress and Strain

Stress: σ = F/A (unit: Pa or N/m²)
Strain: ε = ΔL/L (no units)
Young Modulus: E = σ/ε = (F/A)/(ΔL/L)

2. Force-Extension Graph

  • Limit of proportionality — beyond this, Hooke's law no longer applies.
  • Elastic limit — beyond this, the material will not return to its original length.
  • Yield point — the point at which plastic deformation begins.
  • Breaking stress — the stress at which the material fractures.

3. Hooke's Law

F = kx (valid up to the limit of proportionality)

Force is proportional to extension.

4. Elastic and Plastic Deformation

  • Elastic deformation: material returns to original shape when force is removed.
  • Plastic deformation: material does not return to original shape; permanent deformation occurs.

5. Strain Energy

Strain energy = ½Fx = ½kx² = area under force-extension graph

  • Limit of proportionality → Hooke's law stops
  • Elastic limit → permanent deformation begins
  • Elastic: returns to shape; Plastic: permanent deformation

Solids & Fluids

Detailed Notes

1. Progressive Waves

  • Amplitude (A): maximum displacement from equilibrium.
  • Wavelength (λ): distance between two consecutive points in phase.
  • Frequency (f): number of oscillations per second (Hz).
  • Period (T): time for one complete oscillation (T = 1/f).
  • Wave speed: v = fλ.
v = fλ

2. Transverse and Longitudinal Waves

  • Transverse: oscillations perpendicular to direction of energy transfer (e.g., light, waves on a string).
  • Longitudinal: oscillations parallel to direction of energy transfer (e.g., sound).

3. Electromagnetic Spectrum

From longest to shortest wavelength: radio waves → microwaves → infrared → visible light → ultraviolet → X-rays → gamma rays.

All EM waves travel at c ≈ 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum.

4. Superposition

When two or more waves meet, the resultant displacement is the vector sum of individual displacements.

  • Constructive interference: crest meets crest → amplitude increases.
  • Destructive interference: crest meets trough → amplitude decreases.

5. Stationary Waves

Formed when two waves of same frequency and amplitude travel in opposite directions. Nodes (zero amplitude) and antinodes (maximum amplitude) are formed.

6. Diffraction

The spreading of waves as they pass through an aperture or around an obstacle. Significant when aperture size ≈ wavelength.

Additional Applications

  • Real-world context: Engineering applications, medical physics, environmental monitoring
  • Experimental methods: Common apparatus, measurement techniques, error analysis
  • SI units in practice: Prefix usage in scientific and everyday contexts

Common Exam Question Types

  • Definition questions — state precisely, use correct terminology
  • Calculation questions — show working, include units, check significant figures
  • Explanation questions — use "because" to link cause and effect
  • Graph questions — read axes carefully, describe trends using data
Always define quantities before using them in explanations. E.g., "Force is the rate of change of momentum, therefore..."

Last-Minute Summary

  • Wave equation: v = fλ
  • Transverse: oscillations ⊥ to energy transfer; Longitudinal: oscillations ∥ to energy transfer
  • EM spectrum: radio → microwave → IR → visible → UV → X-ray → gamma (all at c = 3×10⁸ m/s)
  • Superposition: vector sum of displacements
  • Constructive: crest+crest; Destructive: crest+trough
  • Stationary waves: same f, same A, opposite directions → nodes and antinodes
  • Diffraction significant when gap ≈ λ

Temperature

Detailed Notes

1. Electric Field Strength

E = F/Q (force per unit positive charge)
For a point charge: E = Q/(4πε₀r²)

Unit: N/C or V/m.

2. Electric Potential

V = W/Q (work done per unit charge)
For a point charge: V = Q/(4πε₀r)

Unit: volt (V).

3. Relationship between E and V

E = -dV/dr

The electric field strength is the negative gradient of potential.

4. Uniform Electric Field

E = V/d (between parallel plates)

5. Coulomb's Law

F = Q₁Q₂/(4πε₀r²)

Force between two point charges is proportional to the product of charges and inversely proportional to the square of their separation.

6. Electric Field Patterns

  • Point charge: radial field, lines radiate outward (+) or inward (-).
  • Parallel plates: uniform field, straight parallel lines.
  • Dipole: field lines from + to -.

  • E = F/Q; Point charge: E = Q/(4πε₀r²)

  • Uniform field: E = V/d

Detailed Notes

1. Electric Current

I = Q/t (rate of flow of charge)

2. Charge Carriers

3. Potential Difference (Voltage)

4. Resistance

5. Ohm's Law

Thermodynamics

Detailed Notes

1. Circuit Symbols

Know standard circuit symbols: cell, battery, resistor, variable resistor, lamp, switch, ammeter, voltmeter, fuse, diode, LED, thermistor, LDR.

2. Series Circuits

  • Current same through all components.
  • Total p.d. = sum of individual p.d.s.
  • Total resistance: R_total = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...

3. Parallel Circuits

  • P.d. same across all branches.
  • Total current = sum of branch currents.
  • Total resistance: 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + ...

4. Potential Divider

V_out = V_in × R₂/(R₁ + R₂)

5. Kirchhoff's Laws

  • First Law (Current): Total current entering a junction = total current leaving.
  • Second Law (Voltage): Sum of e.m.f.s = sum of p.d.s around any closed loop.

6. Internal Resistance

E = V + Ir = I(R + r)

Where E = e.m.f., V = terminal p.d., I = current, R = external resistance, r = internal resistance.

7. Sensors

  • Thermistor: resistance decreases as temperature increases.
  • LDR (Light Dependent Resistor): resistance decreases as light intensity increases.

Oscillations

Detailed Notes

1. Magnetic Fields

  • Magnetic field lines show the direction a north pole would move.
  • Field around a straight wire: concentric circles (right-hand grip rule).
  • Field around a solenoid: similar to a bar magnet.

2. Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

F = BIL sin θ

Where B = magnetic flux density, I = current, L = length, θ = angle between current and field.

3. Force on a Moving Charge

F = BQv sin θ

For a charge Q moving with velocity v in a magnetic field.

4. Magnetic Flux Density

B = F/IL (when θ = 90°)

Unit: tesla (T).

5. Electromagnetic Induction

An e.m.f. is induced in a conductor when:

  • There is relative motion between conductor and magnetic field.
  • There is a change in magnetic flux linkage.
Faraday's Law: ε = -N(dΦ/dt)
Lenz's Law: The induced current flows in a direction to oppose the change causing it.

6. Transformer

Vₛ/Vₚ = Nₛ/Nₚ
For ideal transformer: VₚIₚ = VₛIₛ

Step-up: Nₛ > Nₚ; Step-down: Nₛ < Nₚ.

Waves

Detailed Notes

1. Atomic Structure

  • Atom consists of protons, neutrons, and electrons.
  • Proton number (Z) = number of protons.
  • Nucleon number (A) = number of protons + neutrons.
  • Isotopes: same Z, different number of neutrons.

2. Radioactive Decay

  • Alpha (α): helium nucleus (₂⁴He). Stopped by paper. High ionisation.
  • Beta (β): fast electron (₋₁⁰e). Stopped by aluminium. Moderate ionisation.
  • Gamma (γ): electromagnetic radiation. Stopped by thick lead. Low ionisation.

3. Decay Equations

Alpha: ᴬ_Z X → ᴬ⁻⁴_Z₋₂ Y + ₂⁴He
Beta: ᴬ_Z X → ᴬ_Z₊₁ Y + ₋₁⁰e + ν̄

4. Half-Life

The time taken for half the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay.

N = N₀(½)^(t/t½)

5. Mass-Energy Equivalence

E = mc²

Mass defect = difference between mass of nucleus and sum of masses of constituent nucleons.

6. Nuclear Fission and Fusion

  • Fission: heavy nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei + neutrons + energy.
  • Fusion: two light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus + energy.

Detailed Notes

1. Temperature Scales

  • Celsius (°C): based on freezing (0°C) and boiling (100°C) of water.
  • Kelvin (K): absolute scale. T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15.
  • Absolute zero = 0 K = -273.15°C.

2. Thermal Expansion

  • Solids: ΔL = αL₀ΔT (linear expansion).
  • Liquids: ΔV = βV₀ΔT (volume expansion).
  • Gases: obey ideal gas law.

3. Ideal Gas Law

pV = nRT = NkT
Where n = moles, N = number of molecules, R = 8.31 J/(mol·K), k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K

4. Kinetic Theory of Gases

pV = ⅓Nmc²
Average kinetic energy of a molecule: Eₖ = ½mc² = (3/2)kT

5. Specific Heat Capacity

Q = mcΔT

Energy required to raise temperature of mass m by ΔT.

6. Specific Latent Heat

Q = mL

Energy required for phase change at constant temperature.

7. First Law of Thermodynamics

ΔU = Q - W

Change in internal energy = heat supplied - work done by system.

Detailed Notes

1. Astronomical Distances

  • Astronomical Unit (AU): mean Earth-Sun distance ≈ 1.50 × 10¹¹ m.
  • Light-year (ly): distance light travels in one year ≈ 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m.

2. Stellar Luminosity

L = 4πr²σT⁴

Where σ = Stefan-Boltzmann constant = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴).

3. Wien's Displacement Law

λ_max T = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K

Peak wavelength is inversely proportional to temperature.

4. Doppler Effect for Light

Δλ/λ ≈ v/c (for v << c)
  • Redshift: source moving away (λ increases).
  • Blueshift: source moving toward (λ decreases).

5. Hubble's Law

v = H₀d

Recession velocity proportional to distance. H₀ ≈ 70 km/(s·Mpc).

6. The Big Bang

  • Universe began from a singularity ~13.8 billion years ago.
  • Evidence: cosmic microwave background radiation, redshift of galaxies, abundance of light elements.

7. Stellar Evolution

  • Main sequence: hydrogen fusion in core.
  • Red giant: core hydrogen exhausted, shell burning.
  • White dwarf: electron degeneracy pressure supports star.
  • Supernova: massive star explodes, may leave neutron star or black hole.
