Cell Membranes & Transport

9700 · 0610

Detailed Notes

1

Fluid Mosaic Model

The fluid mosaic model describes the structure of the cell surface membrane as a dynamic, flexible structure composed of lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates.

Fluid mosaic model: A model of membrane structure in which the membrane is a bilayer of phospholipids with embedded proteins, and both lipids and proteins are free to move laterally within the membrane.

Components of the Membrane

  • Phospholipid bilayer: The basic framework. Hydrophilic heads face the aqueous environment on both sides; hydrophobic tails face each other in the membrane interior. This creates a barrier to water-soluble substances.
  • Intrinsic (integral) proteins: Span the entire membrane (transmembrane proteins). Many are channel proteins or carrier proteins involved in transport. Some are enzymes or receptors.
  • Extrinsic (peripheral) proteins: Found on the inner or outer surface, attached to intrinsic proteins or phospholipid heads. Often involved in cell signalling or maintaining cell structure.
  • Cholesterol: Found in animal cell membranes. Its rigid steroid ring reduces phospholipid movement at high temperatures (reducing fluidity) and prevents tight packing at low temperatures (maintaining fluidity). It also reduces water permeability.
  • Glycoproteins: Proteins with carbohydrate chains attached to the outer surface. Involved in cell recognition, cell adhesion, and as receptors for hormones and neurotransmitters.
  • Glycolipids: Lipids with carbohydrate chains attached to the outer surface. Also involved in cell recognition and as antigens (e.g., ABO blood group antigens).
The membrane is described as "fluid" because phospholipids and proteins can move laterally within the layer. It is a "mosaic" because proteins are scattered throughout the phospholipid bilayer like tiles in a mosaic.

Diffusion

Diffusion: The net movement of molecules or ions from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration down a concentration gradient, as a result of random motion. No metabolic energy is required.

Factors Affecting Rate of Diffusion

  • Concentration gradient: Steeper gradient = faster diffusion.
  • Thickness of exchange surface: Thinner surface = faster diffusion (shorter distance).
  • Surface area: Larger surface area = faster diffusion (more molecules can cross simultaneously).
  • Temperature: Higher temperature = more kinetic energy = faster diffusion.
  • Size of molecule: Smaller molecules diffuse faster.

Simple Diffusion

Small, non-polar molecules can pass directly through the phospholipid bilayer:

  • Oxygen (O₂), carbon dioxide (CO₂), nitrogen (N₂)
  • Steroid hormones (lipid-soluble)
  • Some small polar molecules: water (slowly), urea, glycerol

Facilitated Diffusion

Larger or charged molecules cannot pass through the hydrophobic core of the membrane. They cross using transport proteins:

  • Channel proteins: Form aqueous pores through the membrane. Specific to certain ions (e.g., voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, aquaporins for water). No conformational change needed.
  • Carrier proteins: Bind the molecule, change shape, and release it on the other side. Example: glucose carrier (GLUT) proteins. Slower than channel proteins but can transport larger molecules.
Facilitated diffusion is still passive — it does not require ATP. The movement is down a concentration gradient. The proteins simply provide a pathway for molecules that cannot cross the lipid bilayer directly.
Worked Example: Factors Affecting Diffusion

Question: Explain why oxygen can diffuse directly through the cell membrane but glucose cannot. [2 marks]

Answer:

  • Oxygen is a small, non-polar molecule that can dissolve in and pass through the hydrophobic interior of the phospholipid bilayer (1).
  • Glucose is a large, polar molecule that cannot pass through the hydrophobic core and requires a carrier protein for facilitated diffusion (1).
3

Osmosis

Osmosis: The net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential across a partially permeable membrane, down a water potential gradient.

Water Potential (Ψ)

  • Water potential is the tendency of water molecules to move from one place to another.
  • Pure water has the highest water potential, defined as zero (0 kPa).
  • Adding solutes lowers the water potential (makes it more negative).
  • Water potential = solute potential (Ψs) + pressure potential (Ψp).
  • Water always moves from less negative (higher) to more negative (lower) water potential.
Ψ = Ψs + Ψp
Ψs = solute potential (always negative or zero)
Ψp = pressure potential (usually positive, zero in pure water at atmospheric pressure)

Effects on Animal Cells

SolutionWater PotentialEffect on Animal Cell
Hypotonic (e.g., pure water)Higher than cell cytoplasmWater enters by osmosis; cell swells and may burst (lysis)
Isotonic (e.g., 0.9% NaCl)Equal to cell cytoplasmNo net water movement; cell maintains normal shape
Hypertonic (e.g., concentrated salt)Lower than cell cytoplasmWater leaves by osmosis; cell shrinks (crenation)

Effects on Plant Cells

SolutionEffect on Plant Cell
HypotonicWater enters; cell swells but does not burst due to cell wall; becomes turgid (turgor pressure supports plant)
IsotonicNo net water movement; cell is flaccid (plant wilts)
HypertonicWater leaves; cytoplasm pulls away from cell wall (plasmolysis); plant wilts severely
Worked Example: Water Potential Calculation

Question: A cell has a solute potential of -400 kPa and a pressure potential of 200 kPa. Calculate the water potential of the cell. [1 mark]

Solution:

Ψ = Ψs + Ψp = -400 + 200 = -200 kPa
Common Misconception: Students often say water moves from "high concentration to low concentration." This is imprecise. Water moves from higher water potential to lower water potential. A concentrated solution has a lower water potential (more negative) than pure water, even though it has a higher solute concentration.

Active Transport

Active transport: The movement of molecules or ions across a membrane against a concentration gradient, using energy from ATP and carrier proteins.
  • Requires metabolic energy (ATP): Usually directly hydrolysed by the carrier protein (ATPase activity).
  • Against concentration gradient: Moves substances from low to high concentration.
  • Uses carrier proteins: Specific to the molecule being transported.
  • Saturable: Rate reaches a maximum (Vmax) when all carriers are occupied.

Examples of Active Transport

  • Na⁺/K⁺ pump (sodium-potassium ATPase): Found in all animal cell membranes. Pumps 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in, maintaining electrochemical gradients essential for nerve impulses and secondary active transport.
  • Calcium pump (Ca²⁺-ATPase): Pumps Ca²⁺ into the sarcoplasmic reticulum of muscle cells, allowing muscle relaxation.
  • Proton pump (H⁺-ATPase): In plant cells, pumps H⁺ out of the cell, creating a proton gradient used for co-transport of sugars and amino acids.
  • Glucose absorption in ileum: Na⁺-glucose co-transport uses the Na⁺ gradient (established by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump) to drive glucose uptake against its concentration gradient.
Worked Example: Sodium-Potassium Pump

Question: Explain why the sodium-potassium pump is described as an example of active transport. [3 marks]

Answer:

  • It moves Na⁺ and K⁺ ions against their concentration gradients (Na⁺ out, K⁺ in) (1).
  • It requires energy from ATP hydrolysis to change the shape of the pump protein (1).
  • It is a carrier protein that is specific to Na⁺ and K⁺ ions (1).
5

Bulk Transport

Endocytosis

The bulk uptake of material into the cell by invagination of the cell membrane:

  • Phagocytosis ("cell eating"): Solid particles are engulfed. The membrane extends around the particle, forming a phagosome that fuses with a lysosome for digestion. Example: white blood cells (phagocytes) engulfing bacteria.
  • Pinocytosis ("cell drinking"): Droplets of extracellular fluid are taken in. Non-specific uptake of dissolved substances.
  • Receptor-mediated endocytosis: Specific molecules bind to receptors on the cell surface, triggering invagination. Example: uptake of cholesterol via LDL receptors.

Exocytosis

The bulk export of material from the cell:

  • Vesicles containing substances (e.g., hormones, neurotransmitters, enzymes) fuse with the cell membrane.
  • The vesicle membrane becomes part of the cell membrane.
  • Requires ATP.
  • Example: secretion of insulin by pancreatic β cells; release of acetylcholine at synapses.
Both endocytosis and exocytosis require ATP and involve the cytoskeleton (microfilaments of actin help form vesicles). They are used for molecules too large to cross the membrane by other means.

Surface Area to Volume Ratio

As organisms increase in size, their surface area to volume ratio decreases. This has profound implications for transport and exchange:

  • Small organisms (e.g., bacteria, single-celled protists) have a high SA:V ratio, allowing efficient exchange of materials directly across their surface.
  • Large organisms have a low SA:V ratio, so they need specialised exchange surfaces (lungs, gills, intestines) and transport systems (blood, xylem, phloem) to maintain sufficient exchange.
Surface Area to Volume Ratio = Surface Area / Volume
For a cube of side length L: SA = 6L², V = L³, SA:V = 6/L
Worked Example: SA:V Ratio Calculation

Question: Calculate the SA:V ratio for a cube with sides of 2 cm. [2 marks]

Solution:

Surface area = 6 × (2)² = 24 cm²
Volume = (2)³ = 8 cm³
SA:V = 24 / 8 = 3:1 (or 3 cm⁻¹)
🔬 Real-World Application: Adaptations for Exchange

Specialised exchange surfaces maximise SA:V ratio:

  • Lungs: Millions of alveoli provide ~70 m² of surface area.
  • Small intestine: Villi and microvilli increase surface area ~600-fold.
  • Root hairs: Increase surface area for water and mineral absorption.
  • Leaves: Flat, thin structure with stomata for gas exchange.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Fluid mosaic: Phospholipid bilayer + intrinsic/extrinsic proteins + cholesterol + glycoproteins/glycolipids. Fluid because components move; mosaic because proteins are scattered.
  • Simple diffusion: Small non-polar molecules (O₂, CO₂) pass through lipid bilayer down concentration gradient.
  • Facilitated diffusion: Channel proteins (ions, water) or carrier proteins (glucose) allow larger/charged molecules to cross down concentration gradient. No ATP.
  • Osmosis: Water moves from high Ψ to low Ψ across partially permeable membrane. Pure water Ψ = 0. Solutes lower Ψ.
  • Active transport: Against gradient; needs ATP and carrier proteins. Examples: Na⁺/K⁺ pump, proton pumps, glucose co-transport.
  • Endocytosis: Bulk uptake (phagocytosis, pinocytosis). Exocytosis: bulk export. Both need ATP.
  • SA:V ratio: Decreases as size increases. Large organisms need specialised exchange surfaces and transport systems.

💡 Exam Tips

  • Always describe the fluid mosaic model in detail — phospholipid bilayer, proteins, cholesterol, glycoproteins, and explain why it's called "fluid" and "mosaic"
  • When explaining osmosis, use the term "water potential" not "water concentration"
  • Pure water has Ψ = 0; adding solutes makes Ψ negative. Water moves toward more negative Ψ
  • Distinguish clearly: diffusion (any molecule, down gradient), osmosis (water only, across membrane), facilitated diffusion (needs protein, down gradient, no ATP), active transport (needs protein, against gradient, needs ATP)
  • For active transport, always mention ATP and carrier proteins
  • Phagocytosis is for solids; pinocytosis is for liquids
  • Calculate SA:V carefully — show all working
