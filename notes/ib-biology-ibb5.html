Evolution & Biodiversity

IB DP Biology HL/SL — Topic 5

Overview

Evolution and Biodiversity (Topic 5) explores the mechanisms of evolution, evidence for natural selection, and methods of classification. This topic connects genetics, ecology, and paleontology and is tested across all papers.

5.1 Evidence for Evolution

  • Fossil record: shows progression of life forms over geological time.
  • Biogeography: distribution of species reflects continental drift and isolation.
  • Homologous structures: similar underlying structure, different function (e.g., vertebrate limbs) → common ancestry.
  • Analogous structures: similar function, different structure (e.g., insect wing vs. bird wing) → convergent evolution.
  • Vestigial structures: reduced non-functional structures (e.g., human appendix, whale pelvis).
  • DNA/protein comparisons: more similar sequences indicate closer evolutionary relationships.

5.2 Natural Selection

  • Variation exists within populations.
  • More offspring are produced than can survive (struggle for existence).
  • Individuals with advantageous traits are more likely to survive and reproduce.
  • Over generations, advantageous alleles increase in frequency.
Evolution = change in allele frequencies in a population over time.

5.3 Speciation

  • Speciation: formation of new species when populations become reproductively isolated.
  • Allopatric speciation: geographic barrier separates populations.
  • Sympatric speciation: populations diverge without geographic separation (e.g., polyploidy in plants).

5.4 Classification

  • Binomial nomenclature: genus + species (e.g., Homo sapiens).
  • Five kingdoms: Prokaryotae, Protoctista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia (traditional).
  • Three domains: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya (modern).

Cladistics

  • Classification based on evolutionary relationships (phylogeny).
  • Cladogram: branching diagram showing evolutionary relationships.
  • Based on shared derived characteristics (synapomorphies).

Common Misconceptions

  • Evolution is "just a theory": In science, a theory is a well-supported explanation — evolution is supported by multiple lines of evidence.
  • Survival of the fittest: "Fittest" means best adapted to the environment, not necessarily strongest.
  • Natural selection vs. evolution: Natural selection is a mechanism of evolution; evolution is the broader pattern of change.
  • Homology vs. analogy: Homologous structures indicate common ancestry; analogous structures indicate convergent evolution.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: 3–4 MCQs on evidence for evolution, natural selection, and classification.
  • Paper 2: Structured questions on speciation mechanisms, cladogram interpretation, and evolution of antibiotic resistance.
  • Paper 3: Data analysis of fossil records or genetic divergence data.
  • IA: Biodiversity surveys, natural selection simulations, and classification projects are possible.

Practice Questions

  • Explain how homologous structures provide evidence for evolution.
  • Describe the process of allopatric speciation.
  • Outline how natural selection leads to antibiotic resistance in bacteria.
  • Explain why DNA sequence comparison is more reliable than morphological comparison for determining evolutionary relationships.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Evidence: fossils, biogeography, homology, analogy, vestigial structures, DNA comparisons.
  • Natural selection: variation → overproduction → differential survival → inheritance.
  • Speciation: allopatric (geographic barrier) and sympatric (no barrier).
  • Classification: binomial nomenclature; three domains; cladistics based on shared derived characteristics.

Exam Tips

  • Read each question carefully and identify the command term before answering.
  • Use specific examples (e.g., Darwin's finches, peppered moth) to illustrate natural selection.
  • For cladogram questions, identify the outgroup and shared derived traits.
  • In "explain" questions, describe each step of the process clearly.
  • Distinguish carefully between homologous and analogous structures.
