Cell Theory

All living organisms are composed of cells. The cell is the basic unit of life. All cells arise from pre-existing cells by division.

The cell theory is one of the foundational principles of biology, first proposed in the 19th century by Schleiden, Schwann, and Virchow. It states three key principles that apply to all known living organisms:

  1. All living organisms are composed of one or more cells. From the simplest bacteria to the most complex mammals, every living thing has a cellular basis. Some organisms are unicellular (e.g., Amoeba, Paramecium, bacteria), while others are multicellular (e.g., plants, animals, fungi).
  2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and organisation in organisms. All the functions of life — metabolism, growth, response, reproduction, homeostasis — occur within cells or are mediated by cells. Even in multicellular organisms, each cell carries out the fundamental processes of life.
  3. All cells arise from pre-existing cells. This principle, added by Rudolf Virchow, rejects the idea of spontaneous generation. New cells are produced by the division of existing cells through processes such as mitosis and meiosis.
Cell: The basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms, consisting of cytoplasm enclosed by a plasma membrane and (in eukaryotes) containing a nucleus and other organelles.
🔬 Real-World Application: Stem Cell Research

Because all cells arise from pre-existing cells, stem cell research focuses on understanding how undifferentiated cells divide and differentiate into specialised cell types. This has revolutionary applications in regenerative medicine, including growing replacement tissues for damaged organs and treating conditions such as Parkinson's disease, spinal cord injuries, and type 1 diabetes.

Common Misconception: Viruses are often thought to be living organisms. However, viruses are acellular — they lack cellular structure and cannot carry out metabolic processes independently. They only reproduce inside host cells, so they are not considered living by the cell theory.

Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic Cells

Living cells are divided into two fundamental types based on their internal structure: prokaryotic and eukaryotic. Understanding the differences between these cell types is essential for classification (taxonomy) and for understanding the evolution of life on Earth.

Structural Differences

Ribosomes70S (smaller: 50S + 30S subunits)80S in cytoplasm (60S + 40S); 70S in mitochondria and chloroplasts
Membrane-bound organellesAbsentPresent (mitochondria, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, etc.)
Cell wallPresent in most; contains peptidoglycan (murein)Present in plants (cellulose), fungi (chitin), algae; absent in animals
Size1–10 μm (typically 0.5–5 μm)10–100 μm
ExamplesBacteria, ArchaeaProtists, Fungi, Plants, Animals

Key Details to Remember

  • Prokaryotes include bacteria and archaea. They are the most abundant and diverse organisms on Earth, found in almost every environment.
  • The nucleoid is not a membrane-bound organelle but a concentrated region of DNA. Some prokaryotes also contain small circular DNA molecules called plasmids, which carry accessory genes (e.g., antibiotic resistance).
  • 70S ribosomes are smaller than 80S ribosomes and are the target of some antibiotics (e.g., tetracyclines, macrolides) that do not affect eukaryotic ribosomes.
  • Some prokaryotes have mesosomes (infoldings of the plasma membrane) that may be involved in respiration or cell division, though their exact function is debated.
  • Capsules and slime layers are found outside the cell wall of some bacteria, providing protection and aiding attachment to surfaces.
  • Flagella in prokaryotes are structurally different from eukaryotic flagella — they are made of the protein flagellin and rotate like a propeller, whereas eukaryotic flagella have a 9+2 microtubule arrangement and whip-like motion.
Worked Example: Identifying Cell Types

Question: A cell is observed under an electron microscope. It measures 2 μm in diameter, has no membrane-bound nucleus, and contains 70S ribosomes. Classify this cell and explain your reasoning.

Answer:

This is a prokaryotic cell (1 mark). The evidence is:

  • Small size (2 μm) is typical of prokaryotes, which are generally 1–10 μm (1 mark).
  • Absence of a membrane-bound nucleus — prokaryotes have a nucleoid region instead (1 mark).
  • Presence of 70S ribosomes, which are characteristic of prokaryotes; eukaryotes have 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm (1 mark).
📋 Exam-Style Question

Question: Describe three ways in which a prokaryotic cell differs from a eukaryotic cell. [3 marks]

Mark Scheme:
  • Prokaryotes have no membrane-bound nucleus / DNA is in a nucleoid region (1)
  • Prokaryotes have 70S ribosomes; eukaryotes have 80S ribosomes (1)
  • Prokaryotes lack membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria, ER, Golgi (1)
  • Prokaryotic DNA is circular and naked; eukaryotic DNA is linear and associated with histones (1)
  • Any three correct points accepted

📝 Annotated Diagram Description: Mitochondrion

When drawing or describing a mitochondrion in an exam:

  • Outer membrane: Smooth, fully permeable to small molecules.
  • Inner membrane: Highly folded into cristae to increase surface area for oxidative phosphorylation. Contains electron transport chain proteins and ATP synthase.
  • Matrix: Gel-like substance containing enzymes for the link reaction and Krebs cycle, mitochondrial DNA (circular, like prokaryotic DNA), and 70S ribosomes.
  • Intermembrane space: Between outer and inner membranes; site of proton accumulation during oxidative phosphorylation.

Examiner tip: Always link structure to function. The cristae increase surface area for the electron transport chain and ATP synthase, maximising ATP production.

Worked Example: Structure-Function Relationships

Question: Explain how the structure of the rough endoplasmic reticulum is adapted to its function. [3 marks]

Answer:

  • The RER is composed of a network of membranous sacs (cisternae), providing a large surface area for protein synthesis (1).
  • Ribosomes on the surface synthesise proteins that enter the lumen, where they can be folded and modified in a protected environment (1).
  • The RER is continuous with the nuclear envelope, allowing newly synthesised proteins to be transported directly from the nucleus to the RER for processing (1).
Common Misconception: Students often confuse the functions of the RER and SER. Remember: Rough ER has Ribosomes and makes Roteins. Smooth ER has no ribosomes and makes lipids — think "S" for SER and "S" for Steroids.

Cell Fractionation

Cell fractionation is the process of separating cellular components so they can be studied individually. It involves homogenisation followed by differential centrifugation.

Steps of Cell Fractionation
  1. Homogenisation: Cells are broken open (homogenised) in a cold isotonic buffer.
    • Cold: Reduces enzyme activity that might denature organelles.
    • Isotonic: Same water potential as organelles, preventing osmotic lysis or shrinkage.
    • Buffer: Maintains constant pH to preserve structure and function.
  2. Filtration: The homogenate is filtered through gauze or cheesecloth to remove debris (cell walls, connective tissue).
  3. Ultracentrifugation: The filtrate is spun at increasing speeds to separate organelles by size and density:
    • Low speed (~600 × g): Nuclei pellet at the bottom (heaviest and largest).
    • Medium speed (~10,000–20,000 × g): Mitochondria, lysosomes, and peroxisomes pellet.
    • High speed (~100,000 × g): Ribosomes and small vesicles pellet.
    • Supernatant: Cytosol (soluble cytoplasmic components) remains after all centrifugation steps.
Worked Example: Why Use Cold, Isotonic Buffer?

Question: Explain why a cold isotonic buffer is used during homogenisation. [2 marks]

Answer:

  • Cold temperature reduces metabolic enzyme activity, preventing damage to organelles (1).
  • Isotonic buffer prevents osmotic lysis or shrinkage of organelles by maintaining the same water potential as the organelles (1).
📋 Exam-Style Question

Question: Describe how cell fractionation can be used to isolate mitochondria from liver cells. [4 marks]

Mark Scheme:
  • Liver cells are homogenised in a cold isotonic buffer (1)
  • Filter through gauze to remove cell debris (1)
  • Centrifuge at low speed to pellet nuclei; discard pellet (1)
  • Centrifuge supernatant at medium/high speed to pellet mitochondria (1)

Microscopy & Magnification

Understanding microscopy is essential for cell biology. The two key concepts are magnification and resolution.

Magnification: The degree to which the apparent size of an object is increased. Calculated as:
Magnification = size of image / size of real object
Resolution (resolving power): The ability to distinguish two separate points as distinct. It is the minimum distance between two points that can be seen as separate.

Resolution is more important than magnification. You can magnify an image indefinitely, but if the resolution is poor, the image will just be a blurry enlargement. Electron microscopes have much higher resolution than light microscopes because electrons have shorter wavelengths than visible light (de Broglie wavelength).
Worked Example: Magnification Calculation

Question: A mitochondrion measures 2 μm in real life. In a micrograph, it measures 40 mm. Calculate the magnification.

Solution:

First, convert both measurements to the same units:

Real size = 2 μm = 2 × 10-6 m

Image size = 40 mm = 40 × 10-3 m = 4 × 10-2 m

Magnification = image size / real size = (4 × 10-2) / (2 × 10-6) = 2 × 104 = ×20,000
Common Misconception: Students often confuse the units when calculating magnification. Always convert both measurements to the same unit before dividing. A common error is dividing mm by μm without conversion.

Viruses

Viruses are acellular, meaning they are not cells. They are obligate intracellular parasites — they can only reproduce inside living host cells.

Structure of Viruses

  • Genetic material: Either DNA or RNA (single-stranded or double-stranded), never both.
  • Capsid: Protein coat surrounding the genetic material, composed of protein subunits called capsomeres.
  • Envelope (in some viruses): Lipid bilayer derived from host cell membrane, containing viral glycoproteins (e.g., HIV, influenza).
  • Attachment proteins: On the surface, allowing the virus to bind to specific receptors on host cells.
Viruses are not considered living because they: lack cellular structure, cannot carry out metabolism independently, do not grow, and only reproduce using host cell machinery.
🔬 Real-World Application: Viral Diseases and Vaccines

Viruses cause diseases such as influenza, HIV/AIDS, COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2), measles, and Ebola. Understanding viral structure is crucial for vaccine development. For example, the COVID-19 mRNA vaccines use the virus's spike protein structure to trigger an immune response without causing disease.

📝 Quick Summary

  • Cell theory: all organisms composed of cells; cell is basic unit; cells arise from pre-existing cells
  • Prokaryotes: no nucleus, 70S ribosomes, no membrane-bound organelles, circular naked DNA, peptidoglycan cell wall
  • Eukaryotes: true nucleus, 80S ribosomes, membrane-bound organelles, linear DNA with histones
  • Mitochondria = aerobic respiration (cristae for ETC, matrix for Krebs); Chloroplasts = photosynthesis (thylakoids for light reactions, stroma for Calvin cycle)
  • RER = protein synthesis (has ribosomes); SER = lipid synthesis and detoxification
  • Golgi = modifies, packages, and sorts proteins and lipids
  • Lysosomes = contain hydrolytic enzymes for digestion and autophagy
  • Cell fractionation: homogenise in cold isotonic buffer → filter → ultracentrifuge at increasing speeds
  • Magnification = image size / real size; Resolution = ability to distinguish two points
  • TEM: high resolution, internal structure; SEM: 3D surface detail
  • Viruses: acellular, genetic material + capsid, obligate intracellular parasites

💡 Exam Tips

  • Always compare structure with function — examiners award marks for explaining why a structure suits its role
  • Remember the size difference: prokaryotes 1–10 μm, eukaryotes 10–100 μm
  • Know the order of ultracentrifugation: low speed = nuclei; medium = mitochondria/lysosomes; high = ribosomes
  • In magnification calculations, convert to the same units before dividing
  • Distinguish TEM (internal, 2D, high resolution) from SEM (surface, 3D, lower resolution)
  • For organelle questions, mention the specific features: cristae in mitochondria, ribosomes on RER, nuclear pores in nucleus
  • Viruses are NOT living — be prepared to justify this using cell theory principles
