Global Resource Consumption

IB DP Geography HL/SL — Option C / Core

Overview

Global Resource Consumption examines patterns of resource use, unequal access, geopolitical tensions, and pathways to sustainability. It covers water, food, and energy resources and is examined across all papers. Case studies of resource conflicts and sustainable management are particularly important.

3.1 Patterns of Resource Consumption

  • Ecological footprint: measure of human demand on nature. High-income countries have much larger per capita footprints.
  • Biocapacity: Earth's ability to regenerate resources and absorb waste.
  • Humanity currently uses resources equivalent to ~1.7 Earths — unsustainable.

3.2 Water Resources

  • Physical scarcity: insufficient water to meet demand (e.g., Middle East, North Africa).
  • Economic scarcity: water exists but infrastructure is lacking (e.g., sub-Saharan Africa).
  • Water stress: annual supply below 1700 m³ per person.

Water Management

  • Large dams (Three Gorges, Aswan) — benefits (hydropower, irrigation) and costs (displacement, ecosystem damage).
  • Desalination — energy-intensive, expensive, brine disposal issues.
  • Water transfers — e.g., China's South-North Water Transfer Project.

3.3 Food Resources

  • Food security: physical, social, and economic access to sufficient, safe, nutritious food.
  • Threats: climate change, conflict, land degradation, water scarcity, population growth.
  • Green Revolution: increased yields through HYV seeds, irrigation, fertilisers — but environmental costs.

Food Production Systems

  • Intensive vs. extensive farming.
  • Organic vs. conventional.
  • Local vs. global food systems.
  • Food miles and carbon footprint.

3.4 Energy Resources

  • Fossil fuels: coal, oil, natural gas — high energy density but carbon emissions.
  • Renewables: solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, biomass.
  • Nuclear: low carbon but waste and safety concerns.

Energy Security

  • Uninterrupted availability of energy at affordable prices.
  • Geopolitical implications: oil shocks (1973, 1979), Russia-Ukraine gas disputes.

3.5 Geopolitics of Resources

  • Resource nationalism: states control access to resources (e.g., OPEC).
  • Resource curse: countries rich in natural resources often have poor development outcomes.
  • Conflicts: water disputes (Nile, Indus), oil conflicts (Nigeria, Middle East).

3.6 Sustainability

  • Sustainable development: meeting present needs without compromising future generations (Brundtland Report, 1987).
  • Circular economy: reduce, reuse, recycle.
  • Local and international initiatives: SDGs, fair trade, organic certification.

Common Misconceptions

  • Food security vs. food self-sufficiency: A country can be food secure through imports; self-sufficiency is not required.
  • Renewable = sustainable: Renewables have environmental impacts too (e.g., hydroelectric dams displace communities).
  • Resource curse: Not inevitable — Norway managed oil wealth effectively through sovereign wealth funds.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: Short-answer questions on resource data, ecological footprints, and water stress.
  • Paper 2: Essays on resource conflicts, sustainability, or food security.
  • IA: Resource consumption surveys, waste audits, or water quality testing are possible.

Practice Questions

  • Explain why water scarcity is as much an economic issue as a physical one.
  • Discuss the social, economic, and environmental impacts of the Green Revolution.
  • Evaluate the extent to which renewable energy can replace fossil fuels.
  • Analyse the geopolitical implications of oil dependence for one country or region.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Ecological footprint > biocapacity → unsustainable.
  • Water: physical vs. economic scarcity; dams, desalination, transfers.
  • Food: security depends on access, availability, utilisation, stability.
  • Energy: fossil fuels vs. renewables vs. nuclear; energy security concerns.
  • Resource curse: wealth can hinder development without good governance.
  • Sustainability: circular economy, SDGs, fair trade.

Exam Tips

  • Use named case studies with specific data.
  • Distinguish between different types of scarcity and security.
  • For evaluation, weigh economic, social, and environmental factors.
  • Include maps or diagrams where appropriate.
  • Consider different scales: local, national, global.
