IGCSE A2 Level

Nuclear Physics

9702 (A-Level) · 0625 (IGCSE)

Detailed Notes

1. Atomic Structure

The atom consists of a small, dense nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons in orbitals. The nucleus contains over 99.9% of the atom's mass but occupies only a tiny fraction of its volume.

  • Proton number (Z): Number of protons in the nucleus. Determines the element.
  • Nucleon number (A): Total number of protons + neutrons.
  • Neutron number (N): N = A − Z.
  • Isotopes: Atoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A).

📖 Key Definition

Isotope: Nuclei of the same element (same proton number Z) with different nucleon numbers A / different numbers of neutrons.

2. Radioactive Decay

Unstable nuclei undergo radioactive decay, emitting radiation to become more stable. There are three main types:

TypeSymbolNatureChargePenetrationIonisation
Alpha (α)₂⁴HeHelium nucleus (2p + 2n)+2eStopped by paperVery high
Beta (β)₋₁⁰eFast electron-1eStopped by Al foilModerate
Gamma (γ)γHigh-energy EM photon0Stopped by thick leadLow
Alpha particles have the highest ionising power because of their large mass and double positive charge, but the lowest penetration because they interact strongly with matter. Gamma rays have the lowest ionisation but highest penetration.

3. Decay Equations

In radioactive decay, both nucleon number and proton number are conserved.

Alpha decay: ᴬ_Z X → ᴬ⁻⁴_Z₋₂ Y + ₂⁴He
Beta decay: ᴬ_Z X → ᴬ_Z₊₁ Y + ₋₁⁰e + ν̄_e (antineutrino)
Gamma decay: ᴬ_Z X* → ᴬ_Z X + γ (nucleus loses energy only)
In beta decay, a neutron in the nucleus converts to a proton, emitting an electron (beta particle) and an antineutrino. This explains why the proton number increases by 1 while the nucleon number stays the same.

4. Half-Life

The half-life t½ of a radioactive isotope is the mean time taken for half of the active nuclei in a sample to decay. It is also the time for the activity to halve.

N = N₀(½)^(t/t½)
A = A₀(½)^(t/t½)

where N = number of nuclei remaining, A = activity (decays per second), and subscript 0 indicates initial values.

📝 Worked Example — Half-Life

Question: A radioactive sample has an initial activity of 800 Bq. The half-life is 6.0 hours. Calculate the activity after: (a) 12 hours, (b) 30 hours.

Solution:

(a) 12 hours = 2 half-lives. A = 800 × (½)² = 800/4 = 200 Bq

(b) 30 hours = 5 half-lives. A = 800 × (½)⁵ = 800/32 = 25 Bq

5. Mass-Energy Equivalence

Einstein's famous equation relates mass and energy:

E = mc²

In nuclear reactions, the total mass of the products may differ from the total mass of the reactants. This mass difference (mass defect Δm) is converted to energy:

ΔE = Δm c²

1 atomic mass unit (u) = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg ≡ 931.5 MeV

In both fission and fusion, the products have less mass than the reactants. The "missing" mass is released as kinetic energy of the products and gamma radiation. This is because the binding energy per nucleon is higher for the products.

6. Nuclear Fission and Fusion

  • Nuclear fission: A heavy nucleus (e.g., uranium-235) splits into two smaller nuclei plus neutrons, releasing energy. Triggered by neutron absorption. Used in nuclear power reactors and atomic bombs.
  • Nuclear fusion: Two light nuclei (e.g., hydrogen isotopes) combine to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy. Requires extremely high temperatures (~10⁷ K) to overcome electrostatic repulsion. Powers the Sun and stars; experimental in tokamaks.

📝 Worked Example — Mass-Energy

Question: In a fission reaction, a uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron and splits. The total mass of the products is 0.200 u less than the total mass of the reactants. Calculate the energy released.

Solution:

ΔE = Δm c² = 0.200 × 931.5 = 186 MeV

⚠️ Common Misconception

Students often think that energy is released in nuclear reactions because mass is "converted into energy." It is more accurate to say that the total rest mass of the system decreases and the total energy (including kinetic energy) is conserved. Mass and energy are equivalent — the decrease in rest mass appears as kinetic energy of the products.

🎯 Exam-Style Question

Question: A radioactive isotope has a half-life of 8.0 days. A sample initially contains 4.0 × 10¹⁵ atoms. Calculate: (a) the number of atoms remaining after 24 days, (b) the activity of the sample initially if the decay constant is 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ s⁻¹. [4 marks]

Mark Scheme

• (a) 24 days = 3 half-lives. N = 4.0 × 10¹⁵ × (½)³ = 4.0 × 10¹⁵/8 = 5.0 × 10¹⁴ (2 marks — 1 for method, 1 for answer)

• (b) A = λN = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 4.0 × 10¹⁵ = 4.0 × 10⁹ Bq (2 marks — 1 for method, 1 for answer)

🎯 Exam-Style Question

Question: Complete the nuclear equation for the beta decay of carbon-14: ¹⁴₆C → ? + ? + ? [2 marks]

Mark Scheme

• ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ₋₁⁰e + ν̄_e (2 marks — 1 for correct daughter nucleus, 1 for beta and antineutrino)

Accept without antineutrino for 1 mark total if daughter is correct.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Proton number Z = protons; Nucleon number A = protons + neutrons
  • α = ₂⁴He (stopped by paper, high ionisation); β = ₋₁⁰e (stopped by Al, moderate); γ = EM (stopped by lead, low ionisation)
  • Alpha decay: Z→Z-2, A→A-4; Beta decay: Z→Z+1, A same
  • Half-life: N = N₀(½)^(t/t½); Activity A = λN
  • E = mc²; 1 u = 931.5 MeV
  • Fission: heavy → light + energy + neutrons
  • Fusion: light → heavy + energy (requires high T)

Exam Tips

  • Always balance both Z and A in nuclear equations.
  • Remember that gamma emission does not change Z or A — it only reduces the nucleus's energy.
  • In half-life calculations, check whether t/t½ is a whole number. If not, use N = N₀e^(-λt).
  • For mass-energy calculations, convert u to MeV using 1 u = 931.5 MeV — much faster than using E = mc² directly.
  • In fission, mention that neutrons are released which can trigger further fissions (chain reaction).
  • Fusion requires high temperature to overcome electrostatic repulsion between positively charged nuclei.
