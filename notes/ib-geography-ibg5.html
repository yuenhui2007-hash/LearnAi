Extreme Environments

IB DP Geography HL/SL — Option E / Core

Overview

Extreme Environments examines hot arid and cold environments, the opportunities and challenges they present, and strategies for sustainable management. This topic is examined in Paper 1 and Paper 2, and is a popular choice for IA fieldwork in desert or glacial landscapes.

5.1 Hot Arid Environments

Characteristics

  • High temperatures, low rainfall (< 250 mm/year), high evaporation rates.
  • Diurnal temperature range is large (hot days, cold nights).
  • Vegetation is sparse and adapted (xerophytes, succulents).
  • Soils are thin, saline, and low in organic matter.

Landforms

  • Erosional: yardangs, deflation hollows, zeugen.
  • Depositional: sand dunes (barchan, seif, star), alluvial fans, playas.

Opportunities

  • Tourism (desert safaris, eco-tourism).
  • Mineral extraction (oil, uranium, phosphates).
  • Solar energy potential.
  • Irrigated agriculture (oasis farming).

Challenges

  • Water scarcity and salinisation.
  • Desertification due to overgrazing and deforestation.
  • Extreme temperatures limiting human activity.

5.2 Cold Environments

Characteristics

  • Polar and tundra climates; permafrost (permanently frozen ground).
  • Low precipitation, cold temperatures, short growing seasons.
  • Fragile ecosystems with slow recovery rates.

Landforms

  • Glacial erosion: cirques, arêtes, U-shaped valleys, roche moutonnée.
  • Glacial deposition: moraines, drumlins, eskers.
  • Periglacial: patterned ground, pingos, solifluction.

Opportunities

  • Tourism (Antarctica, Alpine skiing, Northern Lights).
  • Fishing (North Atlantic, Southern Ocean).
  • Mineral and hydrocarbon extraction (Arctic oil, Alaska).

Challenges

  • Permafrost thawing due to climate change — infrastructure damage, methane release.
  • Inaccessibility and high operating costs.
  • Environmental sensitivity — slow recovery from damage.

5.3 Sustainability in Extreme Environments

  • Desert: drip irrigation, solar desalination, controlled tourism, anti-desertification programmes (Great Green Wall).
  • Cold: protected areas (Antarctic Treaty), sustainable fishing quotas, limits on mineral extraction, permafrost monitoring.

Common Misconceptions

  • Deserts: Not all deserts are hot (e.g., Antarctica is a desert by rainfall). Not all are sandy (rocky and gravel deserts are common).
  • Glaciers: Glaciers move by internal deformation and basal sliding, not by "flowing" like liquids.
  • Permafrost: Permafrost does not mean no liquid water — active layers thaw seasonally.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: Short-answer questions on landform identification, climate data, and human activities.
  • Paper 2: Essays on sustainability, opportunities/challenges, or climate change impacts.
  • IA: Fieldwork in arid or glacial environments (e.g., dune morphology, glacier measurement).

Practice Questions

  • Explain the formation of two glacial erosional landforms.
  • Discuss the opportunities and challenges of economic development in hot arid environments.
  • Evaluate the effectiveness of international agreements in protecting cold environments.
  • Analyse the causes of desertification and evaluate strategies to combat it.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Hot arid: low rainfall, high evaporation, xerophytes, sand dunes, alluvial fans.
  • Opportunities: tourism, minerals, solar, oasis agriculture.
  • Challenges: water scarcity, desertification, salinisation.
  • Cold: permafrost, glaciers, fragile ecosystems, slow recovery.
  • Glacial landforms: cirques, arêtes, U-shaped valleys, moraines, drumlins.
  • Sustainability: controlled access, protected areas, renewable energy, anti-desertification.

Exam Tips

  • Use named examples for both hot arid and cold environments.
  • Draw diagrams of landform formation processes.
  • For evaluation, consider environmental, economic, and social sustainability.
  • Link to climate change where relevant (permafrost thaw, desertification).
