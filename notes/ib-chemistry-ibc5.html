Energetics & Thermochemistry

IB DP Chemistry HL/SL — Topic 5

Overview

Energetics and Thermochemistry (Topic 5) deals with the energy changes in chemical reactions. It is essential for predicting reaction feasibility and is tested across all papers. Calorimetry experiments are popular IA topics (Criterion A–D).

5.1 Enthalpy Changes

Enthalpy change (ΔH): heat energy change at constant pressure.

  • Exothermic: ΔH < 0 — heat released to surroundings (products lower in energy than reactants).
  • Endothermic: ΔH > 0 — heat absorbed from surroundings (products higher in energy than reactants).

Types of Enthalpy Change

  • Standard enthalpy of formation (ΔH°f): enthalpy change when 1 mol of compound forms from its elements in standard states.
  • Standard enthalpy of combustion (ΔH°c): enthalpy change when 1 mol of substance burns completely in oxygen.
  • Standard enthalpy of neutralisation: enthalpy change when 1 mol of water is formed from acid–base neutralisation.
  • Standard enthalpy of atomisation: enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous atoms forms from the element in its standard state.

5.2 Calorimetry

q = mcΔT
q = heat energy (J), m = mass of solution (g), c = specific heat capacity (4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ for water), ΔT = temperature change (K or °C)

For reactions in solution, assume the solution behaves like water.

Worked Example 1: Calorimetry

Question: 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl is mixed with 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. The temperature rises from 20.0°C to 26.5°C. Calculate ΔH for neutralisation.

Solution:

Total volume = 100.0 cm³ → mass ≈ 100.0 g
q = (100.0)(4.18)(6.5) = 2717 J
n(H₂O) = n(HCl) = (1.00)(0.0500) = 0.0500 mol
ΔH = −q/n = −2717/0.0500 = −54 340 J mol⁻¹ = −54.3 kJ mol⁻¹

5.3 Hess's Law

The enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the pathway taken.

ΔH_reaction = ΣΔH°f(products) − ΣΔH°f(reactants)
ΔH_reaction = ΣΔH°c(reactants) − ΣΔH°c(products)

Worked Example 2: Hess's Law

Question: Calculate ΔH for C(s) + ½O₂(g) → CO(g) given:
C + O₂ → CO₂, ΔH = −394 kJ mol⁻¹
CO + ½O₂ → CO₂, ΔH = −283 kJ mol⁻¹

Solution:

Reverse second equation: CO₂ → CO + ½O₂, ΔH = +283 kJ mol⁻¹
Add to first: C + O₂ + CO₂ → CO₂ + CO + ½O₂
Simplifies to: C + ½O₂ → CO, ΔH = −394 + 283 = −111 kJ mol⁻¹

5.4 Bond Enthalpies

Average energy required to break 1 mol of a bond in the gaseous state.

ΔH = Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds broken) − Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds formed)
Bonds broken = endothermic (+); bonds formed = exothermic (−)

Bond enthalpies are average values and less accurate than Hess's law calculations using formation/combustion data.

5.5 Entropy & Gibbs Free Energy [HL only]

Entropy (S): measure of disorder or number of ways energy can be distributed.

ΔS° = ΣS°(products) − ΣS°(reactants)
Gibbs free energy: ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°
If ΔG° < 0: reaction is spontaneous
If ΔG° > 0: reaction is non-spontaneous

Temperature Dependence

  • ΔH < 0, ΔS > 0: spontaneous at all temperatures.
  • ΔH > 0, ΔS < 0: non-spontaneous at all temperatures.
  • ΔH < 0, ΔS < 0: spontaneous at low T.
  • ΔH > 0, ΔS > 0: spontaneous at high T.

HL Exam Focus

May 2024 HL Paper 2: Calculate ΔG° and predict spontaneity at different temperatures; explain the decomposition of calcium carbonate.

Common Misconceptions

  • Sign of ΔH: Exothermic = negative ΔH. Students often confuse the sign with temperature change.
  • Hess's law: Remember the direction of arrows — reverse a reaction, reverse the sign of ΔH.
  • Bond enthalpies: These are average values for gaseous molecules only — they cannot be used for solids or liquids directly.
  • Entropy: A negative ΔS° does not mean a reaction cannot occur — check ΔG° at the given temperature.

Practice Questions

  • Calculate the enthalpy change when 2.0 g of ethanol (C₂H₅OH) burns, heating 200 g of water from 18.0°C to 45.0°C.
  • Use Hess's law to find ΔH for the reaction: 2C(s) + H₂(g) → C₂H₂(g) given appropriate combustion data.
  • Estimate ΔH for the hydrogenation of ethene using bond enthalpies.
  • HL: For a reaction with ΔH° = +120 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS° = +150 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, calculate the temperature above which the reaction becomes spontaneous.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Exothermic: ΔH < 0; Endothermic: ΔH > 0.
  • q = mcΔT for calorimetry; assume solution = water.
  • Hess's law: ΔH = ΣΔHf(products) − ΣΔHf(reactants).
  • Bond enthalpies: ΔH = bonds broken − bonds formed.
  • HL: ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°; spontaneous when ΔG° < 0.

Exam Tips

  • Read each question carefully and identify the command term before answering.
  • Show all working with units — method marks are generous in IB Chemistry.
  • Give final answers to 2–3 significant figures unless specified otherwise.
  • In "explain" questions, refer to bond breaking/forming or energy level diagrams.
  • For calorimetry, always mention assumptions (no heat loss, solution ≈ water).
