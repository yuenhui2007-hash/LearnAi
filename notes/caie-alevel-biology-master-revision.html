Cell Structure

Detailed Notes

1. Cell Theory

  • All living organisms composed of cells.
  • Cell is the basic unit of life.
  • All cells arise from pre-existing cells.

2. Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic

3. Key Organelles

  • Nucleus: Chromatin, nucleolus, nuclear pores.
  • Mitochondria: Aerobic respiration, cristae, own DNA.
  • RER: Protein synthesis with ribosomes.
  • SER: Lipid synthesis, detoxification.
  • Golgi: Modifies, packages proteins.
  • Lysosomes: Hydrolytic enzymes.
  • Chloroplasts: Photosynthesis, thylakoids, grana.

4. Cell Fractionation

  • Homogenise in cold isotonic buffer.
  • Filter through gauze.
  • Ultracentrifuge: nuclei → mitochondria → lysosomes → ribosomes.

5. Cell Division — Mitosis

  • Stages: Prophase → Metaphase → Anaphase → Telophase → Cytokinesis
  • Prophase: Chromosomes condense, nuclear envelope breaks down, spindle forms
  • Metaphase: Chromosomes line up on equator
  • Anaphase: Sister chromatids pulled to opposite poles
  • Result: 2 identical diploid daughter cells
Mitosis produces genetically identical cells for growth, repair, and asexual reproduction. No crossing over or independent assortment occurs.

6. Cell Division — Meiosis

  • Two divisions: Meiosis I (reduction) and Meiosis II (separation)
  • Meiosis I: Homologous chromosomes pair (synapsis), crossing over occurs, then separate
  • Result: 4 genetically different haploid gametes
  • Significance: Genetic variation through crossing over and independent assortment

Biological Molecules

Detailed Notes

1. Carbohydrates

Monosaccharides: Glucose (α and β), fructose, galactose.

Disaccharides: Maltose (glu+glu), sucrose (glu+fructose), lactose (glu+gal).

Polysaccharides:

  • Starch: amylose (helical, 1-4α) + amylopectin (branched, 1-4α and 1-6α). Plant storage.
  • Glycogen: highly branched. Animal storage.
  • Cellulose: β-glucose, 1-4 links, microfibrils. Structural.

2. Lipids

Triglycerides: Glycerol + 3 fatty acids. Saturated (no C=C) vs unsaturated (C=C).

Phospholipids: Amphipathic → bilayer.

Cholesterol: Regulates membrane fluidity.

3. Proteins

  • Primary: Amino acid sequence.
  • Secondary: α-helix or β-pleated sheet.
  • Tertiary: 3D folding.
  • Quaternary: Multiple chains.

4. Tests

  • Benedict's: reducing sugars → brick red.
  • Iodine: starch → blue-black.
  • Biuret: protein → lilac.
  • Emulsion: lipids → cloudy.

5. Enzyme Kinetics — Michaelis-Menten

  • Vmax: Maximum rate when all active sites occupied
  • Km: Substrate concentration at which rate = ½Vmax — measures enzyme affinity
  • Competitive inhibition: Inhibitor competes for active site, increases Km
  • Non-competitive inhibition: Inhibitor binds elsewhere, decreases Vmax

Last-Minute Summary

  • Starch = amylose + amypectin; glycogen = more branched; cellulose = β-glucose microfibrils
  • Triglycerides = glycerol + 3 fatty acids; saturated vs unsaturated
  • Phospholipids: amphipathic → bilayer
  • Protein structure: primary → secondary → tertiary → quaternary
  • Tests: Benedict's (sugar), Iodine (starch), Biuret (protein), Emulsion (lipid)

Enzymes

Detailed Notes

1. Nature

  • Globular protein catalysts.
  • Specific due to active site shape.
  • Lower activation energy.

2. Models

  • Lock-and-key: rigid fit.
  • Induced-fit: flexible active site.

3. Factors

Temperature: Rate increases to optimum (~37°C), then denaturation.

pH: Optimum varies; extreme pH denatures.

[S]: Rate ∝ [S] at low concentrations; Vmax at saturation.

[E]: Rate ∝ [E] when substrate in excess.

4. Inhibition

  • Competitive: Binds active site; overcome by more substrate.
  • Non-competitive: Binds allosteric site; cannot be overcome.

5. Cofactors

  • Cofactors: inorganic ions.
  • Coenzymes: organic (e.g., NAD).

  • Variables: Independent (what you change), dependent (what you measure), controlled (kept constant)
  • Validity: Does the experiment test what it claims to test?
  • Reliability: Repeatable results — repeat experiments, calculate means

  • Electron microscope: TEM (internal structure) vs SEM (surface detail)
  • Staining: Methylene blue (nuclei), iodine (starch), eosin (cytoplasm)
  • Cell fractionation: Homogenisation → filtration → ultracentrifugation

Last-Minute Summary

  • Enzymes = globular protein catalysts
  • Active site: lock-and-key (rigid) or induced-fit (flexible)
  • Temperature: increases to optimum, then denatures
  • pH: each enzyme has optimum
  • Competitive: active site, overcome by [S]
  • Non-competitive: allosteric, not overcome by [S]

Cell Membranes

Detailed Notes

1. Fluid Mosaic

Phospholipid bilayer + intrinsic/extrinsic proteins + cholesterol + glycoproteins/glycolipids.

2. Diffusion

  • Simple: small non-polar (O₂, CO₂).
  • Facilitated: through channels/carriers (glucose, ions).

3. Osmosis

Water from high Ψ to low Ψ. Ψ = Ψs + Ψp. Pure water Ψ = 0.

4. Active Transport

Against gradient. Needs ATP and carrier proteins.

5. Bulk Transport

  • Endocytosis: bulk uptake.
  • Exocytosis: bulk export.

Genetic Engineering

Detailed Notes

1. DNA Structure

Double helix, antiparallel. A-T (2 H-bonds), C-G (3 H-bonds).

2. Replication

Semi-conservative. Helicase unwinds. DNA polymerase adds nucleotides 5'→3'.

3. Transcription

DNA → mRNA. RNA polymerase. Splicing removes introns.

4. Translation

mRNA → protein at ribosome. tRNA anticodons match mRNA codons. Peptide bonds form.

5. Genetic Code

Triplet, degenerate, universal, non-overlapping.

Transport

IGCSE Content

Water Uptake

Water is absorbed from soil by root hair cells. Root hairs increase surface area for absorption. Water moves by osmosis from soil (higher water potential) into root hair cells (lower water potential due to dissolved minerals and sugars).

  • Pathways through root: Symplast (through cytoplasm via plasmodesmata) and apoplast (through cell walls). At Casparian strip (suberin in endodermis), water is forced into symplast for selective uptake.
  • Water enters xylem vessels, creating a continuous water column.

Transpiration

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from leaves, mainly through stomata. It creates a transpiration stream (transpiration pull) that draws water up the xylem.

  • Factors affecting transpiration rate:
    • Light: Stomata open in light; more transpiration.
    • Temperature: Increases kinetic energy and evaporation rate.
    • Humidity: High humidity reduces diffusion gradient; less transpiration.
    • Wind: Removes water vapour from leaf surface, maintaining steep gradient.
    • Soil water: Limited water reduces transpiration (wilting).

Xylem Structure

  • Lignified walls for strength and support.
  • End walls break down to form continuous hollow tubes.
  • No cytoplasm or organelles — dead at maturity.
  • Pits allow lateral water movement.

Phloem Structure

  • Sieve tube elements: Living but lack nucleus and most organelles. End walls form sieve plates with pores.
  • Companion cells: Have nucleus, dense cytoplasm, many mitochondria. Provide ATP and proteins via plasmodesmata to sieve tube elements.

Translocation

Translocation is the transport of assimilates (mainly sucrose) in phloem from sources (where produced, e.g., leaves) to sinks (where used or stored, e.g., roots, fruits, growing points).

  • Requires energy (ATP).
  • Translocation can occur in both directions at different times.

AS Level Content

Cohesion-Tension Theory

The mechanism by which water is pulled up the xylem:

  • Transpiration pull: Evaporation from leaf mesophyll cells creates tension (negative pressure) in the xylem water column.
  • Cohesion: Water molecules are attracted to each other by hydrogen bonds, forming a continuous unbroken column.
  • Adhesion: Water molecules are attracted to hydrophilic walls of xylem vessels, counteracting gravity.
  • Root pressure: Active pumping of mineral ions into xylem lowers water potential, drawing water in by osmosis. Causes guttation (water droplets on leaf edges at night).

Structure of Leaf in Relation to Transport

  • Xerophytes: Plants adapted to dry conditions. Features: sunken stomata, reduced leaf area, thick waxy cuticle, hairs, rolled leaves, CAM photosynthesis (stomata open at night).
  • Hydrophytes: Aquatic plants. Reduced xylem, large air spaces (aerenchyma), stomata on upper surface or absent.

Phloem Loading & Mass Flow

  • Active loading: Sucrose is actively transported into companion cells, then moves into sieve tube elements via plasmodesmata.
  • This raises solute concentration in phloem, lowering water potential. Water enters by osmosis from xylem.
  • High hydrostatic pressure at source drives sap to sinks.
  • At sinks, sucrose is removed (used or converted to starch), water potential rises, water leaves into xylem. Lower pressure at sink maintains pressure gradient.
  • Evidence for mass flow: Aphid stylets exude sap under pressure; ringing experiments show phloem transport blocked; radioactive tracers show movement from source to sink.

A2 Level Content

Detailed Mechanisms of Phloem Loading

  • Symplastic loading: Sucrose diffuses through plasmodesmata from mesophyll cells into companion cells and sieve elements. Occurs in some plants (e.g., cucurbits).
  • Apoplastic loading: Sucrose diffuses into cell walls (apoplast), then is actively transported across companion cell membrane via sucrose-H+ co-transport (proton pump creates H+ gradient). Most common in temperate plants.
  • Some plants use polymer trapping: sucrose is converted to raffinose family oligosaccharides in intermediary cells, which are too large to diffuse back, maintaining a diffusion gradient.

Source-Sink Relationships

Sources and sinks change with developmental stage:

  • Young leaves are sinks; mature leaves are sources.
  • During germination, storage cotyledons/endosperm are sources; embryo is sink.
  • During fruit development, leaves are sources; fruits are sinks.
  • Seasonal changes: in deciduous trees before leaf fall, nutrients are translocated from leaves to stems and roots (storage sinks).

Stomatal Regulation

  • Guard cells control stomatal aperture.
  • When turgid, guard cells bow apart, opening stomata. When flaccid, they close.
  • Mechanism: Light activates proton pumps (H+-ATPases) in guard cell membrane. K+ enters through voltage-gated channels, followed by Cl- and malate2-. Water enters by osmosis; guard cells become turgid.
  • ABA (abscisic acid) triggers closure: inhibits proton pumps, K+ leaves, water follows, cells become flaccid.

Gas Exchange

IGCSE Content

Blood Composition

  • Plasma: Pale yellow liquid. Transports nutrients, hormones, antibodies, waste products (urea, CO2), and heat.
  • Red blood cells (erythrocytes): Biconcave discs; no nucleus; contain haemoglobin. Transport oxygen (oxyhaemoglobin in lungs, releases oxygen in tissues). Adaptations: large surface area, flexible, contain carbonic anhydrase.
  • White blood cells (leucocytes): Nucleated; larger than red cells. Phagocytes engulf pathogens. Lymphocytes produce antibodies.
  • Platelets (thrombocytes): Cell fragments; no nucleus. Involved in blood clotting.

Heart Structure

  • Four chambers: right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium, left ventricle.
  • Right side pumps deoxygenated blood to lungs (pulmonary circuit). Left side pumps oxygenated blood to body (systemic circuit).
  • Valves: Tricuspid (right AV), bicuspid/mitral (left AV), semilunar (pulmonary and aortic). Prevent backflow.
  • Septum separates oxygenated and deoxygenated blood.
  • Left ventricle wall thicker than right — pumps blood further and at higher pressure.

Double Circulation

  • Pulmonary circulation: Heart → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → heart.
  • Systemic circulation: Heart → aorta → body → vena cava → heart.
  • Double circulation maintains higher pressure in systemic circuit and separates oxygenated/deoxygenated blood.

Blood Vessels

  • Arteries: Thick muscular and elastic walls; narrow lumen; carry blood away from heart at high pressure. Pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood.
  • Veins: Thin walls with less muscle and elastic tissue; wide lumen; valves prevent backflow; carry blood to heart at low pressure. Pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood.
  • Capillaries: Walls one cell thick (endothelium); narrow lumen; slow blood flow allows efficient exchange. Form extensive networks (capillary beds).

AS Level Content

Haemoglobin

  • Quaternary protein with 4 polypeptide chains (2α, 2β), each with a haem group containing Fe2+.
  • Each haemoglobin can bind up to 4 O2 molecules.
  • Oxygen dissociation curve is sigmoid (S-shaped) due to cooperative binding: binding of first O2 increases affinity of other subunits.
  • Bohr effect: In tissues, high CO2 (low pH) shifts curve right → lower affinity, more O2 released. In lungs, low CO2 (high pH) shifts curve left → higher affinity, more O2 picked up.
  • Fetal haemoglobin: Higher affinity for O2 than adult haemoglobin, allowing transfer across placenta.

Cardiac Cycle

  • Diastole: All chambers relax. Blood flows into atria from veins, then through open AV valves into ventricles. Semilunar valves closed.
  • Atrial systole: Atria contract, pushing remaining blood into ventricles.
  • Ventricular systole: Ventricles contract; pressure rises, closing AV valves (lub sound). Blood forced through semilunar valves into arteries. Semilunar valves open.
  • Relaxation: Ventricles relax; pressure falls below arteries, semilunar valves close (dub sound). Cycle repeats ~70 times/min at rest.

Control of Heart Rate

  • Sinoatrial node (SAN): In right atrium wall. Natural pacemaker; initiates wave of electrical excitation.
  • Excitation spreads across atria → atrial contraction.
  • Atrioventricular node (AVN) delays impulse (~0.1s) to allow complete atrial emptying.
  • Impulse travels down Bundle of His, through Purkyne tissue, up ventricle walls from apex → coordinated ventricular contraction.
  • Medulla oblongata: Cardiovascular centre. Sympathetic nerves (accelerator) increase rate via SAN. Parasympathetic (vagus) decrease rate.
  • Baroreceptors (aorta, carotid sinus) detect blood pressure. Chemoreceptors detect CO2/pH changes during exercise.

Tissue Fluid & Lymph

  • At arteriole end of capillary: hydrostatic pressure > osmotic pressure → fluid forced out, forming tissue fluid.
  • Tissue fluid bathes cells, allowing exchange of nutrients, gases, and wastes.
  • At venule end: hydrostatic pressure < osmotic pressure (due to plasma proteins) → fluid returns to capillary.
  • Excess tissue fluid drains into lymphatic vessels → lymph → returned to blood via subclavian veins.
  • Lymph nodes filter lymph and contain lymphocytes for immune defence.

A2 Level Content

Transport of Carbon Dioxide

  • ~5% dissolved in plasma.
  • ~10% bound to haemoglobin (carbaminohaemoglobin).
  • ~85% as hydrogencarbonate ions (HCO3-): CO2 + H2O → H2CO3 → H+ + HCO3-, catalysed by carbonic anhydrase in red blood cells. H+ binds haemoglobin (HbH+); HCO3- diffuses out via chloride shift (Cl- enters to maintain charge balance).

Atherosclerosis

  • Endothelium damaged by high blood pressure, smoking, or high LDL cholesterol.
  • Inflammatory response; LDLs accumulate, phagocytes engulf them forming foam cells.
  • Fibrous cap forms over fatty deposit (atheroma/plaque).
  • Plaque narrows lumen, reducing blood flow and causing thrombosis (blood clot).
  • Coronary thrombosis → myocardial infarction (heart attack). Cerebral thrombosis → stroke.
  • Risk factors: smoking, high blood pressure, high blood cholesterol, diabetes, obesity, lack of exercise, genetic predisposition, age, gender.

Transport at the Placenta

  • Fetal and maternal blood do not mix.
  • Villi provide large surface area; thin barrier (endothelium, connective tissue, epithelium) allows diffusion.
  • Oxygen, glucose, amino acids, antibodies (IgG) diffuse from mother to fetus.
  • CO2, urea, other wastes diffuse from fetus to mother.
  • Fetal haemoglobin has higher affinity for O2 than maternal haemoglobin, ensuring efficient transfer.

Infectious Disease

IGCSE Content

Gas Exchange Surfaces

All gas exchange surfaces share features: large surface area, thin barrier (one cell thick), good blood supply, moist surface.

  • Humans: Alveoli in lungs. Millions of alveoli provide huge surface area (~70 m2). Walls one cell thick; surrounded by capillaries.
  • Fish: Gills. Water flows over filaments; countercurrent flow maintains steep concentration gradient along entire length.
  • Insects: Tracheal system. Spiracles open into tracheae → tracheoles. Air moves directly to cells. No blood transport needed for gases.
  • Plants: Stomata on leaves. Guard cells control opening. Gases diffuse through air spaces in spongy mesophyll.

Human Breathing Mechanism

  • Inspiration: External intercostal muscles contract, ribs move up and out. Diaphragm contracts and flattens. Thoracic volume increases, pressure decreases below atmospheric. Air rushes in.
  • Expiration: External intercostals relax, ribs move down and in. Diaphragm relaxes and domes up. Thoracic volume decreases, pressure increases above atmospheric. Air forced out.

Respiration

Respiration is the release of energy from glucose (or other organic molecules). It occurs in all living cells.

  • Aerobic respiration: Requires oxygen. Glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy.
    C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + ATP
  • Anaerobic respiration in yeast: Glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide + little energy (fermentation).
  • Anaerobic respiration in muscles: Glucose → lactic acid + little energy. Lactic acid causes fatigue and oxygen debt.

AS Level Content

Aerobic Respiration — Glycolysis

  • Occurs in cytoplasm. Does not require oxygen.
  • Glucose (6C) phosphorylated using 2 ATP → glucose-6-phosphate → fructose-1,6-bisphosphate.
  • Split into two 3-carbon molecules (triose phosphate / GALP).
  • Each triose phosphate oxidised, producing 2 ATP (substrate-level phosphorylation) and 2 NADH.
  • Net gain: 2 ATP, 2 NADH, 2 pyruvate per glucose.

Link Reaction

  • In mitochondrial matrix.
  • Pyruvate (3C) is decarboxylated (CO2 removed) and dehydrogenated (NAD accepts H), forming acetyl group.
  • Acetyl group combines with coenzyme A → acetyl CoA (2C).
  • 2 pyruvate → 2 acetyl CoA + 2 CO2 + 2 NADH.

Krebs Cycle (Citric Acid Cycle)

  • In mitochondrial matrix.
  • Acetyl CoA (2C) combines with oxaloacetate (4C) → citrate (6C).
  • Series of reactions: decarboxylation (2 CO2 released), dehydrogenation (3 NADH and 1 FADH2), substrate-level phosphorylation (1 ATP or GTP).
  • Per glucose: 2 turns → 4 CO2, 6 NADH, 2 FADH2, 2 ATP.

Oxidative Phosphorylation

  • On inner mitochondrial membrane (cristae).
  • NADH and FADH2 donate electrons to electron transport chain (ETC).
  • Electrons pass through carriers (NADH dehydrogenase, cytochrome complexes) releasing energy.
  • Energy pumps protons (H+) from matrix into intermembrane space, creating electrochemical gradient.
  • Protons flow back through ATP synthase (chemiosmosis), driving ATP synthesis.
  • Oxygen is final electron acceptor, combining with electrons and H+ to form water.
  • Per glucose: ~28-34 ATP from oxidative phosphorylation.

Anaerobic Respiration

  • In mammals: Pyruvate reduced by NADH → lactate, catalysed by lactate dehydrogenase. NAD+ regenerated so glycolysis can continue. Lactate transported to liver for Cori cycle (converted back to glucose).
  • In yeast: Pyruvate decarboxylated → ethanal; ethanal reduced by NADH → ethanol. CO2 released. Used in brewing and baking.

A2 Level Content

Respiratory Substrates

  • Carbohydrates: Respiratory quotient (RQ) = CO2 produced / O2 consumed = 1.0 for glucose.
  • Lipids: More reduced; more H per C. RQ ≈ 0.7. Yield more ATP per gram but require more O2.
  • Proteins: Deaminated first (amino group removed as urea). RQ ≈ 0.9. Used when carbohydrate and lipid stores depleted.

Control of Respiration

  • Substrate-level phosphorylation: Direct transfer of phosphate from substrate to ADP (glycolysis, Krebs).
  • Oxidative phosphorylation: ATP synthesis driven by proton gradient (ETC and chemiosmosis).
  • Rate of respiration: Controlled by availability of ADP and substrate; allosteric regulation of phosphofructokinase (inhibited by ATP and citrate, activated by AMP).

Mitochondrial Structure & Function

  • Outer membrane: permeable to small molecules.
  • Inner membrane: impermeable; contains ETC and ATP synthase. Folded into cristae to increase surface area.
  • Matrix: contains enzymes for link reaction and Krebs cycle, mitochondrial DNA, 70S ribosomes.
  • Intermembrane space: site of proton accumulation.

Photosynthesis vs Respiration

  • Photosynthesis: energy capture (endergonic); occurs in chloroplasts; produces glucose and O2.
  • Respiration: energy release (exergonic); occurs in cytoplasm and mitochondria; uses glucose and O2, produces CO2 and H2O.
  • They are not exact opposites: different pathways, enzymes, and cellular locations.

Immunity

IGCSE Content

Nervous System

  • Central nervous system (CNS): Brain and spinal cord. Processes information.
  • Peripheral nervous system (PNS): Nerves connecting CNS to body. Sensory (afferent) and motor (efferent) neurones.
  • Neurone structure: Cell body, dendrites (receive impulses), axon (transmits impulses), myelin sheath (insulation, speeds conduction), Schwann cells (produce myelin), nodes of Ranvier.

Reflex Arc

  • A rapid, automatic response to a stimulus without conscious thought.
  • Pathway: receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone (in CNS) → motor neurone → effector (muscle or gland).
  • Synapses ensure one-way transmission and allow integration.

Synapse

  • Junction between two neurones.
  • Electrical impulse arrives → Ca2+ channels open → vesicles fuse with presynaptic membrane → neurotransmitter (e.g., acetylcholine) released by exocytosis.
  • Neurotransmitter diffuses across synaptic cleft → binds to receptors on postsynaptic membrane → depolarisation → new impulse.
  • Neurotransmitter is then broken down (by acetylcholinesterase) or reabsorbed.

Hormones

  • Chemical messengers transported in blood from endocrine glands to target organs.
  • Slower but longer-lasting than nerve impulses.
  • Insulin: Lowers blood glucose (increases uptake by cells, increases glycogen storage in liver).
  • Glucagon: Raises blood glucose (breaks down glycogen to glucose in liver).
  • ADH: Increases water reabsorption in kidney collecting ducts.

Plant Responses

  • Phototropism: Growth towards light. Auxin accumulates on shaded side → cell elongation → bending towards light.
  • Geotropism (gravitropism): Roots grow downwards (positive), shoots upwards (negative).

AS Level Content

Resting Potential & Action Potential

  • Resting potential: -70 mV. Maintained by Na+/K+ pump (3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in) and K+ leak channels.
  • Action potential:
    • Depolarisation: Stimulus opens voltage-gated Na+ channels; Na+ rushes in; potential rises to +40 mV.
    • Repolarisation: Na+ channels close; K+ channels open; K+ leaves; potential falls.
    • Hyperpolarisation: K+ channels slow to close; potential overshoots resting level.
    • Return to resting: Na+/K+ pump restores ion distribution.
  • All-or-nothing principle: Action potential either fires fully or not at all.
  • Refractory period: Brief period after action potential when neurone cannot fire again, ensuring one-way transmission and limiting frequency.

Saltatory Conduction

  • Myelin sheath is an electrical insulator.
  • Action potential "jumps" between nodes of Ranvier (where voltage-gated channels are concentrated).
  • Faster and more energy-efficient than continuous conduction.

Neurotransmitters & Synapses

  • Excitatory synapses: Neurotransmitter (e.g., acetylcholine, glutamate) opens Na+ channels → depolarisation.
  • Inhibitory synapses: Neurotransmitter (e.g., GABA, glycine) opens Cl- channels or K+ channels → hyperpolarisation, making action potential less likely.
  • Summation: Spatial (multiple inputs simultaneously) and temporal (rapid repeated inputs from one source) summation can trigger action potential.

The Eye & Photoreception

  • Retina: Contains rods (dim light, black/white, high sensitivity, low acuity) and cones (bright light, colour, low sensitivity, high acuity).
  • Fovea: Only cones; highest visual acuity.
  • Blind spot: Optic disc; no photoreceptors.
  • Pupil reflex: Bright light → circular muscles contract, radial relax → pupil constricts. Dim light → radial contract, circular relax → pupil dilates.
  • Accommodation: Ciliary muscles contract → suspensory ligaments slacken → lens thickens (near vision). Ciliary muscles relax → ligaments taut → lens flattens (distant vision).

A2 Level Content

Muscle Contraction

  • Sarcomere structure: Z lines, I band (thin actin only), A band (thick myosin), H zone (myosin only), M line.
  • Sliding filament theory:
    • Action potential arrives at T-tubule → sarcoplasmic reticulum releases Ca2+.
    • Ca2+ binds troponin → tropomyosin moves → reveals myosin-binding sites on actin.
    • Myosin heads bind actin (cross-bridges), swivel using ATP, pull actin filaments toward M line.
    • ATP detaches myosin; re-energised by ATP hydrolysis; cycle repeats if Ca2+ present.
    • Sarcomere shortens; I band and H zone narrow; A band stays same width.
  • Muscle fatigue: accumulation of lactate, depletion of glycogen/ATP.

The Brain

  • Cerebral hemispheres: Conscious thought, language, memory, personality. Motor and sensory areas (contralateral control).
  • Cerebellum: Coordination, balance, posture.
  • Medulla oblongata: Cardiovascular and respiratory centres; reflexes (sneezing, coughing, vomiting).
  • Hypothalamus: Thermoregulation, osmoregulation, endocrine control (posterior pituitary), biological rhythms.
  • Pituitary gland: Master endocrine gland; anterior produces hormones (e.g., FSH, LH, growth hormone); posterior stores/releases ADH and oxytocin.

Plant Hormones in Detail

  • Auxins (IAA): Synthesised in shoot tip. Cell elongation by loosening cell walls (acid growth hypothesis: H+-ATPases acidify cell wall, activating expansins). Transported by polar transport (PIN proteins).
  • Gibberellins: Stem elongation, seed germination (breaks dormancy by stimulating amylase production in barley), fruit development.
  • Abscisic acid (ABA): Stress hormone. Stomatal closure, seed dormancy, inhibits growth.
  • Ethene (ethylene): Fruit ripening, leaf abscission.
  • Cytokinins: Cell division, delay leaf senescence, antagonise auxin in apical dominance.

IGCSE Content

Homeostasis is introduced at IGCSE as the maintenance of a constant internal environment. Key concepts include negative feedback and the control of blood glucose.

  • Negative feedback: A change is detected, response reverses the change, maintaining set point. Most homeostatic mechanisms use negative feedback.
  • Blood glucose: Insulin lowers blood glucose; glucagon raises it. Both secreted by pancreatic islets (β cells and α cells).
  • Skin in temperature regulation: Vasodilation (more blood to skin, more heat loss), vasoconstriction (less blood to skin, less heat loss), sweating (evaporative cooling), shivering (muscle contraction generates heat), erector pili muscles (hair stands up to trap insulating air layer).

AS Level Content

At AS, homeostasis is covered in the context of excretion, osmoregulation, and the kidney:

  • Osmoregulation: Controlled by ADH from posterior pituitary. More ADH → more water reabsorption in collecting ducts → concentrated urine. Less ADH → dilute urine.
  • Nephron structure: Bowman's capsule (filtration), proximal convoluted tubule (reabsorption of glucose, amino acids, salts, water), loop of Henle (water and salt reabsorption, creates medullary gradient), distal convoluted tubule (selective reabsorption/secretion), collecting duct (water reabsorption under ADH control).
  • Ultrafiltration: High hydrostatic pressure in glomerulus forces small molecules (water, glucose, amino acids, urea, salts) through basement membrane into Bowman's capsule. Blood cells and large proteins retained.
  • Selective reabsorption: Glucose and amino acids reabsorbed by active transport in proximal convoluted tubule. Water follows by osmosis.

A2 Level Content

Thermoregulation

Temperature regulation involves the hypothalamus (thermoregulatory centre) with temperature receptors in the skin and hypothalamus itself.

  • Heat gain mechanisms (cold stress):
    • Vasoconstriction of arterioles in skin — less blood flow, less radiation.
    • Shivering — skeletal muscle contractions generate heat.
    • Raising hairs (piloerection) — traps insulating air (more effective in furry mammals).
    • Behavioural: seeking shelter, putting on clothes, huddling, curling up.
    • Increased metabolic rate — thyroxine and adrenaline increase respiration.
  • Heat loss mechanisms (heat stress):
    • Vasodilation — more blood to skin capillaries, increased radiation/convection/conduction.
    • Sweating — evaporation from skin surface removes latent heat.
    • Behavioural: seeking shade, removing clothes, fanning, reduced activity.
    • Panting — evaporative cooling from respiratory surfaces.
  • Hypothalamus: Receives input from peripheral thermoreceptors (skin) and central receptors (hypothalamic blood). Activates autonomic and endocrine responses and behavioural modifications.

Blood Glucose Regulation (A2 Detail)

  • β cells in pancreatic islets detect high blood glucose → secrete insulin.
  • Insulin binds receptors on liver and muscle cells → increases glucose uptake, increases glycogen synthesis (glycogenesis), increases glucose use in respiration, decreases gluconeogenesis.
  • α cells detect low blood glucose → secrete glucagon.
  • Glucagon binds liver cells → glycogen breakdown (glycogenolysis), gluconeogenesis (from amino acids/glycerol), decreased glucose use.
  • Second messenger system: Insulin binds receptor → activates tyrosine kinase → triggers cascade → vesicles with GLUT4 transporters move to membrane → glucose uptake.
  • Diabetes mellitus:
    • Type 1: autoimmune destruction of β cells; no insulin production; treated with insulin injections.
    • Type 2: insulin resistance (receptors reduced/sensitive); often linked to obesity and lifestyle; treated with diet, exercise, metformin (increases insulin sensitivity).

Excretion & the Kidney in A2 Detail

  • Loop of Henle: Counter-current multiplier. Descending limb permeable to water (leaves by osmosis into medulla). Ascending limb impermeable to water but actively transports NaCl out (thick ascending limb). Creates high solute concentration in medulla.
  • Collecting duct: Passes through medulla. Under ADH, aquaporins inserted into membrane → water reabsorbed → concentrated urine. Without ADH, little water reabsorbed → dilute urine.
  • Urea recycling: Urea diffuses out of collecting duct into medulla, contributing to osmotic gradient. Some re-enters loop of Henle — helps maintain gradient.
  • Control of ADH secretion: Osmoreceptors in hypothalamus detect blood osmolarity. High osmolarity → ADH released from posterior pituitary. Low osmolarity → ADH inhibited.

Feedback Mechanisms

  • Negative feedback: Response opposes the original change. Maintains stability. Examples: temperature, blood glucose, osmoregulation.
  • Positive feedback: Response amplifies the original change. Rare in homeostasis but occurs in blood clotting and childbirth (oxytocin stimulates uterine contractions → more oxytocin).

Key Terms

  • Gene: A length of DNA that codes for a protein.
  • Allele: Alternative form of a gene.
  • Homozygous: Two identical alleles (TT or tt).
  • Heterozygous: Two different alleles (Tt).
  • Dominant: Allele expressed in heterozygote (T).
  • Recessive: Allele only expressed in homozygote (t).
  • Genotype: Genetic makeup (alleles present).
  • Phenotype: Observable characteristic.

Monohybrid Crosses

Cross involving one gene. Use Punnett squares to predict offspring ratios.

  • Homozygous dominant × homozygous recessive → all heterozygous (F1).
  • F1 self-cross → 3:1 phenotypic ratio (dominant:recessive).
  • F1 cross back to recessive parent (test cross) → 1:1 ratio.

Co-dominance & Incomplete Dominance

  • Co-dominance: Both alleles expressed in heterozygote. Example: ABO blood group (IA and IB are co-dominant; both expressed as AB).
  • Incomplete dominance: Heterozygote shows intermediate phenotype. Example: snapdragon flower colour (red × white → pink).

Sex Determination

  • Humans: XX = female, XY = male.
  • Male gametes determine sex (X or Y sperm).
  • Sex-linked genes are carried on sex chromosomes (especially X).

Genetic Diagrams

Always show: parental phenotypes, parental genotypes, gametes, offspring genotypes, offspring phenotypes, and ratios. Use clear genetic symbols.

AS Level Content

Dihybrid Crosses

Cross involving two genes. Assuming independent assortment, phenotypic ratio in F2 = 9:3:3:1.

  • Only applies if genes are on different chromosomes (or far apart on same chromosome).
  • Test cross of dihybrid (heterozygous for both) × double recessive gives 1:1:1:1 ratio.

Chi-Squared Test

χ² = Σ [(O - E)² / E]
Degrees of freedom = number of classes - 1
  • Used to compare observed results with expected ratios.
  • Calculate χ² value, compare to critical value at p=0.05.
  • If χ² < critical value → no significant difference; accept null hypothesis (results fit expected ratio).
  • If χ² > critical value → significant difference; reject null hypothesis.

Linkage

  • Genes on same chromosome tend to be inherited together.
  • Recombination frequency indicates map distance (1% recombination = 1 map unit/centimorgan).
  • Crossing over during prophase I separates linked genes; closer genes have lower recombination frequency.

Epistasis

  • Interaction between different genes where one masks the expression of another.
  • Dominant epistasis: A masks B/b. Example: summer squash colour (A_ = white regardless of B).
  • Recessive epistasis: aa masks B/b. Example: coat colour in Labradors (bb = brown; ee = yellow regardless of B).
  • Ratios modified from 9:3:3:1 (e.g., 12:3:1 or 9:3:4).

Multiple Alleles & ABO Blood Group

  • Three alleles: IA, IB, IO. IA and IB co-dominant; both dominant to IO.
  • Phenotypes: A (IAIA or IAIO), B (IBIB or IBIO), AB (IAIB), O (IOIO).

A2 Level Content

Continuous & Discontinuous Variation

  • Discontinuous: Distinct categories; controlled by one/few genes; little environmental influence. Example: blood group, attached/unattached earlobes.
  • Continuous: Range of values; controlled by many genes (polygenic) + environment. Example: height, skin colour, yield in crops.
  • Polygenic inheritance shows normal distribution. Environmental factors shift the distribution.

Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

p + q = 1
p² + 2pq + q² = 1
p = frequency of dominant allele
q = frequency of recessive allele
p² = homozygous dominant frequency
2pq = heterozygous frequency
q² = homozygous recessive frequency
  • Applies to large, randomly mating populations with no mutation, selection, migration, or genetic drift.
  • Useful for calculating carrier frequency (2pq) for recessive genetic diseases.
  • If assumptions are violated, allele frequencies change → evolution.

Genetic Diseases

  • Autosomal dominant: Huntington's disease. Affected individual has affected parent. Homozygous dominant often lethal.
  • Autosomal recessive: Cystic fibrosis, sickle cell anaemia, albinism. Carriers (heterozygotes) unaffected. Both parents must be carriers for affected offspring (25% risk).
  • X-linked recessive: Haemophilia, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, red-green colour blindness. Males more affected (XY). Females carriers (XHXh).
  • X-linked dominant: Rare. Affected father passes to all daughters, none to sons.

Gene Technology & Screening

  • Genetic counselling: Predicting risks using family pedigrees.
  • Pre-natal screening: Amniocentesis (amniotic fluid, 15-20 weeks), chorionic villus sampling (placenta, 10-12 weeks), non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT, fetal DNA in maternal blood).
  • Embryo screening (PGD): IVF embryos tested for genetic disorders before implantation.
  • Ethical considerations: privacy, discrimination, abortion, designer babies.

Energy & Ecosystems

IGCSE Content

Variation

  • Genetic variation: Differences in alleles due to mutation, meiosis (crossing over, independent assortment), and random fertilisation.
  • Environmental variation: Differences caused by environment (diet, climate, lifestyle).
  • Most characteristics are influenced by both genes and environment.

Natural Selection

Mechanism proposed by Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace:

  • Within a population there is variation.
  • There is competition for limited resources (struggle for existence).
  • Individuals with advantageous characteristics are more likely to survive and reproduce.
  • These advantageous alleles become more common in the population over generations.

Evolution

Evolution is the change in allele frequencies in a population over time. Natural selection is the main mechanism driving adaptive evolution.

Artificial Selection

  • Humans select individuals with desired characteristics to breed.
  • Over many generations, breeds/cultivars with exaggerated traits are produced.
  • Examples: dairy cows (high milk yield), wheat (disease resistance, high yield), dogs (various breeds).
  • Reduces genetic diversity compared to natural populations.

Antibiotic Resistance

  • Bacteria show natural variation in resistance to antibiotics.
  • Antibiotic use creates selection pressure.
  • Resistant bacteria survive and reproduce, passing resistance genes (often on plasmids).
  • Overuse and misuse of antibiotics accelerate resistance.

AS Level Content

Speciation

Formation of new species from existing ones:

  • Species definition: Group of organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
  • Isolation mechanisms: Prevent gene flow between populations.
    • Geographic isolation: Physical barrier (river, mountain, ocean) separates populations.
    • Reproductive isolation: Pre-zygotic (temporal, behavioural, mechanical, gamete incompatibility) or post-zygotic (hybrid inviability, hybrid sterility).
  • Allopatric speciation: Speciation due to geographic isolation. Different selection pressures and genetic drift cause divergence.
  • Sympatric speciation: Speciation without geographic separation (e.g., polyploidy in plants).

Evidence for Evolution

  • Fossil record: Shows gradual changes and transitional forms (e.g., Archaeopteryx, Tiktaalik).
  • Anatomy: Homologous structures (same origin, different function — vertebrate limbs) show common ancestry. Analogous structures (different origin, same function — wings of birds and insects) show convergent evolution.
  • Embryology: Early embryos of vertebrates show similar structures (pharyngeal pouches, tail).
  • Biochemistry: Similar proteins and DNA sequences indicate relatedness. Cytochrome c and haemoglobin compared across species.
  • Biogeography: Distribution of species reflects continental drift and isolation.

Industrial Melanism

  • Peppered moth (Biston betularia): light form camouflaged on lichen-covered trees; dark form (melanic) rare.
  • Industrial pollution killed lichen and darkened tree bark. Birds preyed more on light moths.
  • Melanic form increased in frequency due to natural selection.
  • After clean air laws, lichen returned, light form increased again.
  • Direct evidence of selection (though some controversy about exact predation experiments).

A2 Level Content

Sources of Variation

  • Mutation: Ultimate source of new alleles. Point mutations, chromosomal mutations (deletion, duplication, inversion, translocation), polyploidy.
  • Meiosis: Crossing over (prophase I) and independent assortment (metaphase I) reshuffle alleles.
  • Random fertilisation: Any gamete can fuse with any other, creating unique combinations.
  • Genetic drift: Random changes in allele frequencies, especially in small populations. Founder effect and bottleneck effect.

Types of Selection

  • Stabilising selection: Favours intermediate phenotypes. Reduces variation. Example: human birth weight (very small and very large babies have higher mortality).
  • Directional selection: Favours one extreme. Shifts mean phenotype. Example: antibiotic resistance, industrial melanism, Darwin's finch beak size during drought.
  • Disruptive (diversifying) selection: Favours both extremes. Can lead to speciation. Example: different beak sizes in finches when both large and small seeds are abundant.
  • Balancing selection: Maintains multiple alleles in population. Example: heterozygote advantage — sickle cell trait (HbA/HbS) confers malaria resistance.

Molecular Evidence for Evolution

  • DNA hybridisation: Measure how much DNA from different species binds together. More binding = more similar = more closely related.
  • DNA sequencing: Compare base sequences directly. Fewer differences = more recent common ancestor.
  • Protein sequencing: Compare amino acid sequences of proteins like cytochrome c or haemoglobin.
  • Molecular clocks: Assume mutation rate is constant; calculate time since divergence from number of differences.

Social & Ethical Issues

  • Genetic modification: Inserting genes from one organism into another. Benefits (increased yield, pest resistance, medicines like insulin) vs risks (gene flow to wild relatives, reduction in biodiversity, unknown long-term effects).
  • Conservation: Maintaining genetic diversity is crucial for species' ability to adapt to changing environments.

Key Points

  • Speciation requires isolation (geographic or reproductive) + divergence.

  • Types of selection: stabilising (intermediate favoured), directional (one extreme favoured), disruptive (both extremes favoured), balancing (multiple alleles maintained).

  • Artificial selection: humans choose parents; reduces genetic diversity.
  • Antibiotic resistance: selection pressure from antibiotic use favours resistant bacteria.
  • Industrial melanism: classic example of directional selection.

Nervous Coordination

IGCSE Content

Levels of Organisation

  • Species: Group of organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
  • Population: All organisms of one species in a defined area at a given time.
  • Community: All populations of different species living in the same area.
  • Ecosystem: Community + abiotic (non-living) environment.
  • Habitat: Place where an organism lives.
  • Niche: Role of an organism in its ecosystem (interactions, resources used, when and where it lives).

Energy Flow

  • Energy enters ecosystems via photosynthesis (light energy → chemical energy in glucose).
  • Energy transferred through food chains and food webs.
  • At each trophic level, energy is lost as heat (respiration), in undigested material (faeces), and through excretion.
  • Typically only ~10% of energy is transferred between trophic levels.
  • Food chains rarely have more than 5 trophic levels due to energy loss.

Nutrient Cycles

  • Carbon cycle: Photosynthesis removes CO2; respiration, combustion, and decomposition return it.
  • Nitrogen cycle: Nitrogen fixation (bacteria in legume root nodules or soil) converts N2 to ammonia/nitrates. Nitrification (Nitrosomonas → nitrite; Nitrobacter → nitrate). Denitrification returns N2 to atmosphere. Decomposition releases ammonia.

Population Growth

  • Exponential growth: When resources are unlimited. J-shaped curve.
  • Logistic growth: As resources become limited, growth slows and stabilises at carrying capacity (K). S-shaped curve.
  • Limiting factors: Food, water, space, light, predators, disease.

Human Impact

  • Deforestation, pollution, overfishing, agriculture, global warming.
  • Conservation methods: protected areas, breeding programmes, sustainable harvesting, education, legislation.

AS Level Content

Ecological Sampling

  • Quadrats: Used to estimate plant population density or percentage cover. Random sampling using random coordinates or random number table avoids bias.
  • Transects: Line or belt transects show distribution of species along an environmental gradient (e.g., from shore to land).
  • Mark-release-recapture: Estimate mobile animal populations.
    N = (n1 × n2) / m
    N = population estimate
    n1 = number caught and marked first time
    n2 = number caught second time
    m = number of marked individuals recaptured
  • Assumptions: marked individuals mix randomly; marking does not affect survival; no immigration/emigration/births/deaths between samples.

Productivity & Energy Transfer

  • Gross primary productivity (GPP): Total energy fixed by photosynthesis.
  • Net primary productivity (NPP): Energy available to herbivores. NPP = GPP - R (respiration by plants).
  • Secondary productivity: Energy available to next trophic level = energy ingested - energy lost in faeces - respiration.
  • Efficiency of energy transfer = (energy at trophic level n+1 / energy at trophic level n) × 100%.

Ecological Pyramids

  • Pyramid of numbers: Number of individuals at each trophic level. Can be inverted if producers are large (e.g., tree supporting many insects).
  • Pyramid of biomass: Dry mass at each trophic level. Usually upright. Can be inverted if producers reproduce rapidly (e.g., phytoplankton in open ocean).
  • Pyramid of energy: Energy at each trophic level. Always upright (second law of thermodynamics).

Decomposition

  • Detritivores (earthworms, woodlice) ingest dead organic matter, increasing surface area for microbial action.
  • Saprobiotic microorganisms (bacteria and fungi) secrete extracellular enzymes to digest dead matter extracellularly, absorbing soluble products.
  • Conditions affecting decomposition: temperature, moisture, oxygen availability, pH.

A2 Level Content

Ecological Succession

  • Primary succession: Colonisation of bare rock or newly formed land (e.g., after volcanic eruption). Pioneer species (lichens, mosses) break down rock to form soil. Gradual increase in complexity and biodiversity. Climax community is stable, self-perpetuating.
  • Secondary succession: Re-colonisation after disturbance of existing soil (e.g., after forest fire, abandoned farmland). Faster than primary succession.
  • Deflected succession: Human activity (grazing, mowing) prevents reaching climax, maintaining plagioclimax community (e.g., grassland, heathland).

Population Ecology

  • Density-dependent factors: Effect increases with population density (competition, disease, predation, accumulation of waste).
  • Density-independent factors: Affect population regardless of density (natural disasters, climate extremes, fires).
  • r-selected species: Many small offspring, little parental care, rapid maturation, unpredictable environments. Example: insects, weeds.
  • K-selected species: Few large offspring, extensive parental care, slow maturation, stable environments. Example: elephants, humans, whales.

Conservation & Biodiversity

  • Biodiversity: Genetic diversity, species diversity, ecosystem diversity.
  • Reasons for conservation: Ethical, aesthetic, economic (medicines, agriculture, tourism), ecological (maintaining nutrient cycles, food webs, gene pools).
  • In-situ conservation: In natural habitat (national parks, nature reserves, wildlife corridors).
  • Ex-situ conservation: Outside natural habitat (zoos, botanic gardens, seed banks, captive breeding).
  • Sustainable management: Maximum sustainable yield, quotas, restrictions, habitat restoration.

Global Warming & Climate Change

  • Greenhouse gases (CO2, methane, N2O, water vapour) trap infrared radiation.
  • Human activities (fossil fuel combustion, deforestation, agriculture, livestock) increase greenhouse gas concentrations.
  • Effects: rising sea levels, altered rainfall patterns, increased extreme weather, shifts in species ranges, coral bleaching, phenological changes.
  • Carbon footprint reduction: renewable energy, afforestation, energy efficiency, reduced meat consumption, sustainable transport.

Homeostasis

IGCSE Content

At IGCSE, immunity is introduced as the body's defence against pathogens:

  • Pathogens: Disease-causing organisms (bacteria, viruses, fungi, protists).
  • Physical barriers: Skin, mucous membranes, cilia, stomach acid, tears (lysozyme).
  • Phagocytosis: White blood cells (phagocytes) engulf and digest pathogens.
  • Antibodies: Produced by lymphocytes; specific to particular antigens on pathogens.
  • Memory cells: Remain after infection; enable rapid response on re-exposure.
  • Active immunity: Body makes own antibodies (natural infection or vaccination).
  • Passive immunity: Receive ready-made antibodies (maternal antibodies across placenta, in breast milk, or antivenom).

AS Level Content

At AS, immunity is covered in the context of disease and health:

  • Antigens: Molecules (usually proteins or glycoproteins) on cell surfaces that trigger immune response. Self antigens = body's own; non-self = foreign.
  • Phagocytes: Neutrophils (short-lived, abundant, engulf bacteria) and macrophages (in tissues, also antigen-presenting cells).
  • Antibody structure: Y-shaped; two heavy chains, two light chains. Variable regions bind specific antigen. Constant region determines class (IgG, IgM, IgA, etc.).
  • Primary and secondary response: Primary = slower, smaller peak; secondary = faster, larger, longer-lasting due to memory B and T cells.
  • Vaccination: Stimulates primary response without disease. Uses attenuated pathogens, killed pathogens, toxoids, or subunit vaccines. Herd immunity protects unvaccinated individuals.

A2 Level Content

Innate Immunity

  • Non-specific; present from birth; no memory.
  • Physical barriers: Skin (keratin, antimicrobial peptides), mucous membranes, ciliated epithelium (mucociliary escalator).
  • Chemical barriers: Lysozyme in tears/saliva, stomach acid, sebum, interferons (proteins that inhibit viral replication in neighbouring cells).
  • Cellular defences: Phagocytosis by neutrophils and macrophages; natural killer (NK) cells destroy virus-infected and tumour cells.
  • Inflammation: Damaged cells release histamine → vasodilation and increased capillary permeability → redness, heat, swelling. Complement proteins attract phagocytes and form membrane attack complexes (MAC) to lyse pathogens.

Adaptive (Acquired) Immunity

  • Specific; involves recognition of antigens; has memory.
  • Cell-mediated immunity (T cells):
    • T helper cells (CD4+): Activated by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) displaying antigen on MHC II. Release cytokines to activate B cells, cytotoxic T cells, and macrophages.
    • Cytotoxic T cells (CD8+): Recognise antigen presented on MHC I of infected body cells. Release perforin (forms pores) and granzymes (induce apoptosis).
    • Memory T cells: Long-lived; rapid response on re-exposure.
  • Humoral immunity (B cells):
    • B cells have surface antibodies (B cell receptors) that bind specific antigen.
    • Activated by antigen binding + T helper cell cytokines (T-dependent) or directly by repeating antigens (T-independent).
    • Clonal expansion: rapid division producing plasma cells and memory B cells.
    • Plasma cells secrete large amounts of antibody.
    • Memory B cells: long-lived; faster, stronger secondary response.

Antibody Structure & Function

  • Four polypeptide chains: 2 heavy, 2 light. Disulfide bonds hold together.
  • Variable region at tips of Y: antigen-binding site. Unique for each B cell clone.
  • Constant region: determines class and effector function.
  • Functions: Neutralisation (block pathogen binding), opsonisation (mark for phagocytosis), agglutination (clump pathogens), complement activation (lysis).

Antigen Presentation

  • Macrophages and dendritic cells (APCs) engulf pathogens, process antigens, and display them on MHC molecules.
  • MHC I: On all nucleated cells; displays endogenous antigens (from inside cell, e.g., viral proteins) to cytotoxic T cells.
  • MHC II: On APCs; displays exogenous antigens (from outside cell) to helper T cells.

Autoimmune Diseases

  • Immune system attacks body's own cells.
  • Type 1 diabetes: T cells destroy pancreatic β cells.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis: Antibodies attack joint tissues.
  • Multiple sclerosis: Immune attack on myelin sheath.
  • Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE): Antibodies against nuclear antigens.

Allergy (Hypersensitivity)

  • Type I hypersensitivity: IgE antibodies bind mast cells and basophils. On re-exposure to allergen, cross-linking triggers histamine release → inflammation, vasodilation, bronchoconstriction, mucus secretion.
  • Can lead to anaphylaxis — systemic, life-threatening reaction treated with adrenaline (epinephrine).

Monoclonal Antibodies

  • Produced by hybridoma cells: B cells fused with myeloma (cancer) cells → immortal antibody-producing cell line.
  • Uses: diagnostic tests (pregnancy tests, pathogen detection), cancer treatment (targeted delivery of drugs/radiation to tumour cells), transplant rejection prevention, autoimmune disease treatment.
  • Advantages: highly specific, can be produced in large quantities, consistent quality.

Vaccines

  • Live attenuated: Weakened pathogen. Strong immune response; risk to immunocompromised. Examples: MMR, BCG.
  • Killed/inactivated: Dead pathogen. Safer; may need boosters. Examples: polio (Salk), hepatitis A.
  • Subunit/recombinant: Purified antigen or genetically engineered protein. Very safe. Examples: hepatitis B, HPV, acellular pertussis.
  • mRNA vaccines: mRNA codes for viral antigen; host cells produce antigen, stimulating immune response. Examples: COVID-19 vaccines (Pfizer, Moderna).
  • Herd immunity: When sufficient proportion of population is immune, disease cannot spread effectively, protecting vulnerable unvaccinated individuals.
