Transport in Animals

9700 (A-Level) · 0610 (IGCSE)

IGCSE Content

1

Blood Composition

  • Plasma: Pale yellow liquid (about 55% of blood volume). Transports nutrients (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids), hormones, antibodies, waste products (urea, CO₂), and heat. Contains plasma proteins (albumin, fibrinogen, globulins).
  • Red blood cells (erythrocytes): Biconcave discs; no nucleus; contain haemoglobin. Transport oxygen (oxyhaemoglobin in lungs, releases oxygen in tissues). Adaptations: large surface area to volume ratio, flexible (can squeeze through capillaries), contain carbonic anhydrase for CO₂ transport.
  • White blood cells (leucocytes): Nucleated; larger than red cells. Phagocytes (neutrophils, macrophages) engulf pathogens. Lymphocytes (B cells, T cells) produce antibodies and coordinate immune responses.
  • Platelets (thrombocytes): Cell fragments; no nucleus. Involved in blood clotting by releasing clotting factors and forming a plug at injury sites.
Biconcave shape increases surface area for oxygen diffusion and allows red blood cells to fold and squeeze through narrow capillaries. The absence of a nucleus provides more space for haemoglobin.
2

Heart Structure

  • Four chambers: right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium, left ventricle.
  • Right side pumps deoxygenated blood to lungs (pulmonary circuit). Left side pumps oxygenated blood to body (systemic circuit).
  • Valves: Tricuspid (right AV), bicuspid/mitral (left AV), semilunar (pulmonary and aortic). Prevent backflow.
  • Septum separates oxygenated and deoxygenated blood.
  • Left ventricle wall thicker than right — pumps blood further and at higher pressure (systemic circulation).
📝 Annotated Diagram Description: Heart Cross-Section

When drawing the heart:

  • Label all four chambers: right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium, left ventricle.
  • Label the septum dividing left and right sides.
  • Label all valves and the direction of blood flow.
  • Label major blood vessels: vena cava, pulmonary artery, pulmonary vein, aorta.
  • Show that the left ventricle wall is thicker than the right.
3

Double Circulation

  • Pulmonary circulation: Heart → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → heart. Deoxygenated blood becomes oxygenated.
  • Systemic circulation: Heart → aorta → body → vena cava → heart. Oxygenated blood delivers O₂ and nutrients; picks up CO₂ and wastes.
  • Double circulation maintains higher pressure in systemic circuit and separates oxygenated/deoxygenated blood, maximising oxygen delivery efficiency.

The walls of arteries contain more elastic tissue and smooth muscle to withstand and maintain high pressure. Veins have valves to prevent backflow because blood pressure is too low to ensure one-way flow. Capillaries have walls one cell thick to minimise diffusion distance for efficient exchange.

AS Level Content

5

Haemoglobin

  • Quaternary protein with 4 polypeptide chains (2α, 2β), each with a haem group containing Fe²⁺.
  • Each haemoglobin can bind up to 4 O₂ molecules (one per haem group).
  • Oxygen dissociation curve is sigmoid (S-shaped) due to cooperative binding: binding of first O₂ increases affinity of other subunits (positive cooperativity).
  • Bohr effect: In tissues, high CO₂ (low pH) shifts curve right → lower affinity, more O₂ released. In lungs, low CO₂ (high pH) shifts curve left → higher affinity, more O₂ picked up.
  • Fetal haemoglobin (HbF): Has a higher affinity for O₂ than adult haemoglobin (HbA), allowing transfer across placenta from mother to fetus.
📝 Annotated Diagram Description: Oxygen Dissociation Curve

When drawing the oxygen dissociation curve:

  • X-axis: partial pressure of oxygen (pO₂) in kPa.
  • Y-axis: percentage saturation of haemoglobin with oxygen.
  • Draw a sigmoid (S-shaped) curve starting near origin and plateauing at ~100%.
  • Label the steeper middle section where cooperative binding occurs.
  • Show a second curve to the left for fetal haemoglobin (higher affinity).
  • Show a curve shifted right for tissues with high CO₂ (Bohr effect).
Worked Example: Bohr Effect

Question: Explain why the oxygen dissociation curve shifts to the right in active muscle tissue. [3 marks]

Answer:

  • Active muscle produces more CO₂ from aerobic respiration (1).
  • CO₂ reacts with water to form carbonic acid, lowering pH (1).
  • Lower pH reduces haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen, causing it to release more O₂ to the muscle (Bohr shift) (1).
6

Cardiac Cycle

Stages of the Cardiac Cycle
  1. Diastole: All chambers relax. Blood flows into atria from veins, then through open AV valves into ventricles. Semilunar valves closed. Ventricles fill to about 70% of capacity passively.
  2. Atrial systole: Atria contract, pushing remaining blood into ventricles (the final 30%). AV valves open.
  3. Ventricular systole: Ventricles contract; pressure rises, closing AV valves ("lub" sound). Blood forced through semilunar valves into arteries. Semilunar valves open.
  4. Relaxation: Ventricles relax; pressure falls below arteries, semilunar valves close ("dub" sound). Cycle repeats ~70 times/min at rest.
The "lub" sound (first heart sound) is caused by closure of the AV valves at the start of ventricular systole. The "dub" sound (second heart sound) is caused by closure of the semilunar valves at the start of ventricular diastole.
7

Control of Heart Rate

  • Sinoatrial node (SAN): In right atrium wall. Natural pacemaker; initiates wave of electrical excitation.
  • Excitation spreads across atria → atrial contraction.
  • Atrioventricular node (AVN) delays impulse (~0.1s) to allow complete atrial emptying.
  • Impulse travels down Bundle of His, through Purkyne tissue, up ventricle walls from apex → coordinated ventricular contraction.
  • Medulla oblongata: Cardiovascular centre. Sympathetic nerves (accelerator) increase rate via SAN. Parasympathetic (vagus) decrease rate.
  • Baroreceptors (aorta, carotid sinus) detect blood pressure. Chemoreceptors detect CO₂/pH changes during exercise.
Worked Example: Response to Exercise

Question: Explain how heart rate is increased during exercise. [4 marks]

Answer:

  • Exercise increases CO₂ production and decreases blood pH (1).
  • Chemoreceptors in aorta and carotid arteries detect these changes and send signals to the medulla oblongata (1).
  • The cardiovascular centre in the medulla sends impulses via sympathetic nerves to the SAN (1).
  • The SAN increases the rate of electrical impulses, increasing heart rate and cardiac output (1).
8

Tissue Fluid & Lymph

  • At arteriole end of capillary: hydrostatic pressure > osmotic pressure → fluid forced out, forming tissue fluid.
  • Tissue fluid bathes cells, allowing exchange of nutrients, gases, and wastes.
  • At venule end: hydrostatic pressure < osmotic pressure (due to plasma proteins, mainly albumin) → fluid returns to capillary.
  • Excess tissue fluid drains into lymphatic vessels → lymph → returned to blood via subclavian veins.
  • Lymph nodes filter lymph and contain lymphocytes for immune defence.
Tissue fluid formation: Hydrostatic pressure forces fluid OUT at arteriole end.
Tissue fluid return: Osmotic pressure draws fluid BACK IN at venule end.
Lymph = excess tissue fluid that does not return to capillaries.

A2 Level Content

9

Transport of Carbon Dioxide

  • ~5% dissolved in plasma.
  • ~10% bound to haemoglobin (carbaminohaemoglobin).
  • ~85% as hydrogencarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻): CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻, catalysed by carbonic anhydrase in red blood cells. H⁺ binds haemoglobin (HbH⁺); HCO₃⁻ diffuses out via chloride shift (Cl⁻ enters to maintain charge balance).
The chloride shift maintains electrical neutrality as HCO₃⁻ leaves the red blood cell. Chloride ions (Cl⁻) move into the cell, which is why venous blood has a higher chloride concentration than arterial blood (chloride shift/Hamburger effect).
10

Atherosclerosis

Development of Atherosclerosis
  1. Endothelial damage: High blood pressure, smoking, or high LDL cholesterol damages the endothelium.
  2. Inflammatory response: LDLs accumulate in the artery wall; phagocytes (macrophages) engulf them, forming foam cells.
  3. Fibrous cap formation: Smooth muscle cells produce collagen, forming a fibrous cap over the fatty deposit (atheroma/plaque).
  4. Plaque growth: The plaque narrows the lumen, reducing blood flow.
  5. Thrombosis: Plaque rupture exposes underlying tissue, triggering blood clot formation. A clot blocking a coronary artery causes myocardial infarction (heart attack). A clot in a cerebral artery causes stroke.
Risk factors for atherosclerosis: smoking, high blood pressure, high blood cholesterol (especially LDL), diabetes, obesity, lack of exercise, genetic predisposition, age, and male gender.
🔬 Real-World Application: Treating Cardiovascular Disease

Statins reduce LDL cholesterol synthesis in the liver. Aspirin reduces platelet aggregation, lowering thrombosis risk. Angioplasty uses a balloon to widen narrowed arteries, often with stent insertion. Coronary bypass surgery grafts vessels to bypass blocked coronary arteries. Lifestyle changes (diet, exercise, smoking cessation) are fundamental.

11

Transport at the Placenta

  • Fetal and maternal blood do not mix.
  • Villi provide large surface area; thin barrier (endothelium, connective tissue, epithelium) allows diffusion.
  • Oxygen, glucose, amino acids, antibodies (IgG) diffuse from mother to fetus.
  • CO₂, urea, other wastes diffuse from fetus to mother.
  • Fetal haemoglobin has higher affinity for O₂ than maternal haemoglobin, ensuring efficient transfer.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Blood: plasma (transport), RBCs (O₂ via haemoglobin), WBCs (phagocytes, lymphocytes), platelets (clotting).
  • Heart: 4 chambers; left ventricle thickest; valves prevent backflow; septum separates circuits.
  • Double circulation: pulmonary (to lungs) and systemic (to body).
  • Arteries (thick, muscular, high pressure) → arterioles → capillaries (exchange) → venules → veins (thin, valves, low pressure).
  • Haemoglobin: 4 subunits, 4 O₂; sigmoid curve; cooperative binding; Bohr effect (right shift in tissues).
  • Cardiac cycle: diastole → atrial systole → ventricular systole → relaxation. Lub = AV valves close; Dub = semilunar valves close.
  • Heart conduction: SAN → AVN → Bundle of His → Purkyne fibres. Medulla controls via sympathetic/parasympathetic nerves.
  • Tissue fluid: filtered out at arteriole end, returns at venule end; excess = lymph.
  • CO₂ transport: mostly as HCO₃⁻; carbonic anhydrase; chloride shift.
  • Atherosclerosis: damage → LDL accumulation → plaque → thrombosis → MI/stroke.

💡 Exam Tips

  • Draw the oxygen dissociation curve with correct axes (pO₂ on x-axis, % saturation on y-axis).
  • Label fetal haemoglobin curve to the left of adult. Label Bohr shift (right) for tissues.
  • In cardiac cycle questions, always mention pressure changes causing valve opening/closing.
  • For atherosclerosis, describe events in sequence: endothelial damage → LDL accumulation → foam cells → fibrous cap → plaque → thrombosis.
  • Remember the chloride shift: HCO₃⁻ out, Cl⁻ in, maintaining charge balance.
  • Tissue fluid: high hydrostatic pressure at arteriole end pushes fluid out; high osmotic pressure at venule end draws it back.
