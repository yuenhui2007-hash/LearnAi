📘 Subject Context

Stalin's Russia, 1924–1953 is a significant area of study in IGCSE History. Understanding the causes, events, and consequences of historical developments is essential for constructing analytical essays and source-based answers.

IGCSE History examines knowledge, source analysis, and essay-writing skills. Paper 1 tests depth studies through structured questions; Paper 2 tests breadth studies through source analysis and essays. Always use specific evidence.

Stalin's Russia, 1924–1953

Cambridge 9389 A-Level / 0470 IGCSE

📘 IGCSE Content

The Power Struggle (1924–29)

  • Lenin died (21 Jan 1924). No clear successor. Testament (Dec 1922–Jan 1923) criticised all leading Bolsheviks; recommended removing Stalin as General Secretary. Not published fully.
  • Contenders: Trotsky (brilliant orator, Red Army founder, but arrogant, Jewish, late Bolshevik), Zinoviev and Kamenev (Leningrad/Moscow), Bukharin (right, pro-NEP), Stalin (General Secretary, built patronage).
  • Stalin's strategy: formed alliances to eliminate rivals. With Zinoviev and Kamenev against Trotsky (1924–25); then with Bukharin against Zinoviev and Kamenev (1926–27); then destroyed Bukharin (1928–29).
  • Trotsky expelled from party (1927); exiled (1929); assassinated in Mexico (1940).
  • By 1929 Stalin was undisputed leader.

Collectivisation (1929–34)

  • Goal: consolidate peasant land into collective farms (kolkhozy); state-controlled grain collection; finance industrialisation.
  • Kulaks (wealthier peasants) targeted as "class enemies." Dekulakisation: 1.8 million sent to labour camps or executed; property confiscated.
  • Forced collectivisation met violent resistance. Peasants slaughtered livestock rather than surrender. Grain production fell.
  • Famine (1932–33): Ukraine (Holodomor), Kazakhstan, North Caucasus. 5–7 million dead. Grain exported during famine.
  • Results: by 1937, 93% of peasant households collectivised. Agriculture never fully recovered; inefficiency persisted.

The Five-Year Plans (1928–41)

  • First Five-Year Plan (1928–32): priority to heavy industry (steel, coal, electricity). Magnitogorsk, Dnieprostroi dam. Targets not met but impressive growth.
  • Second Five-Year Plan (1933–37): consumer goods slightly more emphasis. Stakhanovite movement (Alexei Stakhanov, coal miner, 1935).
  • Third Five-Year Plan (1938–41): rearmament priority due to threat of war.
  • Results: USSR became second industrial power. But quality poor; consumer goods neglected; urban housing crisis; rationing.

The Great Terror (1936–38)

  • Show trials: Old Bolsheviks confessed to absurd crimes. Zinoviev and Kamenev (1936); Radek and Pyatakov (1937); Bukharin and Rykov (1938).
  • Army purges: Marshal Tukhachevsky and 8 senior commanders executed (1937). 35,000 officers arrested. Weakened Red Army before WWII.
  • Yezhovshchina (named after NKVD head Yezhov): 700,000–1.2 million executed 1937–38; millions sent to Gulag.
  • Yezhov himself arrested and shot (1940); replaced by Beria.

World War II and Aftermath

  • Nazi-Soviet Pact (Aug 1939): bought time; gained Baltic states and eastern Poland.
  • Operation Barbarossa (22 June 1941): Germany invaded. Initial disasters; 3 million Soviet POWs by end of 1941.
  • Scorched earth; factories moved east of Urals. Leningrad siege (1941–44): 1 million dead. Stalingrad (Aug 1942–Feb 1943): turning point. Kursk (July 1943): largest tank battle.
  • Soviet victory: Red Army reached Berlin (April 1945). 27 million Soviet dead (approx.).
  • Post-war: Stalin reimposed control. Purges in Eastern Europe (show trials: Rajk 1949, Slánský 1952). Leningrad Affair (1949–50). Doctors' Plot (1952–53) — anti-Semitic campaign cut short by Stalin's death.
  • Stalin died (5 March 1953).

📗 AS Level Content

Why Did Stalin Win the Power Struggle?

  • Institutional power: General Secretary controlled appointments (patronage), party organisation, and records. Built loyal network.
  • Ideological flexibility: shifted from left (anti-NEP) to right (pro-NEP) and back to left, outmanoeuvring opponents.
  • Trotsky's weaknesses: underestimated Stalin; missed Lenin's funeral (possibly due to illness — Stalin gave wrong date); arrogant; "permanent revolution" unpopular.
  • Stalin's image: "man of the people," modest, hardworking. Cultivated image of Lenin's loyal disciple.

Collectivisation: Why and With What Results?

  • Motives: ideological (socialist agriculture); political (destroy kulaks as class); economic (extract grain for export and industrialisation); strategic (feed cities and army).
  • Resistance: passive (slaughtering livestock) and active (rebellions in Ukraine and North Caucasus). Government responded with terror and deportation.
  • Human cost: famine of 1932–33 was man-made — grain requisitioned while peasants starved. Some historians debate whether it was genocide (Ukrainian and some Western historians) or catastrophic policy failure (some Russian historians).
  • Long-term: agriculture remained inefficient; kolkhozniki had no incentive; USSR dependent on food imports by 1970s.

The Purges: Causes and Consequences

  • Traditional explanation: Stalin's paranoia and desire for total power. Destroyed all potential rivals.
  • Alternative explanations: social engineering (Sheila Fitzpatrick) — purges as a tool to create a new Soviet elite; local initiative (Getty, Naumov) — regional bosses used purges to settle scores; economic pressures — scapegoating for plan failures.
  • Consequences: Red Army weakened (affected early WWII performance); created climate of fear; but also opened careers for ambitious young workers and party members.

📙 A2 Level Content

Totalitarian Model vs. Revisionism

  • Totalitarian (Carl Friedrich, Zbigniew Brzezinski): Stalin controlled everything through ideology, terror, and monopoly of communications. Society was atomised and passive.
  • Revisionist (Sheila Fitzpatrick, Ronald Suny): Soviet society was not passive. There was social mobility, genuine enthusiasm for modernisation, and negotiation between state and society. Purges were also driven from below (denunciations, local conflicts).
  • Post-revisionist (Stephen Kotkin): Stalinism was a civilisation — people internalised values, worked within system, made careers. Magnitogorsk as case study.

Stalin as Revolutionary or Conservative?

  • Revolutionary: transformed USSR from agrarian to industrial; destroyed old elites; created new socialist society; spread communism.
  • Conservative: reinforced authoritarian tradition of Russian state; used nationalism ("Great Patriotic War"); restored family values (1936 anti-abortion law); hierarchical and bureaucratic.
  • Continuity debate: was Stalinism a continuation of Leninism (one-party state, Cheka, War Communism) or a betrayal?

Comparing Stalin and Hitler

  • Similarities: one-party states, cult of personality, terror camps (Gulag vs. concentration camps), control of culture and education, aggressive foreign policy, mass murder.
  • Differences: Stalin rose within party bureaucracy; Hitler came to power through election/alliance. Nazi ideology was racial; Soviet was class-based. Stalin's terror was often directed at party members; Hitler's initially targeted outsiders (Jews, Communists).
  • Historiographical caution: comparison risks equating two distinct systems; but useful for understanding totalitarianism as a concept.

Essay Frameworks

  • "Stalin's industrialisation was a success": USSR second industrial power by 1937; won WWII; modernised society. Counter: appalling human cost; inefficiency; consumer neglect; agriculture damaged.
  • "The purges were caused by Stalin's paranoia": Eliminated rivals, destroyed Old Bolsheviks, controlled all power. Counter: local dynamics, social engineering, economic scapegoating.
  • "Stalinism was a continuation of Leninism": One-party state, secret police, censorship, terror. Counter: Lenin allowed some debate (until 1921); NEP was market-based; Stalin's personal dictatorship exceeded Lenin's.

📜 Worked Example

Question: To what extent was Germany responsible for WWI?

Essay Plan:
FOR: Blank cheque, Schlieffen Plan, naval expansion, Fischer thesis
AGAINST: Austrian ultimatum, Russian mobilisation, alliance system, Clark "sleepwalkers"
Conclusion: Significant responsibility but not sole blame
Writing narrative instead of analysis. History essays need CAUSATION — explain WHY and SIGNIFICANCE.
Always use SPECIFIC EVIDENCE — dates, names, statistics. "On 28 June 1914" not "in 1914".

Source Analysis Framework

  • Content: What does the source say? What information does it provide?
  • Provenance: Who wrote it? When? Why? What is their perspective?
  • Reliability: Is it trustworthy? What biases might exist?
  • Utility: How useful is it for a specific enquiry?

Essay Structure

  • Introduction: Define key terms, set out argument/line of reasoning
  • Paragraphs: Point → Evidence → Explanation → Link back to question
  • Synthesis: Compare factors, weigh importance, show interconnections
  • Conclusion: Judgment based on evidence, avoid introducing new points
Always answer the EXACT question asked. Underline key words ("To what extent", "How far", "Most important") and address them directly.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Describing a source instead of analysing it — explain what it shows and why it matters.
  • Using vague generalisations instead of specific evidence — always include dates, names, and statistics.
  • Writing narrative instead of analysis — history essays need causation and significance, not just chronology.
  • Using modern perspectives to judge historical actions — consider the context of the time.
  • Failing to reach a clear judgement in essay questions — your conclusion should answer the question directly.

✅ Key Points to Remember

  • Practise writing timed essays — time management is crucial in the examination.
  • Always plan your essay before writing — a clear structure improves coherence and argumentation.
  • Memorise key dates, names, and events — specific evidence is essential for high marks.
  • Learn to evaluate sources using NOP (Nature, Origin, Purpose) and content analysis.
  • Be able to compare and contrast different historical interpretations of the same event.

Historical Analysis: h9

History is not about memorising dates and events — it is about understanding causation, change, continuity, and significance. Examiners reward analysis over narrative.

Essay Structure
  1. Introduction: Define key terms and state your line of argument.
  2. Paragraphs: Each should make one point, supported by specific evidence.
  3. Analysis: Explain why events happened and why they matter.
  4. Conclusion: Directly answer the question with a clear judgement.
Evidence and Analysis

Poor: "The Treaty of Versailles was signed in 1919 and Germany had to pay reparations."

Better: "The Treaty of Versailles imposed reparations of £6.6 billion on Germany because the war guilt clause (Article 231) held Germany responsible for WWI. This meant that the German economy was crippled by debt, leading to hyperinflation in 1923 and resentment that fuelled Nazi support."

Specific evidence earns marks. "On 28 June 1914, Franz Ferdinand was assassinated in Sarajevo" is far better than "in 1914 something happened". Always use names, dates, and statistics.

Worked Example 1: Analysing a Source

Problem

Analyse the usefulness of a government propaganda poster from 1916 encouraging men to enlist in the army.

Solution

Content: The poster shows a smiling soldier with the caption "Your Country Needs You." It portrays military service as honourable and patriotic.

Provenance: Created by the British government in 1916, after conscription was introduced. The purpose was to encourage voluntary enlistment and maintain public support for the war.

Usefulness: Useful for showing how governments used emotional appeals to recruit soldiers. However, limited by bias — it does not show the realities of trench warfare. It should be compared with letters from soldiers or casualty figures for a balanced view.

Worked Example 2: Causation Essay Plan

Problem

To what extent was the Treaty of Versailles responsible for the outbreak of World War II?

Essay Plan

Introduction: Define the Treaty of Versailles (1919) and state that while it contributed to WWII, it was not the sole cause.

FOR (Treaty was responsible):

  • War guilt clause (Article 231) humiliated Germany
  • Reparations crippled the German economy (hyperinflation 1923)
  • Territorial losses (13% of land, colonies, Alsace-Lorraine) created resentment
  • Demilitarisation was seen as unfair and weak

AGAINST (Other factors):

  • Failure of the League of Nations to stop aggression (Manchuria 1931, Abyssinia 1935)
  • Appeasement policy of Britain and France (Munich Agreement 1938)
  • Great Depression created economic instability worldwide
  • Hitler's expansionist ambitions and rearmament

Conclusion: The Treaty created conditions for resentment but did not make war inevitable. The failures of the League, appeasement, and Hitler's actions were equally or more important.

Worked Example 3: Comparing Interpretations

Problem

Historian A argues that the Cold War was caused by Soviet aggression. Historian B argues it was caused by American policy. Evaluate these interpretations.

Solution

Historian A (Soviet aggression): Supported by Soviet expansion into Eastern Europe (1945–48), creation of puppet states, Berlin Blockade (1948), and invasion of Hungary (1956).

Historian B (American policy): Supported by Truman Doctrine (1947), Marshall Plan (1948), NATO (1949), and US support for anti-communist regimes worldwide.

Evaluation: Both interpretations have merit. Soviet actions in Eastern Europe were aggressive, but US policies like containment were also confrontational. The orthodox view (Soviet blame) dominated until the 1960s; the revisionist view (US blame) emerged with the Vietnam War. Post-revisionist historians (like Gaddis) argue both sides share responsibility.

Practice Questions

Question 1

Analyse the reliability of a diary entry from a soldier on the Western Front in 1916.

Answer: Consider proximity to events (likely reliable on details), personal perspective (may be emotional or biased), purpose (private vs published), compare with official records or other soldiers' accounts.

Question 2

Explain why the League of Nations failed to stop Japanese aggression in Manchuria (1931).

Answer: Economic sanctions not applied (USA not member, major trading partner); no military force available; Britain and France unwilling to act (focused on domestic issues); Japan simply left the League; slow decision-making process.

Question 3

"The Cuban Missile Crisis was a victory for the USA." How far do you agree?

Answer: FOR: missiles removed, Khrushchev backed down, USA appeared strong. AGAINST: USA had to remove missiles from Turkey secretly, crisis showed dangers of brinkmanship, hotline established afterwards. CONCLUSION: Short-term victory for USA but long-term recognition of need for détente.

🎯 Examination Tips

  • Use the formula for source evaluation: what it says + what this suggests + how reliable/useful it is + why.
  • Each paragraph should make one main point, supported by evidence and explanation.
  • In essay questions, use specific evidence — "On 28 June 1914" not "in 1914".
  • Leave time to review — check that you have answered the question asked, not the one you expected.
  • Check the number of marks — higher marks require greater depth, analysis, and evaluation.
