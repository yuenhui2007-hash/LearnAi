The Origins of the Civil War in the USA

Cambridge 9389 A-Level / 0470 IGCSE

📘 IGCSE Content

Slavery and the Antebellum South

  • By 1860: 4 million enslaved people in 15 slave states. Cotton kingdom — "King Cotton" dominated exports and global textile industry.
  • Slave system: plantation agriculture (cotton, tobacco, rice, sugar). Slave codes restricted movement, education, marriage. Punishments: whipping, sale of family members.
  • Domestic slave trade: 1 million enslaved people forcibly moved from Upper South to Deep South (1810–60) — "Second Middle Passage."
  • Free Black population: 500,000 by 1860; faced discrimination and risk of kidnapping.

North and South: Diverging Economies and Societies

  • North: industrialising, urbanising, wage labour, immigration (Irish, German), diversified economy, railroads, canals.
  • South: agrarian, dependent on slave labour, limited industrialisation, wealth concentrated in planter class (top 10% owned 50% of slaves).
  • Political balance: 11 free states and 11 slave states by 1819. Missouri Compromise (1820) maintained balance: Missouri slave, Maine free; slavery prohibited north of 36°30′ (except Missouri).

Expansion and the Slavery Question

  • Texas (1836–45): Independent after rebellion against Mexico; annexed 1845 — provoked Mexican-American War (1846–48).
  • Mexican Cession (1848): USA gained California, New Mexico, Arizona, etc. Wilmot Proviso (1846) sought to ban slavery in new territories — defeated but inflamed debate.
  • Compromise of 1850: California free state; Utah and New Mexico territories decide by popular sovereignty; Fugitive Slave Act strengthened; slave trade banned in DC (but slavery retained).
  • Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854): Stephen Douglas's bill organised territories on basis of popular sovereignty — overturned Missouri Compromise. Led to "Bleeding Kansas" — pro- and anti-slavery settlers fought (1854–56).

The Rise of Abolitionism and Southern Reaction

  • Abolitionism: William Lloyd Garrison (The Liberator, 1831); Theodore Weld; Frederick Douglass (escaped slave, orator, writer); Harriet Tubman (Underground Railroad).
  • Uncle Tom's Cabin (Harriet Beecher Stowe, 1852): bestselling novel; shaped Northern opinion against slavery.
  • Southern defence of slavery: "positive good" argument (John C. Calhoun); biblical justifications; paternalism. Gag rules in Congress suppressed anti-slavery petitions.

Political Crisis: 1850s

  • Fugitive Slave Act (1850): Required citizens to assist in capturing escaped slaves. Outraged North; Personal Liberty Laws passed by Northern states.
  • Dred Scott decision (1857): Supreme Court ruled African Americans could not be citizens; Congress could not ban slavery in territories. Undermined Republican platform.
  • Lincoln-Douglas debates (1858): Senate race in Illinois. Douglas defended popular sovereignty; Lincoln opposed expansion of slavery but did not call for abolition where it existed. Douglas won Senate seat but debates made Lincoln nationally known.
  • John Brown's raid (Oct 1859): Abolitionist seized federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry; hoped to spark slave rebellion. Executed. Seen as martyr in North; terrorist in South.

The Election of 1860 and Secession

  • 1860 election: Democratic Party split (Northern Douglas vs. Southern Breckinridge). Republicans nominated Lincoln on platform of opposing slavery's expansion (not abolition).
  • Lincoln won without a single Southern electoral vote. 39.8% popular vote, 180 electoral votes.
  • South Carolina seceded (20 Dec 1860). By Feb 1861, 7 states had seceded. Confederate States of America formed (Feb 1861); Jefferson Davis president.
  • Lincoln's inaugural (4 March 1861): pledged not to interfere with slavery where it existed but vowed to hold federal property.
  • Fort Sumter (12 April 1861): Confederate forces fired on federal fort in Charleston harbour. Lincoln called for volunteers to suppress rebellion. 4 more states seceded (Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, North Carolina).

📗 AS Level Content

Was the Civil War Inevitable?

  • Earlier compromises (1820, 1850) had preserved Union. But each compromise was more difficult and left both sides dissatisfied.
  • Structural factors: slavery was incompatible with free labour ideology; expansion made compromise impossible because each new state tilted balance.
  • Contingent factors: if Douglas had won 1860, or if different candidate had run, or if Fort Sumter had been evacuated — war might have been delayed or avoided. But fundamental conflict likely to erupt eventually.

The Role of Political Parties

  • Second Party System (Whigs vs Democrats) collapsed over slavery. Whigs divided (Conscience vs Cotton Whigs); collapsed by 1856.
  • Republican Party founded (1854): anti-slavery expansion, free soil, free labour, protective tariffs. Drew from former Whigs, Free-Soilers, anti-slavery Democrats.
  • Democrats: only national party remaining but split along sectional lines by 1860.
  • Know-Nothings (American Party): nativist, anti-immigrant, anti-Catholic; briefly strong but irrelevant to slavery issue.

Lincoln's Position on Slavery

  • Lincoln was anti-slavery but not an abolitionist in 1860. Opposed expansion but promised not to interfere in slave states. This was enough to provoke secession.
  • Why did South secede despite Lincoln's moderation? Because slave economy required expansion to maintain political power and economic viability. "Slave Power" feared encirclement by free states.
  • Lincoln's evolution: Emancipation Proclamation (1863) transformed war aims. But see Civil Rights notes for Reconstruction era.

Economic Interpretations

  • Charles Beard (1920s): war was conflict between industrial Northeast and agrarian South over economic interests (tariffs, banks, internal improvements). Slavery was pretext.
  • Critics: Beard underestimated moral and political dimensions. Most Northerners fought to preserve Union, not for economic gain. Slavery was central to Southern economy and identity.

📙 A2 Level Content

Historiography: Causes of the Civil War

  • "Blundering generation" (James Randall, Avery Craven): War caused by failures of political leadership and extremists on both sides. Moderates lost control; emotion overran reason. Slavery could have died out peacefully.
  • Revisionist (1930s–40s): War was avoidable; abolitionists and fire-eaters (Southern extremists) pushed moderates into conflict.
  • Neo-revisionist / Consensus: War was fundamentally about slavery, but not necessarily inevitable. Compromises failed because of political miscalculations.
  • Modern consensus (since 1960s): Slavery was the central cause. Without slavery, no secession; without secession, no war. Economic, political, and cultural differences all derived from slavery system.

Slavery as a Constitutional and Moral Issue

  • Constitutional ambiguity: Constitution never mentioned "slavery" directly ("persons held to service"). 3/5 clause gave South disproportionate power. Fugitive Slave clause.
  • States' rights vs federal authority: South used states' rights to protect slavery; but supported federal Fugitive Slave Act. Inconsistency revealed slavery as real issue, not abstract states' rights.
  • Moral dimensions: abolitionism was a moral crusade; Southern defence of slavery also moralised ("positive good," biblical, paternalistic).

The International Dimension

  • Britain and France depended on Southern cotton but had anti-slavery sentiment. British workers supported North despite Lancashire cotton famine.
  • Trent Affair (1861): Union intercepted British ship carrying Confederate diplomats; nearly caused Anglo-American war. Lincoln released diplomats.
  • Emancipation Proclamation (1863) made it impossible for Britain to support South.

Essay Frameworks

  • "Slavery was the only cause of the Civil War": Secession documents cite slavery; economy dependent on it; Dred Scott, Kansas-Nebraska, all about slavery. Counter: states' rights, tariff disputes, cultural differences also factors — but these derived from slavery.
  • "The Civil War was caused by political failure": Compromises of 1820, 1850 failed; party system collapsed; leaders polarised. Counter: political failure was symptom of irreconcilable differences, not cause.
  • "The South seceded to protect slavery, not states' rights": Confederate constitution protected slavery; states' rights rhetoric inconsistent (supported Fugitive Slave Act). Counter: Southerners genuinely believed in limited federal government.

📝 Last-Minute Summary

  • 1820: Missouri Compromise maintains balance.
  • 1846: Wilmot Proviso — first major sectional clash over expansion.
  • 1850 Compromise: California free; popular sovereignty for Utah/New Mexico; Fugitive Slave Act.
  • 1854: Kansas-Nebraska Act → "Bleeding Kansas."
  • 1857: Dred Scott — slavery cannot be banned in territories.
  • 1858: Lincoln-Douglas debates.
  • 1859: John Brown's raid.
  • 1860: Lincoln elected; South Carolina secedes (Dec).
  • 1861: 7 states secede by Feb; Confederate States formed; Fort Sumter (12 April) → war begins; 4 more states secede.
  • Historiography: "Blundering generation" vs modern consensus that slavery was central cause. Beard's economic interpretation largely rejected.
  • Exam Tip: Know the chronology 1820→1850→1854→1857→1860→1861. Always link sectional crises back to slavery. Distinguish Lincoln's 1860 position (anti-expansion, not abolitionist) from later Emancipation.
