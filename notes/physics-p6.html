IGCSE AS Level

Waves & Superposition

9702 (A-Level) · 0625 (IGCSE)

Detailed Notes

1. Progressive Waves

A progressive (travelling) wave transfers energy from one place to another without transferring matter. All waves can be described by the same fundamental quantities:

  • Amplitude (A): Maximum displacement from equilibrium position. Unit: metres.
  • Wavelength (λ): Distance between two consecutive points in phase (e.g., crest to crest). Unit: metres.
  • Frequency (f): Number of complete oscillations per second. Unit: hertz (Hz).
  • Period (T): Time for one complete oscillation. T = 1/f. Unit: seconds.
  • Wave speed (v): Distance travelled by the wave per unit time. v = fλ.
v = fλ

📖 Key Definition

Displacement: The distance of a point on the wave from its equilibrium position at a given time.

Phase: A measure of the stage of oscillation of a point on the wave, expressed as an angle (0° to 360° or 0 to 2π radians). Points one wavelength apart are in phase (phase difference = 0° or 360°).

The equation of a progressive wave travelling in the +x direction can be written as:

y = A sin(ωt − kx)

where ω = 2πf (angular frequency) and k = 2π/λ (wave number).

2. Transverse and Longitudinal Waves

  • Transverse waves: Oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer. Examples: waves on a string, electromagnetic waves (light, radio, X-rays), water waves.
  • Longitudinal waves: Oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer. Examples: sound waves in air, compression waves in a spring.
In transverse waves, we refer to crests (maximum positive displacement) and troughs (maximum negative displacement). In longitudinal waves, we refer to compressions (regions of high pressure/density) and rarefactions (regions of low pressure/density).

3. Electromagnetic Spectrum

All electromagnetic waves are transverse, travel at c ≈ 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum, and require no medium. They are arranged by wavelength/frequency:

TypeWavelength range (approx)Source/Use
Radio waves> 1 mRadio/TV broadcasting
Microwaves1 mm – 1 mMobile phones, cooking, radar
Infrared700 nm – 1 mmHeat radiation, remote controls
Visible light400 – 700 nmHuman vision
Ultraviolet10 – 400 nmSun tanning, sterilisation
X-rays0.01 – 10 nmMedical imaging
Gamma rays< 0.01 nmNuclear decay, cancer therapy
The energy of an electromagnetic photon is E = hf, where h is Planck's constant. Gamma rays have the highest frequency and therefore the highest photon energy.

4. Superposition Principle

When two or more waves meet at a point, the resultant displacement is the vector sum of the individual displacements. This principle explains interference, diffraction, and standing waves.

  • Constructive interference: Crest meets crest (or trough meets trough) → amplitudes add, resulting in a larger displacement.
  • Destructive interference: Crest meets trough → amplitudes subtract. If the amplitudes are equal, the resultant displacement is zero.
For constructive interference, the path difference between the two waves must be an integer multiple of the wavelength: path difference = nλ (where n = 0, 1, 2, ...). For destructive interference: path difference = (n + ½)λ.

5. Stationary (Standing) Waves

A stationary wave is formed when two progressive waves of the same frequency and amplitude travel in opposite directions and superpose. No net energy is transferred.

  • Nodes (N): Points of zero amplitude (permanent destructive interference). Distance between adjacent nodes = λ/2.
  • Antinodes (A): Points of maximum amplitude (permanent constructive interference). Distance between adjacent antinodes = λ/2. Distance from node to nearest antinode = λ/4.

On a string fixed at both ends, the allowed wavelengths are:

λₙ = 2L/n (where n = 1, 2, 3, ...)
Fundamental (n=1): λ = 2L, f = v/(2L)
First overtone (n=2): λ = L, f = v/L
Second overtone (n=3): λ = 2L/3, f = 3v/(2L)

📝 Worked Example — Stationary Waves on a String

Question: A string of length 0.80 m is fixed at both ends. The speed of transverse waves on the string is 40 m/s. Calculate the frequencies of the fundamental and first two overtones.

Solution:

Fundamental: f₁ = v/(2L) = 40/(2 × 0.80) = 25 Hz

First overtone: f₂ = 2f₁ = 50 Hz

Second overtone: f₃ = 3f₁ = 75 Hz

6. Diffraction

Diffraction is the spreading of waves as they pass through an aperture or around an obstacle. Diffraction effects are most significant when the gap width or obstacle size is comparable to the wavelength (≈ λ).

  • Narrow gap (≈ λ): significant spreading, semicircular wavefronts.
  • Wide gap (>> λ): minimal spreading, waves pass through almost straight.
Diffraction explains why you can hear sounds around corners (sound has wavelengths comparable to doorways, ~1 m) but cannot see around corners (light has very short wavelengths, ~500 nm).

7. Double-Slit Interference

When coherent light (constant phase relationship) passes through two narrow slits, an interference pattern of bright and dark fringes is produced on a screen.

Fringe spacing: w = λD/a
where D = slit-to-screen distance, a = slit separation

📝 Worked Example — Double-Slit

Question: In a double-slit experiment, light of wavelength 600 nm produces fringes spaced 2.4 mm apart on a screen 1.5 m from the slits. Calculate the slit separation.

Solution:

w = λD/a → a = λD/w

a = (600 × 10⁻⁹ × 1.5) / (2.4 × 10⁻³) = 3.75 × 10⁻⁴ m (0.375 mm)

⚠️ Common Misconception

Students often confuse diffraction and interference. Diffraction is the spreading of waves around obstacles or through gaps. Interference is the superposition of waves from coherent sources. In the double-slit experiment, diffraction occurs at each slit, and interference occurs between the waves from the two slits.

📝 Worked Example — Path Difference

Question: Two coherent sources S₁ and S₂ emit waves of wavelength 4.0 cm. A detector is placed 60 cm from S₁ and 64 cm from S₂. Determine whether constructive or destructive interference occurs at the detector.

Solution:

Path difference = 64 − 60 = 4.0 cm = λ

Since path difference = nλ (n = 1), constructive interference occurs.

🎯 Exam-Style Question

Question: (a) State two conditions necessary for observable interference between two light sources. (b) In a double-slit experiment using light of wavelength 550 nm, the slit separation is 0.40 mm and the screen is 2.0 m from the slits. Calculate the separation of adjacent bright fringes. [4 marks]

Mark Scheme

• (a) Any two from: same frequency/wavelength; constant phase difference / coherent; similar amplitudes (2 marks — 1 each)

• (b) w = λD/a = (550 × 10⁻⁹ × 2.0) / (0.40 × 10⁻³) (1 mark)

• w = 2.75 × 10⁻³ m (accept 2.8 mm) (1 mark)

🎯 Exam-Style Question

Question: A stationary wave is set up on a string of length 1.2 m fixed at both ends. There are three antinodes along the string. The wave speed is 72 m/s. Calculate: (a) the wavelength, (b) the frequency of the wave. [3 marks]

Mark Scheme

• (a) Three antinodes means n = 3 (second overtone). λ = 2L/3 = (2 × 1.2)/3 = 0.80 m (1 mark)

• (b) f = v/λ = 72/0.80 = 90 Hz (2 marks — 1 for method, 1 for answer)

Last-Minute Summary

  • Wave equation: v = fλ
  • Transverse: oscillations ⊥ to energy transfer; Longitudinal: oscillations ∥ to energy transfer
  • EM spectrum: radio → microwave → IR → visible → UV → X-ray → gamma (all at c = 3×10⁸ m/s in vacuum)
  • Superposition: vector sum of displacements
  • Constructive: path difference = nλ; Destructive: path difference = (n + ½)λ
  • Stationary waves: same f, same A, opposite directions → nodes and antinodes
  • Node-to-node distance = λ/2; node to antinode = λ/4
  • Diffraction significant when gap ≈ λ
  • Double-slit: w = λD/a

Exam Tips

  • Always draw wavefront diagrams for interference and diffraction problems.
  • Remember that for coherent sources, the waves must have the same frequency and a constant phase difference.
  • When calculating fringe spacing, ensure all lengths are in consistent units (usually metres).
  • For stationary waves on a string fixed at both ends, the fundamental has one antinode; each overtone adds one more antinode.
  • In the double-slit formula, w is the fringe spacing (centre to centre of adjacent bright fringes).
  • Diffraction explains spreading; interference explains the pattern of bright and dark fringes.
