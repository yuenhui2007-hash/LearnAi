Organic Synthesis Strategies

Organic synthesis involves building target molecules from simple starting materials through a series of reactions. Planning a synthesis requires knowledge of functional group interconversions and reaction conditions.

📘 Key Definitions

Retrosynthesis: Working backwards from the target molecule to identify suitable starting materials.

Functional group interconversion: Changing one functional group into another.

Protecting group: A functional group added temporarily to prevent unwanted reactions.

Common Synthetic Routes

TargetRoute
AlkeneAlcohol → elimination (conc. H₂SO₄, 170°C)
HalogenoalkaneAlcohol + PCl₅ or alcohol + HX
AlcoholAlkene + H₂O (H₃PO₄) or carbonyl + NaBH₄ / LiAlH₄
AldehydePrimary alcohol + [O], distil
KetoneSecondary alcohol + [O], reflux
Carboxylic acidPrimary alcohol + [O], reflux or nitrile + H⁺/H₂O
AmineNitrile + H₂/Ni or LiAlH₄
NitrileHalogenoalkane + KCN (ethanol)
Worked Example: Synthesis of Propanoic Acid from Propene

Step 1: CH₃CH=CH₂ + HBr → CH₃CHBrCH₃ (Markovnikov addition)

Step 2: CH₃CHBrCH₃ + KCN → CH₃CH(CN)CH₃ + KBr

Step 3: CH₃CH(CN)CH₃ + 2H₂O + H⁺ → CH₃CH(CH₃)COOH + NH₄⁺

Note: This gives 2-methylpropanoic acid. For propanoic acid directly:

Alternative: CH₃CH=CH₂ + H₂O → CH₃CH₂CH₂OH (anti-Markovnikov via hydroboration, or via hydrolysis of CH₃CH₂CH₂Br)

CH₃CH₂CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃CH₂COOH

When planning syntheses: 1. Count carbon atoms — ensure they match 2. Consider regioselectivity (Markovnikov vs anti-Markovnikov) 3. Choose appropriate reagents and conditions 4. Consider yield and number of steps (fewer is better) 5. Think about purification methods

Infrared Spectroscopy

Infrared (IR) spectroscopy identifies functional groups by measuring the absorption of infrared radiation, which causes molecular vibrations (stretching, bending, twisting).

📘 Key Definitions

Wavenumber: The number of waves per centimetre (cm⁻¹). Proportional to frequency and energy.

Fingerprint region: The region below 1500 cm⁻¹ containing complex patterns unique to each compound.

BondWavenumber (cm⁻¹)Intensity
O−H (alcohol)3230–3550Strong, broad
O−H (carboxylic acid)2500–3300Very broad
N−H (amine/amide)3300–3500Medium
C−H (alkane)2850–2960Strong
C−H (alkene)3010–3100Medium
C≡H (terminal alkyne)3300Strong
C=O (aldehyde, ketone, acid, ester)1680–1750Strong
C=O (amide)1630–1690Strong
C=C (alkene)1620–1680Variable
C≡C (alkyne)2100–2260Variable
C≡N (nitrile)2210–2260Medium
C−O (alcohol, ester, ether)1000–1300Strong
The fingerprint region (below 1500 cm⁻¹) is unique to each compound and can be used to identify a substance by matching against a database. For identifying functional groups, focus on the region above 1500 cm⁻¹.
Worked Example: Interpreting IR Spectra

A compound shows peaks at 1720 cm⁻¹ (strong), 2950 cm⁻¹, and 1200 cm⁻¹. Identify the functional groups.

Solution:

1720 cm⁻¹: C=O (carbonyl) — could be aldehyde, ketone, ester, or carboxylic acid

2950 cm⁻¹: C-H stretching (alkane/alkyl groups)

1200 cm⁻¹: C-O stretching

Combination suggests an ester (C=O + C-O, no broad O-H of acid).

Mass Spectrometry in Organic Chemistry

Mass spectrometry determines molecular mass and provides structural information through fragmentation patterns.

The molecular ion peak (M⁺) gives the relative molecular mass (Mr). It is the peak with the highest m/z value on the right side of the spectrum.

Common Fragmentation Patterns

m/zFragmentLost from M⁺
M−15CH₃•Methyl loss
M−29CHO• or C₂H₅•Aldehyde or ethyl loss
M−43CH₃CO• or C₃H₇•Acetyl or propyl loss
M−45COOH•Carboxylic acid
15CH₃⁺Methyl cation
29CHO⁺ or C₂H₅⁺Aldehyde or ethyl cation
43CH₃CO⁺ or C₃H₇⁺Acetyl or propyl cation
77C₆H₅⁺Phenyl cation
91C₇H₇⁺Benzyl cation
Worked Example: Identifying a Compound from Mass Spectrum

A compound has M⁺ at m/z 58. Major fragments at m/z 43 and 15. Suggest a structure.

Solution:

M = 58 suggests C₃H₆O (propanal or propanone)

m/z 43 = CH₃CO⁺ (loss of CH₃, M−15)

m/z 15 = CH₃⁺

This pattern fits propanone (CH₃COCH₃) — symmetrical, easy loss of CH₃ to give stable acylium ion.

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) Spectroscopy

NMR spectroscopy provides detailed information about the carbon-hydrogen framework of organic molecules. ¹H NMR (proton NMR) is most commonly used at A-Level.

📘 Key Definitions

Chemical shift (δ): The position of an NMR signal, measured in ppm from a reference compound (TMS = 0 ppm).

Integration: The area under each peak, proportional to the number of equivalent protons.

Splitting pattern (multiplicity): The number of peaks in a signal, determined by the n+1 rule.

n+1 rule: A signal is split into (n+1) peaks by n equivalent adjacent protons.

Type of ProtonChemical Shift (δ/ppm)
R-CH₃0.9–1.0

R₃CH1.4–1.6
C=C-H (alkene)4.5–6.5

R-O-H (alcohol)1.0–5.5 (variable)
R-CHO (aldehyde)9.0–10.0
R-COO-H (carboxylic acid)10.0–12.0

Splitting Patterns

Adjacent Protons (n)SplittingPattern
0Singlet (s)1 peak
1Doublet (d)2 peaks, 1:1
2Triplet (t)3 peaks, 1:2:1
3Quartet (q)4 peaks, 1:3:3:1
Worked Example: Interpreting ¹H NMR

A compound C₃H₆O has ¹H NMR: δ 2.1 (singlet, 3H), δ 2.2 (singlet, 3H). Identify the compound.

Solution:

Two singlets → no adjacent protons on either group.

δ 2.1 and 2.2 → typical of methyl ketone (CH₃COCH₃, propanone).

Both CH₃ groups are equivalent in a symmetrical environment.

Worked Example: NMR of Ethyl Ethanoate

CH₃COOCH₂CH₃

• CH₃ (acyl): singlet, δ ~2.0, 3H

• CH₂: quartet, δ ~4.1, 2H (adjacent to CH₃, n=3, n+1=4)

• CH₃ (ethyl): triplet, δ ~1.2, 3H (adjacent to CH₂, n=2, n+1=3)

In low-resolution NMR, focus on: number of peaks (types of proton environment), chemical shifts (functional groups), integration (relative numbers of protons), and splitting (neighbouring protons). High-resolution NMR shows further splitting from non-equivalent neighbouring protons.

Chromatography

Chromatography separates mixtures based on differential partitioning between a stationary phase and a mobile phase.

📘 Key Definitions

Stationary phase: A solid or liquid fixed in place.

Mobile phase: A liquid or gas that moves through the stationary phase, carrying the sample.

Retention factor (Rf): In TLC, Rf = distance moved by spot / distance moved by solvent front.

Types of Chromatography

TypeStationary PhaseMobile PhaseApplication
Thin-layer (TLC)Silica gel / alumina on plateLiquid solventMonitoring reactions, identifying compounds
Gas-liquid (GLC/GC)High bp liquid on solid supportInert gas (N₂, He)Volatile mixtures, quantitative analysis
PaperWater in paper fibresLiquid solventSeparating amino acids, sugars
In chromatography, components that interact more strongly with the stationary phase move more slowly. Less polar compounds move faster in non-polar stationary phases; more polar compounds move faster in polar stationary phases (normal phase vs reverse phase).

Exam-Style Questions

📝 Exam Question 1

An organic compound has the following spectral data:

IR: strong peak at 1715 cm⁻¹

¹H NMR: δ 1.1 (triplet, 3H), δ 2.4 (quartet, 2H), δ 9.8 (triplet, 1H)

Mass spec: M⁺ at m/z 72

Identify the compound and explain your reasoning. [5]

Mark Scheme:
IR: 1715 cm⁻¹ = C=O stretch (1). 9.8 ppm = aldehyde proton (1).
NMR: triplet at 1.1 ppm = CH₃ next to CH₂ (1). Quartet at 2.4 ppm = CH₂ next to CH₃ (1). Integration 3:2:1 consistent with propanal (1). M⁺ = 72 matches C₃H₆O (1). Max 5.
📝 Exam Question 2

Explain how GLC can be used to determine the composition of a mixture of volatile organic compounds. [3]

Mark Scheme:
Mixture is vaporised and carried by inert gas through column (1). Different components interact differently with stationary phase (1). Components separate based on retention times; peak area proportional to amount (1).

📝 Quick Summary

  • Organic synthesis: plan routes using functional group interconversions
  • IR: identifies functional groups by bond vibrations; fingerprint region is unique
  • Key IR peaks: O-H (3200–3550), C=O (1680–1750), C-H (2850–3100)
  • Mass spec: M⁺ gives Mr; fragmentation gives structural clues
  • NMR: chemical shift (environment), integration (number of H), splitting (neighbours)
  • n+1 rule: n adjacent equivalent H → n+1 peaks
  • Chromatography separates by partitioning between stationary and mobile phases
  • Rf = distance spot / distance solvent in TLC

💡 Exam Tips

  • When interpreting spectra, consider ALL available data together
  • IR fingerprint region below 1500 cm⁻¹ is unique to each compound
  • In NMR, always apply the n+1 rule to predict splitting
  • A singlet at δ 9–10 = aldehyde; δ 10–12 = carboxylic acid
  • Use mass spec M⁺ to confirm molecular formula
  • In synthesis questions, count carbons and ensure reagents are appropriate
