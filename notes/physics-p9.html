IGCSE AS Level

D.C. Circuits

9702 (A-Level) · 0625 (IGCSE)

Detailed Notes

1. Circuit Symbols

You must be able to recognise and draw standard circuit symbols: cell, battery, resistor, variable resistor, lamp, switch, ammeter, voltmeter, fuse, diode, LED, thermistor, LDR, and op-amp. A circuit diagram should be drawn with straight lines, right-angle corners, and correct symbols.

2. Series Circuits

In a series circuit, components are connected end-to-end, so there is only one path for current.

  • Current is the same through all components.
  • Total p.d. = sum of individual p.d.s: V_total = V₁ + V₂ + V₃ + ...
  • Total resistance: R_total = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...
R_total = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...
Adding resistors in series always increases the total resistance. The total resistance is greater than the largest individual resistor.

3. Parallel Circuits

In a parallel circuit, components are connected across the same two points, providing multiple paths for current.

  • P.d. is the same across all branches.
  • Total current = sum of branch currents: I_total = I₁ + I₂ + I₃ + ...
  • Total resistance: 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + ...
1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + ...
Adding resistors in parallel always decreases the total resistance. The total resistance is less than the smallest individual resistor. For two resistors: R_total = R₁R₂/(R₁ + R₂).

4. Potential Divider

A potential divider (voltage divider) uses two resistors in series to obtain a fraction of the supply voltage:

V_out = V_in × R₂/(R₁ + R₂)

This principle is used in sensor circuits where one resistor is a sensor (thermistor or LDR) whose resistance changes with the physical quantity being measured.

📝 Worked Example — Potential Divider

Question: A potential divider consists of a 4.0 kΩ resistor and a 6.0 kΩ resistor connected across a 12 V supply. Calculate the output voltage across the 6.0 kΩ resistor.

Solution:

V_out = 12 × 6000/(4000 + 6000) = 12 × 6000/10000 = 7.2 V

5. Kirchhoff's Laws

Kirchhoff's laws are fundamental principles that apply to all circuits:

  • First Law (Current Law): At any junction, the total current entering equals the total current leaving. ΣI_in = ΣI_out. This is a statement of conservation of charge.
  • Second Law (Voltage Law): Around any closed loop, the sum of e.m.f.s equals the sum of p.d.s. ΣE = ΣIR. This is a statement of conservation of energy.

📝 Worked Example — Kirchhoff's Laws

Question: In the circuit shown, a 6.0 V cell (negligible internal resistance) is connected to resistors of 2.0 Ω and 4.0 Ω in parallel, with a 3.0 Ω resistor in series with the combination. Calculate the current through each resistor.

Solution:

Parallel combination: 1/R_p = 1/2.0 + 1/4.0 = 3/4.0 → R_p = 4.0/3 = 1.33 Ω

Total resistance = 3.0 + 1.33 = 4.33 Ω

Total current I = 6.0/4.33 = 1.385 A (through 3.0 Ω resistor)

P.d. across parallel combination = 1.385 × 1.33 = 1.84 V

Current through 2.0 Ω = 1.84/2.0 = 0.92 A

Current through 4.0 Ω = 1.84/4.0 = 0.46 A

Check: 0.92 + 0.46 = 1.38 A ✓

6. Internal Resistance

Real cells and batteries have internal resistance r. When current flows, some voltage is "lost" across this internal resistance.

E = V + Ir = I(R + r)

where E = e.m.f. (total energy per coulomb), V = terminal p.d. (useful energy per coulomb), I = current, R = external resistance, r = internal resistance.

The terminal p.d. decreases as current increases (V = E − Ir). When a cell is on open circuit (I = 0), the terminal p.d. equals the e.m.f.

📝 Worked Example — Internal Resistance

Question: A cell of e.m.f. 1.5 V and internal resistance 0.50 Ω is connected to a 2.5 Ω resistor. Calculate: (a) the current, (b) the terminal p.d.

Solution:

(a) I = E/(R + r) = 1.5/(2.5 + 0.50) = 1.5/3.0 = 0.50 A

(b) V = IR = 0.50 × 2.5 = 1.25 V (or V = E − Ir = 1.5 − 0.50 × 0.50 = 1.25 V)

7. Sensors

  • Thermistor: A temperature-dependent resistor. For an NTC thermistor, resistance decreases as temperature increases. Used in temperature sensors and fire alarms.
  • LDR (Light Dependent Resistor): Resistance decreases as light intensity increases. Used in light-sensitive switches (e.g., street lighting).

⚠️ Common Misconception

Students often confuse the behaviour of NTC thermistors and LDRs. Remember: for both NTC thermistors and LDRs, resistance decreases when the stimulus (temperature or light) increases. This is why they are often used as the lower resistor in a potential divider — increasing the stimulus increases V_out.

🎯 Exam-Style Question

Question: A battery of e.m.f. 9.0 V and internal resistance 1.0 Ω is connected to a resistor of 8.0 Ω. Calculate: (a) the current in the circuit, (b) the power dissipated in the 8.0 Ω resistor, (c) the efficiency of the circuit. [5 marks]

Mark Scheme

• (a) I = E/(R + r) = 9.0/(8.0 + 1.0) = 1.0 A (1 mark)

• (b) P = I²R = 1.0² × 8.0 = 8.0 W (1 mark)

• (c) Total power = EI = 9.0 × 1.0 = 9.0 W (1 mark)

• Efficiency = (8.0/9.0) × 100 = 89% (accept 88.9%) (2 marks — 1 method, 1 answer)

🎯 Exam-Style Question

Question: In the potential divider circuit shown, R₁ = 2.0 kΩ and R₂ is an NTC thermistor. At 20°C, R₂ = 4.0 kΩ. The supply voltage is 12 V. Calculate: (a) the output voltage at 20°C, (b) explain what happens to the output voltage as the temperature increases. [4 marks]

Mark Scheme

• (a) V_out = 12 × 4000/(2000 + 4000) = 12 × 4000/6000 = 8.0 V (1 mark)

• (b) As temperature increases, R₂ decreases (1 mark)

• R₂/(R₁ + R₂) decreases (1 mark)

• Therefore V_out decreases (1 mark)

Last-Minute Summary

  • Series: I same, V adds, R_total = R₁+R₂+R₃...
  • Parallel: V same, I adds, 1/R_total = 1/R₁+1/R₂+1/R₃...
  • Potential divider: V_out = V_in × R₂/(R₁+R₂)
  • Kirchhoff 1st: ΣI_in = ΣI_out at junction (conservation of charge)
  • Kirchhoff 2nd: Σe.m.f. = Σp.d. around loop (conservation of energy)
  • E = V + Ir = I(R+r)
  • Thermistor (NTC): R↓ as T↑; LDR: R↓ as light↑

Exam Tips

  • Always redraw complex circuits to identify series and parallel sections clearly.
  • For parallel resistors, the product-over-sum formula (R₁R₂/(R₁+R₂)) only works for two resistors.
  • When using Kirchhoff's laws, define current directions before writing equations. If your answer is negative, the actual direction is opposite.
  • Remember that ammeters have very low resistance and are connected in series; voltmeters have very high resistance and are connected in parallel.
  • For internal resistance: open circuit → V = E; short circuit → I = E/r.
  • In potential divider sensor circuits, state clearly whether R increases or decreases with the stimulus.
