Britain, 1918–1951

Cambridge 9389 A-Level / 0470 IGCSE

📘 IGCSE Content

Post-War Britain and the Lloyd George Coalition (1918–22)

  • "Khaki election" (Dec 1918): Lloyd George's coalition won landslide on promise to "make Germany pay."
  • Treaty of Versailles: Britain gained mandates (Palestine, Iraq, Tanganyika), German colonies, and reparations.
  • Domestic reforms: Fisher Education Act (1918) raised school leaving age to 14; Addison Act (1919) began council housing.
  • Economic problems: return to gold standard (1925) at pre-war rate (£1 = $4.86) hurt exports. Unemployment remained high in staple industries (coal, textiles, shipbuilding).
  • Anglo-Irish Treaty (Dec 1921): Irish Free State created (26 counties); Northern Ireland (6 counties) remained in UK. Civil War in Ireland (1922–23).
  • Lloyd George fell (Oct 1922): Conservative backbenchers (Carlton Club meeting) withdrew support.

The Interwar Period: Conservative Dominance (1922–29)

  • Bonar Law (1922–23); Baldwin (1923, 1924–29, 1935–37); MacDonald (Labour, 1924, 1929–31, 1931–35 in National Government).
  • General Strike (1926): miners' dispute over wages and hours. TUC called general strike (4–12 May). Government prepared with volunteers and propaganda (BBC). Strike collapsed; Trade Disputes Act (1927) restricted sympathy strikes.
  • Coal miners locked out until Nov 1926; many accepted worse terms.
  • "Flapper election" (1929): Labour became largest party for first time. Baldwin's "Safety First" campaign failed.

The Great Depression and the National Government (1931–39)

  • Wall Street Crash (1929) hit Britain hard. Gold standard abandoned (Sept 1931). MacDonald's Labour government split over cuts to unemployment benefits.
  • National Government (Aug 1931): MacDonald led coalition with Conservatives and Liberals. "Doctor's Mandate" election (Oct 1931): massive National Government victory (554 seats).
  • Economy: south and Midlands recovered (new industries: cars, chemicals, electronics); north and Wales suffered ("Special Areas"). Jarrow March (1936): 200 unemployed men marched to London.
  • Abdication crisis (1936): Edward VIII wanted to marry Wallis Simpson (divorced American). Baldwin and government opposed; Edward abdicated (Dec 1936); George VI succeeded.
  • Foreign policy: appeasement. Rearmament began slowly (1935) but accelerated after Munich (1938). Conscription introduced (April 1939).

World War II (1939–45)

  • Phoney War (Sept 1939–May 1940); Churchill became PM (10 May 1940) after Norway disaster.
  • Dunkirk (May–June 1940); Battle of Britain (July–Oct 1940); Blitz (Sept 1940–May 1941).
  • North Africa: El Alamein (Nov 1942) — Montgomery defeated Rommel. Turning point in British war.
  • D-Day (June 1944); VE Day (8 May 1945).
  • Home front: rationing (from Jan 1940), evacuation, conscription of women, Bevin Boys (mines), Land Army.
  • Beveridge Report (Dec 1942): proposed welfare state — attack on "five giants" (want, disease, ignorance, squalor, idleness).

The Attlee Government (1945–51)

  • Labour landslide (July 1945): 393 seats. Mandate for radical change.
  • Nationalisation: Bank of England (1946), coal (1947), railways and canals (1948), steel (1951 — reversed by Conservatives).
  • NHS (1948): Aneurin Bevan. Free at point of use. Doctors initially opposed (BMA).
  • Welfare state: National Insurance Act (1946); National Assistance Act (1948); Education Act (1944, implemented by Labour) — Butler Act, tripartite system (grammar, technical, secondary modern), free secondary education, school leaving age raised to 15.
  • India and Pakistan independent (1947); Burma and Ceylon (1948); end of British Mandate in Palestine (1948).
  • Marshall Aid (1948): Britain received largest share. Austerity continued; bread rationing (1946–48).
  • Korea (1950–53): Britain contributed troops.
  • 1951 election: Conservatives won. Churchill returned to office (Oct 1951).

📗 AS Level Content

The Decline of the Liberal Party and Rise of Labour

  • Liberal split (1916): Lloyd George vs. Asquith. Never reunited. Lloyd George's "coupon election" (1918) associated Liberals with Conservatives.
  • Labour growth: trade union expansion (from 4 million 1914 to 8 million 1920); 1918 constitution (Clause IV committed to common ownership); individual membership.
  • 1924: first Labour government (minority). MacDonald as PM. Not radical — focused on housing and unemployment. Zinoviev Letter (Oct 1924) probably forged; contributed to Labour defeat.
  • By 1931 Labour had replaced Liberals as main opposition.

Depression and the National Government

  • Was the National Government a betrayal of Labour? MacDonald and Snowden accepted cuts; most Labour MPs opposed. Expelled from party.
  • Economic policy: orthodox (balanced budgets, deflation). Abandoned gold standard; protective tariffs (Ottawa Conference, 1932); cheap money (low interest rates).
  • Recovery was patchy: new industries in Midlands/south; old staples in north declined. "Two Britains." Government's "Special Areas" Act (1934) was limited.
  • Political consensus: no party proposed radical alternatives. Labour moved left (Socialism and Peace, 1934) but remained out of power.

Appeasement: Why and With What Results?

  • Motives: guilt over Versailles; fear of another war; belief Hitler had legitimate grievances; military unpreparedness; economic constraints; focus on empire; pacifist public opinion.
  • Munich (Sept 1938): Chamberlain returned with "peace for our time." Critics (Churchill, Duff Cooper, Eden) argued it was dishonourable and encouraged Hitler.
  • Rearmament: actually began in 1935 (Defence White Paper) and accelerated 1938–39. By 1939 Britain was spending heavily on air force and radar.
  • Guarantee to Poland (March 1939): marked end of appeasement. Conscription (April 1939).

War and Social Change

  • "People's War" thesis: war broke down class barriers; created expectations of welfare state; Labour's 1945 victory reflected desire for change.
  • Evidence: evacuation exposed middle classes to poverty; bombing was egalitarian; common sacrifice; full employment.
  • Counter: class differences persisted; middle-class evacuees often returned; rationing was accepted but not enjoyed; Churchill still popular in 1945 but voters wanted domestic reform.

📙 A2 Level Content

Consensus Politics: Was There a Post-War Settlement?

  • Butler-Adler thesis (Paul Addison): wartime coalition created cross-party consensus on welfare state, full employment, and mixed economy. Both parties accepted Keynesianism and Beveridge.
  • Critics: consensus exaggerated. Labour was more radical than Conservatives. Conservatives accepted welfare state under pressure (1945 defeat). Differences remained over nationalisation, trade unions, and taxation.
  • Thatcherite critique (1970s–80s): consensus was a mistake — stifled enterprise, created dependency, led to decline.

The British Empire and Decline

  • 1918–39: Empire at greatest territorial extent. But Dominion status (Statute of Westminster, 1931) weakened control. India Act (1935) granted limited self-government.
  • Post-1945: rapid decolonisation. India (1947) most important. Reasons: Labour's anti-imperialism, economic weakness, Indian nationalism (Congress, Muslim League), WWII discredited empire.
  • Debate: was decolonisation planned retreat or forced surrender? Some argue Britain managed orderly withdrawal; others that it was driven by nationalist pressure and economic incapacity.

Comparing Interwar Britain with Other Democracies

  • Britain avoided fascism and communism. Why? Stable parliamentary tradition; gradual reform; Labour as constitutional party; no military defeat or economic catastrophe on German scale.
  • But: Mosley's BUF (1932) attracted some support; Cable Street (1936) demonstrated anti-fascist opposition. BBC and press were largely anti-fascist.
  • Comparison with France (Popular Front, instability) and USA (New Deal): Britain's response was more conservative, less radical.

Essay Frameworks

  • "The General Strike was a defeat for the trade unions": TUC called off strike; miners lost; Trade Disputes Act. Counter: demonstrated solidarity; government overestimated trade union threat; unions survived.
  • "Appeasement was the only realistic policy": Military unpreparedness, economic constraints, public opinion. Counter: encouraged Hitler, sacrificed Czechoslovakia, moral failure.
  • "The Attlee government transformed Britain": Welfare state, NHS, nationalisation, decolonisation. Counter: many wartime plans already made; Conservatives would have implemented similar reforms; economic constraints limited achievement.

📜 Worked Example

Question: To what extent was Germany responsible for WWI?

Essay Plan:
FOR: Blank cheque, Schlieffen Plan, naval expansion, Fischer thesis
AGAINST: Austrian ultimatum, Russian mobilisation, alliance system, Clark "sleepwalkers"
Conclusion: Significant responsibility but not sole blame
Writing narrative instead of analysis. History essays need CAUSATION — explain WHY and SIGNIFICANCE.
Always use SPECIFIC EVIDENCE — dates, names, statistics. "On 28 June 1914" not "in 1914".

Source Analysis Framework

  • Content: What does the source say? What information does it provide?
  • Provenance: Who wrote it? When? Why? What is their perspective?
  • Reliability: Is it trustworthy? What biases might exist?
  • Utility: How useful is it for a specific enquiry?

Essay Structure

  • Introduction: Define key terms, set out argument/line of reasoning
  • Paragraphs: Point → Evidence → Explanation → Link back to question
  • Synthesis: Compare factors, weigh importance, show interconnections
  • Conclusion: Judgment based on evidence, avoid introducing new points
Always answer the EXACT question asked. Underline key words ("To what extent", "How far", "Most important") and address them directly.
