Gas Exchange & Respiration

9700 (A-Level) · 0610 (IGCSE)

IGCSE Content

1

Gas Exchange Surfaces

All gas exchange surfaces share common features that maximise diffusion efficiency:

  • Large surface area — maximises area for diffusion.
  • Thin barrier (one cell thick) — short diffusion distance.
  • Good blood supply — maintains concentration gradient by removing absorbed gases.
  • Moist surface — gases must dissolve before diffusing.

Examples of Gas Exchange Surfaces

OrganismGas Exchange SurfaceKey Adaptations
HumansAlveoli in lungsMillions of alveoli (~70 m² surface area); walls one cell thick; surrounded by capillaries
FishGillsFilaments with lamellae; countercurrent flow maintains steep gradient along entire length
InsectsTracheal systemSpiracles → tracheae → tracheoles; air moves directly to cells; no blood transport needed for gases
PlantsStomata on leavesGuard cells control opening; gases diffuse through air spaces in spongy mesophyll
Countercurrent flow in fish gills is highly efficient because blood flows in the opposite direction to water, maintaining a steep concentration gradient along the entire length of the gill filament. This allows fish to extract up to 80% of dissolved oxygen from water.
2

Human Breathing Mechanism

Inspiration (Inhalation)

  • External intercostal muscles contract, ribs move up and out.
  • Diaphragm contracts and flattens.
  • Thoracic volume increases, pressure decreases below atmospheric.
  • Air rushes in down the pressure gradient.

Expiration (Exhalation)

  • External intercostals relax, ribs move down and in.
  • Diaphragm relaxes and domes up.
  • Thoracic volume decreases, pressure increases above atmospheric.
  • Air forced out down the pressure gradient.
Breathing is an active process during inspiration (muscles contract) but largely passive during expiration at rest (muscles relax, elastic recoil of lungs). During forced expiration, internal intercostal muscles and abdominal muscles contract.
3

Respiration Overview

Respiration: The release of energy from glucose (or other organic molecules) in living cells. It occurs in all living cells and is NOT the same as breathing (ventilation).
  • Aerobic respiration: Requires oxygen. Complete oxidation of glucose.
    C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ATP (≈ 30-32 ATP per glucose)
  • Anaerobic respiration in yeast: Fermentation. Glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide + little energy (2 ATP).
    C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ + 2 ATP
  • Anaerobic respiration in muscles: Glucose → lactic acid + little energy (2 ATP).
    C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₃H₆O₃ + 2 ATP
Common Misconception: Breathing is NOT respiration. Breathing (ventilation) is the mechanical process of moving air in and out of lungs. Respiration is the biochemical process of releasing energy from glucose inside cells.

AS Level Content

4

Aerobic Respiration — Glycolysis

  • Occurs in cytoplasm. Does not require oxygen.
  • Glucose (6C) is phosphorylated using 2 ATP → glucose-6-phosphate → fructose-1,6-bisphosphate.
  • Split into two 3-carbon molecules (triose phosphate / GALP).
  • Each triose phosphate is oxidised, producing 2 ATP (substrate-level phosphorylation) and 2 NADH.
  • Net gain: 2 ATP, 2 NADH, 2 pyruvate per glucose.
Glycolysis is the first stage of both aerobic and anaerobic respiration. It occurs in the cytoplasm and does not require oxygen. The net gain is 2 ATP per glucose.
5

Link Reaction

  • In mitochondrial matrix.
  • Pyruvate (3C) is decarboxylated (CO₂ removed) and dehydrogenated (NAD accepts H), forming acetyl group.
  • Acetyl group combines with coenzyme A → acetyl CoA (2C).
  • 2 pyruvate → 2 acetyl CoA + 2 CO₂ + 2 NADH.
6

Krebs Cycle (Citric Acid Cycle)

  • In mitochondrial matrix.
  • Acetyl CoA (2C) combines with oxaloacetate (4C) → citrate (6C).
  • Series of reactions: decarboxylation (2 CO₂ released), dehydrogenation (3 NADH and 1 FADH₂), substrate-level phosphorylation (1 ATP or GTP).
  • Per glucose: 2 turns → 4 CO₂, 6 NADH, 2 FADH₂, 2 ATP.
The Krebs cycle completes the oxidation of glucose. All carbon from glucose is released as CO₂. The energy is captured in NADH and FADH₂, which feed into the electron transport chain.
7

Oxidative Phosphorylation

The Electron Transport Chain and Chemiosmosis
  1. NADH and FADH₂ donate electrons to the electron transport chain (ETC) on the inner mitochondrial membrane (cristae).
  2. Electrons pass through carriers (NADH dehydrogenase, cytochrome complexes, cytochrome oxidase) releasing energy.
  3. Energy pumps protons (H⁺) from the matrix into the intermembrane space, creating an electrochemical gradient (proton-motive force).
  4. Protons flow back into the matrix through ATP synthase (chemiosmosis), driving ATP synthesis from ADP + Pi.
  5. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor, combining with electrons and H⁺ to form water.
  6. Per glucose: ~28-34 ATP from oxidative phosphorylation.
Oxygen is essential as the final electron acceptor. Without oxygen, the ETC backs up, NADH cannot be reoxidised, and oxidative phosphorylation stops. This is why aerobic respiration yields far more ATP than anaerobic.
Worked Example: ATP Yield from Respiration

Question: Calculate the total ATP yield from one molecule of glucose in aerobic respiration. [3 marks]

Answer:

Glycolysis: 2 ATP (net) + 2 NADH → ~5 ATP
Link reaction: 2 NADH → ~5 ATP
Krebs cycle: 2 ATP + 6 NADH + 2 FADH₂ → ~2 + 15 + 3 = ~20 ATP
Total: approximately 30-32 ATP per glucose
8

Anaerobic Respiration

  • In mammals: Pyruvate is reduced by NADH → lactate, catalysed by lactate dehydrogenase. NAD⁺ is regenerated so glycolysis can continue. Lactate is transported to the liver for the Cori cycle (converted back to glucose).
    Pyruvate + NADH + H⁺ → Lactate + NAD⁺
  • In yeast: Pyruvate is decarboxylated → ethanal; ethanal is reduced by NADH → ethanol. CO₂ is released. Used in brewing and baking.
    Pyruvate → Ethanal + CO₂
    Ethanal + NADH + H⁺ → Ethanol + NAD⁺
The purpose of anaerobic respiration is to regenerate NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue producing ATP. Without NAD⁺ regeneration, glycolysis would stop and no ATP would be produced.

A2 Level Content

Respiratory Substrates

  • Carbohydrates: Respiratory quotient (RQ) = CO₂ produced / O₂ consumed = 1.0 for glucose.
  • Lipids: More reduced; more H per C. RQ ≈ 0.7. Yield more ATP per gram but require more O₂.
  • Proteins: Deaminated first (amino group removed as urea). RQ ≈ 0.9. Used when carbohydrate and lipid stores depleted.
RQ = CO₂ produced / O₂ consumed
Carbohydrate (glucose): RQ = 6/6 = 1.0
Lipid (tripalmitin): RQ ≈ 0.7
Protein: RQ ≈ 0.9
10

Mitochondrial Structure & Function

  • Outer membrane: Permeable to small molecules (contains porins).
  • Inner membrane: Impermeable; contains ETC complexes and ATP synthase. Folded into cristae to increase surface area for oxidative phosphorylation.
  • Matrix: Contains enzymes for link reaction and Krebs cycle, mitochondrial DNA, 70S ribosomes.
  • Intermembrane space: Site of proton accumulation during ETC.
Mitochondria have their own DNA (circular, like prokaryotes) and 70S ribosomes, supporting the endosymbiotic theory that mitochondria evolved from ancient prokaryotes.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Gas exchange: large SA, thin barrier, moist, good blood supply. Alveoli, gills (countercurrent), tracheae, stomata.
  • Breathing: inspiration (diaphragm contracts, intercostals contract, volume up, pressure down); expiration (reverse).
  • Glycolysis: cytoplasm; glucose → 2 pyruvate + 2 ATP + 2 NADH.
  • Link reaction: mitochondrial matrix; pyruvate → acetyl CoA + CO₂ + NADH.
  • Krebs cycle: matrix; acetyl CoA → CO₂ + NADH + FADH₂ + ATP.
  • Oxidative phosphorylation: cristae; ETC pumps H⁺; chemiosmosis through ATP synthase; O₂ final electron acceptor.
  • Anaerobic: mammals → lactate; yeast → ethanol + CO₂. Purpose: regenerate NAD⁺ for glycolysis.
  • RQ values: carbohydrate = 1.0; lipid = 0.7; protein = 0.9.

💡 Exam Tips

  • Always state where each stage occurs: glycolysis = cytoplasm; link + Krebs = mitochondrial matrix; oxidative phosphorylation = inner membrane (cristae).
  • In ETC questions, explain chemiosmosis: proton gradient → proton motive force → ATP synthase.
  • For anaerobic, emphasise NAD⁺ regeneration is essential.
  • Countercurrent flow in fish: explain why it's efficient (maintains gradient along entire length).
  • Remember RQ values: carbohydrate = 1.0, lipid = 0.7. A respirometer experiment with RQ < 1 indicates lipid is being respired.
  • Mitochondria: cristae increase surface area for ETC and ATP synthase.
