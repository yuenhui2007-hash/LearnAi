📘 Subject Context

Understanding Cell Division is fundamental to IGCSE Biology. This topic frequently appears in both Paper 2 (Multiple Choice) and Paper 4 (Theory), so mastering the details is essential for achieving a high grade.

In IGCSE Biology examinations, you may be asked to recall facts, explain processes, interpret diagrams, or apply knowledge to unfamiliar contexts. Always use precise biological terminology — marks are awarded for specific key words.

Cell Division

0610 (IGCSE)

Detailed Notes

1. Mitosis

Prophase → Metaphase → Anaphase → Telophase → Cytokinesis. Produces 2 identical diploid cells.

2. Meiosis

Two divisions. Produces 4 genetically different haploid cells. Crossing over in prophase I. Independent assortment in metaphase I.

3. Binary Fission

Prokaryotic division. DNA replicates, cell divides.

Experimental Design

  • Variables: Independent (what you change), dependent (what you measure), controlled (kept constant)
  • Validity: Does the experiment test what it claims to test?
  • Reliability: Repeatable results — repeat experiments, calculate means
  • Accuracy: Close to true value — use appropriate precision instruments

Microscopy Techniques

  • Light microscope: Max magnification ~×1500, resolution ~200 nm
  • Electron microscope: TEM (internal structure) vs SEM (surface detail)
  • Staining: Methylene blue (nuclei), iodine (starch), eosin (cytoplasm)
  • Cell fractionation: Homogenisation → filtration → ultracentrifugation
Resolution is the ability to distinguish two separate points. Electron microscopes have much higher resolution than light microscopes because electrons have shorter wavelengths than visible light.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Forgetting to mention the role of ATP in active processes — always check if energy is required.
  • Saying "the cell wall controls what enters and leaves" — it is the cell membrane that controls entry and exit; the cell wall provides structural support.
  • Forgetting that bacteria are prokaryotes and lack a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.
  • Stating that enzymes are "killed" by high temperature — enzymes are denatured, not killed (they are not living).
  • Confusing osmosis with diffusion — osmosis is specifically the diffusion of water molecules across a partially permeable membrane.

✅ Key Points to Remember

  • Use precise terminology — examiners award marks for specific words like "denatured", "partially permeable", and "semi-conservative".
  • Draw diagrams clearly with labels — even in theory papers, sketching can help structure your answer.
  • Know the stages of the nitrogen cycle and carbon cycle with specific organisms involved.
  • Memorise the balanced equation for photosynthesis and aerobic respiration.
  • Understand the concept of limiting factors and be able to apply it to photosynthesis.

Answering "Explain" Questions

In biology, "explain" questions require you to give a reason or mechanism. A good structure is:

Observation → Because → Mechanism → Therefore → Consequence

Example: "Explain why enzymes are denatured by high temperature."

Enzymes are denatured by high temperature because the increased kinetic energy causes vibrations that break the hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds maintaining the tertiary structure. This means that the active site changes shape and no longer fits the substrate. Therefore, the enzyme cannot catalyse the reaction.

Always use precise biological terminology in your answers. Words like "denatured", "partially permeable", "semi-conservative", and "complementary" earn specific marks.

Worked Example 1: Calculating Magnification

Problem

A micrograph shows a chloroplast with a measured width of 45 mm. The actual width is 3.0 μm. Calculate the magnification.

Solution

Magnification = image size / actual size

Convert to same units: 45 mm = 45,000 μm

Magnification = 45,000 / 3.0 = ×15,000

Worked Example 2: Explaining Adaptations

Problem

Explain how red blood cells are adapted for transporting oxygen.

Solution
  1. Biconcave shape: Increases surface area to volume ratio for faster diffusion of oxygen.
  2. No nucleus: More space for haemoglobin, increasing oxygen-carrying capacity.
  3. Contains haemoglobin: Iron-containing protein that reversibly binds oxygen.
  4. Thin membrane: Short diffusion distance for oxygen to enter and exit.
  5. Flexible: Can squeeze through narrow capillaries to reach all tissues.

Worked Example 3: Interpreting Experimental Data

Problem

An experiment investigated the effect of temperature on enzyme activity. The results showed activity increasing from 20°C to 37°C, then decreasing sharply above 45°C. Explain these results.

Solution

20°C to 37°C: As temperature increases, kinetic energy of enzyme and substrate molecules increases. More frequent successful collisions occur between enzyme active sites and substrate molecules, increasing the rate of reaction.

Above 45°C: The high temperature causes vibrations that break hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds maintaining the enzyme's tertiary structure. The active site changes shape (denaturation), so substrate molecules no longer fit. The reaction rate decreases sharply.

Practice Questions

Question 1

A student observed a palisade mesophyll cell with a measured length of 35 mm in a micrograph. The actual length is 70 μm. Calculate the magnification and explain two ways palisade cells are adapted for photosynthesis.

Answer: ×500. Adaptations: many chloroplasts, cylindrical shape/columnar arrangement for maximum light absorption, large vacuole pushes cytoplasm to edge for shorter diffusion path.

Question 2

Explain why the rate of photosynthesis increases as light intensity increases up to a certain point, then levels off.

Answer: More light energy excites more chlorophyll molecules, producing more ATP and NADPH for the Calvin cycle. At saturation point, another factor (CO₂ or temperature) becomes limiting.

Question 3

Describe and explain the role of the following in protein synthesis: (a) mRNA, (b) ribosomes, (c) tRNA.

Answer: (a) mRNA carries the genetic code from DNA to ribosome. (b) Ribosomes are the site of protein synthesis where amino acids are joined. (c) tRNA brings specific amino acids to the ribosome and has anticodons that pair with mRNA codons.

🎯 Examination Tips

  • When comparing, always mention both items — e.g., "X is... whereas Y is...".
  • Plan extended response questions before writing — a brief bullet plan helps structure your answer.
  • In data analysis questions, quote actual figures from the graph or table.
  • Check that your answer matches the number of marks available — 2 marks usually need 2 distinct points.
  • For definition questions, give a complete, precise definition — half-statements may not earn full marks.

🔬 Worked Example

Question: Explain how the structure of the small intestine is adapted for absorption.

Answer:
1. Villi: Finger-like projections increase surface area
2. Microvilli: Further increase surface area on epithelial cells
3. Thin walls: Single cell layer reduces diffusion distance
4. Rich blood supply: Maintains concentration gradient
5. Lacteals: Absorb fatty acids and glycerol
Confusing absorption (taking nutrients into blood) with assimilation (incorporating nutrients into cells/tissues).
Surface area to volume ratio is crucial — as organisms get larger, SA:V decreases, requiring specialised exchange surfaces.

Quick Recall Flashcards

⚡ Memorise These
  • Prokaryotes: No nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles, 70S ribosomes, peptidoglycan cell wall
  • Eukaryotes: True nucleus, membrane-bound organelles, 80S ribosomes
  • Mitochondria: Double membrane, cristae, ATP production, aerobic respiration
  • Chloroplasts: Thylakoids, grana, stroma, photosynthesis
  • RER: Protein synthesis; SER: Lipid synthesis
  • Golgi: Modifies, packages, transports proteins
  • Lysosomes: Hydrolytic enzymes, digestion, autophagy
  • Cell fractionation: Homogenise → filter → ultracentrifuge (low → medium → high speed)

Examiner's Top Tips for This Topic

🎓 From the Mark Scheme
  • Always link structure to function — "the mitochondria have folded cristae BECAUSE this increases surface area for ATP production."
  • Use precise terminology: "partially permeable" not "has holes", "denatured" not "killed".
  • For comparisons, always mention BOTH items explicitly — e.g., "prokaryotes have 70S ribosomes WHILE eukaryotes have 80S ribosomes."
  • When describing organelles, mention at least TWO structural features and their functions.
  • In cell fractionation, explain WHY cold and isotonic conditions are used.

Additional Practice with Mark Schemes

Question 4 [4 marks]

A student observed a cell under a microscope. The cell had a cell wall, chloroplasts, and a large central vacuole.

(a) Identify the type of cell. [1]

(b) Name two structures present in this cell that are not present in animal cells and explain their functions. [3]

Mark Scheme:

(a) Plant cell [1]

(b) Any two from:

  • Cell wall [½] — provides support and maintains cell shape [½]
  • Chloroplasts [½] — site of photosynthesis [½]
  • Large vacuole [½] — maintains turgor pressure / stores water and ions [½]
Question 5 [3 marks]

Explain why mitochondria are more numerous in muscle cells than in skin cells.

Mark Scheme:

Muscle cells require more energy/ATP [1]

Mitochondria are the site of aerobic respiration/ATP production [1]

Muscle cells contract frequently / have high metabolic activity [1]
