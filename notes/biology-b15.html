Immunity (A2)

9700 (A-Level) · A2 Only

IGCSE Content

At IGCSE, immunity is introduced as the body's defence against pathogens:

  • Pathogens: Disease-causing organisms (bacteria, viruses, fungi, protists).
  • Physical barriers: Skin, mucous membranes, cilia, stomach acid, tears (lysozyme).
  • Phagocytosis: White blood cells (phagocytes) engulf and digest pathogens.
  • Antibodies: Produced by lymphocytes; specific to particular antigens on pathogens.
  • Memory cells: Remain after infection; enable rapid response on re-exposure.
  • Active immunity: Body makes own antibodies (natural infection or vaccination).
  • Passive immunity: Receive ready-made antibodies (maternal antibodies across placenta, in breast milk, or antivenom).

AS Level Content

At AS, immunity is covered in the context of disease and health:

  • Antigens: Molecules (usually proteins or glycoproteins) on cell surfaces that trigger immune response. Self antigens = body's own; non-self = foreign.
  • Phagocytes: Neutrophils (short-lived, abundant, engulf bacteria) and macrophages (in tissues, also antigen-presenting cells).
  • Antibody structure: Y-shaped; two heavy chains, two light chains. Variable regions bind specific antigen. Constant region determines class (IgG, IgM, IgA, etc.).
  • Primary and secondary response: Primary = slower, smaller peak; secondary = faster, larger, longer-lasting due to memory B and T cells.
  • Vaccination: Stimulates primary response without disease. Uses attenuated pathogens, killed pathogens, toxoids, or subunit vaccines. Herd immunity protects unvaccinated individuals.

A2 Level Content

1

Innate Immunity

Innate immunity: Non-specific defence mechanisms present from birth that provide immediate protection against pathogens. No immunological memory.

Physical and Chemical Barriers

  • Skin: Keratinised outer layer; antimicrobial peptides (defensins).
  • Mucous membranes: Trap pathogens; mucus contains lysozyme.
  • Ciliated epithelium: Mucociliary escalator moves trapped particles out of respiratory tract.
  • Stomach acid: pH 1-2 kills most ingested pathogens.
  • Lysozyme: In tears, saliva, and sweat — breaks down bacterial cell walls.

Cellular Defences

  • Phagocytosis: Neutrophils and macrophages engulf pathogens into phagosomes, which fuse with lysosomes for digestion.
  • Natural killer (NK) cells: Destroy virus-infected and tumour cells by inducing apoptosis.

Inflammation

  • Damaged cells release histamine → vasodilation and increased capillary permeability.
  • Redness, heat, swelling, pain.
  • Complement proteins attract phagocytes and form membrane attack complexes (MAC) to lyse pathogens.
2

Adaptive (Acquired) Immunity

Adaptive immunity: Specific defence mechanisms involving recognition of antigens and immunological memory. Slower to develop but highly specific and long-lasting.

Cell-Mediated Immunity (T Cells)

  • T helper cells (CD4⁺): Activated by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) displaying antigen on MHC II. Release cytokines to activate B cells, cytotoxic T cells, and macrophages.
  • Cytotoxic T cells (CD8⁺): Recognise antigen presented on MHC I of infected body cells. Release perforin (forms pores) and granzymes (induce apoptosis).
  • Memory T cells: Long-lived; rapid response on re-exposure.

Humoral Immunity (B Cells)

  • B cells have surface antibodies (B cell receptors) that bind specific antigen.
  • Activated by antigen binding + T helper cell cytokines (T-dependent) or directly by repeating antigens (T-independent).
  • Clonal expansion: Rapid division producing plasma cells and memory B cells.
  • Plasma cells secrete large amounts of antibody.
  • Memory B cells provide faster, stronger secondary response.
3

Antibody Structure & Function

  • Four polypeptide chains: 2 heavy, 2 light. Disulfide bonds hold together.
  • Variable region at tips of Y: antigen-binding site. Unique for each B cell clone.
  • Constant region: determines class and effector function.
  • Functions:
    • Neutralisation: Block pathogen binding to host cells.
    • Opsonisation: Mark pathogens for phagocytosis.
    • Agglutination: Clump pathogens together.
    • Complement activation: Trigger lysis of pathogens.
4

Antigen Presentation

  • Macrophages and dendritic cells (APCs) engulf pathogens, process antigens, and display them on MHC molecules.
  • MHC I: On all nucleated cells; displays endogenous antigens (from inside cell, e.g., viral proteins) to cytotoxic T cells.
  • MHC II: On APCs; displays exogenous antigens (from outside cell) to helper T cells.
5

Autoimmune Diseases

  • Immune system attacks body's own cells.
  • Type 1 diabetes: T cells destroy pancreatic β cells.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis: Antibodies attack joint tissues.
  • Multiple sclerosis: Immune attack on myelin sheath.
  • Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE): Antibodies against nuclear antigens.
6

Allergy (Hypersensitivity)

  • Type I hypersensitivity: IgE antibodies bind mast cells and basophils.
  • On re-exposure to allergen, cross-linking triggers histamine release → inflammation, vasodilation, bronchoconstriction, mucus secretion.
  • Can lead to anaphylaxis — systemic, life-threatening reaction treated with adrenaline (epinephrine).
7

Monoclonal Antibodies

  • Produced by hybridoma cells: B cells fused with myeloma (cancer) cells → immortal antibody-producing cell line.
  • Uses: Diagnostic tests (pregnancy tests, pathogen detection), cancer treatment (targeted delivery of drugs/radiation to tumour cells), transplant rejection prevention, autoimmune disease treatment.
  • Advantages: highly specific, can be produced in large quantities, consistent quality.
🔬 Real-World Application: Monoclonal Antibodies in Cancer Therapy

Monoclonal antibodies such as trastuzumab (Herceptin) target HER2 receptors on breast cancer cells. Rituximab targets CD20 on B-cell lymphomas. These "magic bullets" deliver chemotherapy directly to cancer cells, sparing healthy tissue. CAR-T cell therapy (genetically modified T cells with chimeric antigen receptors) represents the next frontier in targeted immunotherapy.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Innate immunity: non-specific, no memory. Barriers, phagocytosis, inflammation, complement, NK cells.
  • Adaptive immunity: specific, has memory. Cell-mediated (T cells) and humoral (B cells/antibodies).
  • T helper cells (CD4⁺): activated by MHC II; release cytokines. Cytotoxic T cells (CD8⁺): kill infected cells via MHC I.
  • B cells: antigen binding → clonal expansion → plasma cells (antibody secretion) + memory B cells.
  • Antibodies: Y-shaped; variable region binds antigen; functions = neutralisation, opsonisation, agglutination, complement activation.
  • Primary response slower; secondary response faster, larger, longer due to memory cells.
  • Autoimmune: immune system attacks self. Allergy: IgE-mediated histamine release.
  • Monoclonal antibodies: hybridoma technology; diagnostic and therapeutic uses.
  • Vaccines: live attenuated, killed, subunit, mRNA. Herd immunity protects unvaccinated.

💡 Exam Tips

  • Distinguish clearly between innate and adaptive immunity.
  • In cell-mediated response, always mention MHC molecules and cytokines.
  • For monoclonal antibodies, explain hybridoma formation.
  • Draw antibody structure labelling variable and constant regions.
  • In vaccine questions, compare live vs killed vs subunit in terms of safety and efficacy.
  • Primary response: slow, IgM first. Secondary response: fast, IgG dominant, memory cells involved.
