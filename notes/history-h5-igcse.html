📘 Subject Context

Civil Rights in the USA, 1863–1968 is a significant area of study in IGCSE History. Understanding the causes, events, and consequences of historical developments is essential for constructing analytical essays and source-based answers.

IGCSE History examines knowledge, source analysis, and essay-writing skills. Paper 1 tests depth studies through structured questions; Paper 2 tests breadth studies through source analysis and essays. Always use specific evidence.

Civil Rights in the USA, 1863–1968

history-h5

📘 IGCSE Content

Reconstruction (1865–77)

  • 13th Amendment (1865): Abolished slavery
  • 14th Amendment (1868): Citizenship and equal protection
  • 15th Amendment (1870): Voting rights regardless of race
  • Failure: Jim Crow laws, sharecropping, KKK violence

Jim Crow Era (1877–1954)

  • Plessy v Ferguson (1896): "Separate but equal" justified segregation
  • Disenfranchisement: Poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses
  • Economic discrimination: Limited jobs, lower wages, segregated facilities

Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955–56)

  • Rosa Parks: Refused to give up seat (1 Dec 1955)
  • Martin Luther King Jr: Emerged as leader; 381-day boycott
  • Result: Supreme Court banned bus segregation (Browder v Gayle)

Key Legislation

  • Civil Rights Act (1964): Banned discrimination in public places and employment
  • Voting Rights Act (1965): Eliminated literacy tests; federal oversight

📗 AS Level Content

Campaign Strategies

  • NAACP: Legal strategy — challenged segregation in courts (Brown v Board 1954)
  • SCLC: Non-violent direct action; moral persuasion
  • SNCC: Student activism; Freedom Rides (1961), Freedom Summer (1964)
  • Nation of Islam: Black separatism; Malcolm X until 1965

Opposition & Violence

  • White Citizens' Councils: Economic boycott of activists
  • KKK: Bombings, murders, intimidation
  • Police brutality: Birmingham (1963) — attacked with dogs and fire hoses

📙 A2 Level Content

Evaluation of Success

  • Political: Voting rights secured; Black elected officials increased
  • Economic: Limited progress — wealth gap persisted
  • Social: De jure segregation ended; de facto segregation remained
  • Black Power: Stokely Carmichael, Black Panthers — shift from integration to empowerment

Comparative Analysis

  • Comparison with other civil rights movements globally
  • Role of federal government vs grassroots activism

📜 Worked Example

Question: To what extent was Germany responsible for WWI?

Essay Plan:
FOR: Blank cheque, Schlieffen Plan, naval expansion, Fischer thesis
AGAINST: Austrian ultimatum, Russian mobilisation, alliance system, Clark "sleepwalkers"
Conclusion: Significant responsibility but not sole blame
Writing narrative instead of analysis. History essays need CAUSATION — explain WHY and SIGNIFICANCE.
Always use SPECIFIC EVIDENCE — dates, names, statistics. "On 28 June 1914" not "in 1914".

⚡ Quick Recall

  • Long-term: Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism
  • Short-term: Assassination of Franz Ferdinand
  • Treaty of Versailles: War guilt, reparations, territorial losses
  • Cold War: Containment, Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan

Source Analysis Framework

  • Content: What does the source say? What information does it provide?
  • Provenance: Who wrote it? When? Why? What is their perspective?
  • Reliability: Is it trustworthy? What biases might exist?
  • Utility: How useful is it for a specific enquiry?

Essay Structure

  • Introduction: Define key terms, set out argument/line of reasoning
  • Paragraphs: Point → Evidence → Explanation → Link back to question
  • Synthesis: Compare factors, weigh importance, show interconnections
  • Conclusion: Judgment based on evidence, avoid introducing new points
Always answer the EXACT question asked. Underline key words ("To what extent", "How far", "Most important") and address them directly.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Forgetting to evaluate sources for reliability and usefulness in Paper 2 questions.
  • Describing a source instead of analysing it — explain what it shows and why it matters.
  • Ignoring the provenance of sources — who wrote it, when, and why? This affects reliability.
  • Forgetting to link back to the question in each paragraph — every point should support your argument.
  • Using vague generalisations instead of specific evidence — always include dates, names, and statistics.

✅ Key Points to Remember

  • Learn to evaluate sources using NOP (Nature, Origin, Purpose) and content analysis.
  • Understand the difference between causes (why it happened) and consequences (what resulted).
  • Practise writing timed essays — time management is crucial in the examination.
  • Memorise key dates, names, and events — specific evidence is essential for high marks.
  • Understand the concept of historical significance — why does this event matter?

🎯 Examination Tips

  • Each paragraph should make one main point, supported by evidence and explanation.
  • For "to what extent" questions, consider evidence for and against before reaching a judgement.
  • Check the number of marks — higher marks require greater depth, analysis, and evaluation.
  • For source questions, always comment on both content and provenance — this is the key to high marks.
  • Use the formula for source evaluation: what it says + what this suggests + how reliable/useful it is + why.
