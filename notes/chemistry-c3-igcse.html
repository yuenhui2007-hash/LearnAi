The Cell Theory: Historical Development and Modern Understanding

The cell theory is one of the foundational principles of biology. It was developed through the work of several scientists over two centuries.

Key Contributors
ScientistContributionYear
Robert HookeFirst observed "cells" in cork using a microscope1665
Anton van LeeuwenhoekObserved living cells (bacteria, sperm, blood cells)1670s
Matthias SchleidenProposed all plants are made of cells1838
Theodor SchwannProposed all animals are made of cells1839
Rudolf VirchowProposed omnis cellula e cellula (all cells from cells)1855
The Three Tenets
  1. All living organisms are composed of one or more cells. This applies from the simplest bacterium to the most complex multicellular organism.
  2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and organisation in organisms. Cells are the smallest unit that can exist independently and carry out all life processes.
  3. All cells arise from pre-existing cells by cell division. This disproved spontaneous generation and explained how organisms grow and reproduce.
Virchow's contribution was crucial because it completed the theory by explaining the origin of cells. Before this, some scientists believed cells could form spontaneously from non-living matter.

Exceptions and Extensions

Modern biology has revealed some nuances:

  • Viruses: Not composed of cells — they are acellular and can only reproduce inside host cells.
  • Striated muscle fibres: Formed from the fusion of many cells, resulting in multinucleate structures.
  • Companion cells: In phloem, some cells lose their nuclei but remain functional.

Ideal Gas Equation

PV = nRT
P = pressure (Pa), V = volume (m³)
n = moles, R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
T = temperature (K)
Worked Example

Find volume of 2 moles of gas at 300 K and 100 kPa.

V = nRT/P = (2 × 8.314 × 300)/100000 = 0.0499 m³ = 49.9 dm³

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using "amount" when you mean "mass" or "concentration" — be precise with chemical terminology.
  • Forgetting that Group 1 metals react vigorously with water, producing hydrogen gas and an alkaline solution.
  • Using the wrong mole ratio from a balanced equation — always check the stoichiometric coefficients.
  • Forgetting to state the state symbols (s, l, g, aq) in chemical equations when required.
  • Forgetting to convert cm³ to dm³ when using n = c × V — divide by 1000.

✅ Key Points to Remember

  • Understand the difference between strong and weak acids in terms of ionisation, not concentration.
  • Practice balancing equations — it becomes automatic with repetition.
  • Be able to calculate percentage yield and atom economy — these are common calculation topics.
  • Know the conditions and catalysts for the Haber Process and Contact Process.
  • Learn the colours of common transition metal compounds and flame test colours.

Deep Dive: c3

This topic is central to IGCSE Chemistry and connects to many other areas of the syllabus. Understanding the underlying principles will help you solve both straightforward recall questions and more complex application problems.

Core Principles
  1. Always write balanced chemical equations with correct state symbols.
  2. Show all working in calculations — method marks are awarded at every stage.
  3. Use the mole concept as the bridge between macroscopic measurements and atomic-scale quantities.
  4. Link observations to chemical explanations — examiners reward understanding over memorisation.
Problem-Solving Framework

Step 1: Identify what information is given and what is asked.

Step 2: Write a balanced equation if relevant.

Step 3: Calculate moles using the appropriate formula (n = mass/Mᵣ or n = c × V).

Step 4: Use the mole ratio from the balanced equation.

Step 5: Convert moles to the required quantity and check units.

Chemistry questions often test multiple skills in sequence. A calculation might require writing an equation, calculating moles, using a mole ratio, and converting back to mass or concentration.

Worked Example 1: Calculating Relative Atomic Mass from Mass Spectrum

Problem

A sample of boron contains 20% ¹⁰B and 80% ¹¹B. Calculate the relative atomic mass of boron.

Solution

Aᵣ = (10 × 20 + 11 × 80) / 100

Aᵣ = (200 + 880) / 100 = 1080 / 100 = 10.8

Worked Example 2: Writing Ionic Equations

Problem

Write the ionic equation for the reaction between aqueous silver nitrate and aqueous sodium chloride, including state symbols.

Solution

Molecular equation: AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq)

Ionic equation: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)

The Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ ions are spectator ions and do not appear in the ionic equation.

Worked Example 3: Mole Calculations

Problem

Calculate the mass of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) needed to produce 11.2 dm³ of CO₂ at room temperature and pressure. (Mᵣ: CaCO₃ = 100, 1 mole of gas occupies 24 dm³ at rtp)

Solution

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

Moles of CO₂ = 11.2 / 24 = 0.467 mol

Mole ratio CaCO₃ : CO₂ = 1 : 1

Moles of CaCO₃ = 0.467 mol

Mass = 0.467 × 100 = 46.7 g

Practice Questions

Question 1

Magnesium reacts with hydrochloric acid: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. Calculate the volume of hydrogen gas produced at rtp when 0.48 g of magnesium reacts with excess acid. (Mᵣ Mg = 24, 1 mole gas = 24 dm³ at rtp)

Answer: 0.48 dm³ (or 480 cm³)

Question 2

Neon has two isotopes: ²⁰Ne (90.5%) and ²²Ne (9.5%). Calculate the relative atomic mass of neon to 1 decimal place.

Answer: 20.2

Question 3

Explain why magnesium oxide has a higher melting point than sodium chloride, despite both being ionic compounds.

Answer: Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ have higher charges than Na⁺ and Cl⁻, resulting in stronger electrostatic attraction and more energy required to break the lattice.

🎯 Examination Tips

  • In organic chemistry, learn the general formulae and functional groups thoroughly.
  • For titration calculations, write the equation first, then use the mole ratio methodically.
  • For "explain" questions, link the observation to the underlying chemistry.
  • Always show your working in calculations — method marks are often worth more than the answer mark.
  • Use the Periodic Table provided — atomic numbers and relative atomic masses are given.

📝 Quick Summary

  • Solids: fixed shape and volume, particles vibrate
  • Liquids: fixed volume, take shape of container
  • Gases: fill container, particles far apart
  • Ideal gas equation: PV = nRT
  • Temperature in Kelvin = °C + 273

💡 Exam Tips

  • Convert units: 1 dm³ = 0.001 m³, 1 kPa = 1000 Pa
  • Always use Kelvin for temperature
  • At STP (273 K, 101325 Pa), 1 mole = 22.4 dm³
