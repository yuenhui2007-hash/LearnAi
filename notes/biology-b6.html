Detailed Notes

1

DNA Structure

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid): A double-stranded helical polymer of nucleotides that carries the genetic information of an organism.

Components of a Nucleotide

Each nucleotide consists of three components:

  • Pentose sugar: Deoxyribose in DNA; ribose in RNA.
  • Phosphate group: Attached to the 5' carbon of the sugar, forming the sugar-phosphate backbone.
  • Nitrogenous base: Attached to the 1' carbon. There are four bases in DNA:
    • Purines (double-ring): Adenine (A) and Guanine (G)
    • Pyrimidines (single-ring): Cytosine (C) and Thymine (T)

The Double Helix

  • Two antiparallel polynucleotide strands twisted into a double helix.
  • Antiparallel means the strands run in opposite directions: one 5'→3', the other 3'→5'.
  • Complementary base pairing: A pairs with T via 2 hydrogen bonds; G pairs with C via 3 hydrogen bonds.
  • The sugar-phosphate backbones are on the outside; base pairs are on the inside.
  • The two strands are held together by hydrogen bonds between bases.
The sequence of bases along a DNA strand forms the genetic code. Because of complementary base pairing, knowing the sequence of one strand allows you to determine the sequence of the other.
📝 Annotated Diagram Description: DNA Double Helix

When drawing DNA in an exam:

  • Show two antiparallel strands (label 5' and 3' ends).
  • Draw sugar-phosphate backbones as alternating circles (sugars) and triangles/phosphates.
  • Show base pairs as rungs of a ladder: A-T with 2 H-bonds, G-C with 3 H-bonds.
  • Label deoxyribose, phosphate, and the four bases.
Worked Example: Complementary Strand

Question: Write the complementary strand for the following DNA sequence: 5'-ATGCGTACCT-3'. [2 marks]

Answer:

Original: 5'-ATGCGTACCT-3'
Complementary: 3'-TACGCATGGA-5'

Or written 5' to 3': 5'-AGGTACGCAT-3'

2

DNA Replication

Semi-conservative replication: Each new DNA molecule consists of one original (parent) strand and one newly synthesised strand.

This was demonstrated by Meselson and Stahl (1958) using heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N) and light nitrogen (¹⁴N) isotopes. After one round of replication in light medium, DNA was of intermediate density, supporting the semi-conservative model.

Steps of DNA Replication
  1. Initiation: DNA helicase binds to the origin of replication and breaks hydrogen bonds between base pairs, unwinding the double helix to form two single strands.
  2. Primer synthesis: Primase synthesises a short RNA primer (about 10 nucleotides) complementary to the template strand. DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides to an existing 3'-OH group.
  3. Elongation: DNA polymerase III adds DNA nucleotides in the 5'→3' direction, using the original strand as a template. Complementary base pairing ensures accuracy.
  4. Leading strand: Synthesised continuously in the 5'→3' direction, moving toward the replication fork.
  5. Lagging strand: Synthesised discontinuously as short fragments called Okazaki fragments (also 5'→3', but away from the fork). DNA polymerase I replaces RNA primers with DNA. DNA ligase joins Okazaki fragments.
  6. Termination: Replication proceeds until the entire molecule is copied. Proofreading by DNA polymerase removes and replaces incorrectly paired nucleotides.
DNA replication is semi-conservative because each daughter DNA molecule contains one original (conserved) strand and one newly synthesised strand. This ensures genetic fidelity.
Worked Example: Calculating DNA Copies

Question: A cell replicates its DNA three times. How many DNA molecules will be present? [2 marks]

Solution:

After n rounds of replication, DNA molecules = 2ⁿ
After 3 rounds: 2³ = 8 DNA molecules
Of these, 2 will contain one original strand each (semi-conservative).
3

Transcription

Transcription: The synthesis of a single-stranded mRNA molecule from a DNA template. Occurs in the nucleus of eukaryotic cells.
Steps of Transcription
  1. Initiation: RNA polymerase binds to a promoter region on the DNA. The DNA double helix unwinds.
  2. Elongation: RNA polymerase synthesises mRNA in the 5'→3' direction, using one DNA strand as a template. RNA contains uracil (U) instead of thymine (T).
  3. Termination: RNA polymerase reaches a terminator sequence and detaches. The mRNA is released.
  4. Post-transcriptional modification (in eukaryotes):
    • 5' cap: A modified guanine nucleotide added to the 5' end — protects mRNA from degradation and aids ribosome binding.
    • Poly-A tail: A string of adenine nucleotides added to the 3' end — increases stability and aids export from nucleus.
    • Splicing: Introns (non-coding sequences) are removed; exons (coding sequences) are joined together. Catalysed by spliceosomes (snRNPs).
In eukaryotes, the primary transcript (pre-mRNA) contains both exons and introns. Splicing removes introns. Some genes can produce different proteins through alternative splicing (different combinations of exons).
Common Misconception: Many students confuse transcription and translation. Remember: Transcription = DNA → RNA (in the nucleus). Translation = RNA → Protein (at the ribosome).
4

Translation

Translation: The synthesis of a polypeptide chain using the information encoded in mRNA. Occurs at ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
Steps of Translation
  1. Initiation: The small ribosomal subunit binds to mRNA at the start codon (AUG, which codes for methionine). A tRNA with the anticodon UAC and carrying methionine binds to the start codon. The large ribosomal subunit joins.
  2. Elongation:
    • A second tRNA (with appropriate anticodon) binds to the next codon at the A site.
    • A peptide bond forms between the amino acids in the P and A sites, catalysed by peptidyl transferase (ribozyme activity of rRNA).
    • The ribosome moves one codon along the mRNA (translocation).
    • The first tRNA moves to the E site and is released.
    • The process repeats, adding amino acids one at a time.
  3. Termination: A stop codon (UAA, UAG, or UGA) is reached. A release factor binds, causing the polypeptide to be released and the ribosomal subunits to separate.

The Genetic Code

  • Triplet code: Three bases (a codon) specify one amino acid.
  • Degenerate: Most amino acids are coded for by more than one codon. This reduces the impact of point mutations.
  • Universal: The same code is used by almost all organisms (with minor exceptions in mitochondria and some protists).
  • Non-overlapping: Each base is part of only one codon.
  • Commaless: There are no "punctuation marks" between codons.
  • Start codon: AUG (methionine).
  • Stop codons: UAA, UAG, UGA (do not code for amino acids).
Worked Example: Translation

Question: Given the mRNA sequence: 5'-AUG GGC UAU CAA UAA-3', determine the amino acid sequence produced. [2 marks]

Answer:

Codons: AUG | GGC | UAU | CAA | UAA
Amino acids: Met (Methionine) — Gly (Glycine) — Tyr (Tyrosine) — Gln (Glutamine) — STOP
5

tRNA and Ribosomes

Transfer RNA (tRNA)

  • Small, cloverleaf-shaped RNA molecules.
  • Each tRNA has an anticodon (three bases complementary to an mRNA codon) at one end.
  • At the 3' end, it carries the specific amino acid corresponding to its anticodon.
  • Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases attach the correct amino acid to each tRNA — this is a critical proofreading step.

Ribosomes

  • Composed of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and proteins.
  • Prokaryotes: 70S ribosomes (50S + 30S subunits).
  • Eukaryotes: 80S ribosomes in cytoplasm (60S + 40S subunits); 70S in mitochondria and chloroplasts.
  • Three binding sites for tRNA: A (aminoacyl), P (peptidyl), and E (exit) sites.
🔬 Real-World Application: Antibiotics Targeting Ribosomes

Many antibiotics exploit differences between prokaryotic and eukaryotic ribosomes. For example, tetracyclines block the A site of prokaryotic ribosomes, preventing tRNA binding. Macrolides (e.g., erythromycin) block the exit tunnel. These drugs do not affect human 80S ribosomes, making them selectively toxic to bacteria.

Gene Mutation

Gene mutation: A change in the sequence of nucleotides in a gene. The most common type is a point mutation (substitution, deletion, or insertion of a single nucleotide).
TypeDescriptionEffect
SubstitutionOne base is replaced by anotherMay be silent (no change in amino acid), missense (different amino acid), or nonsense (premature stop codon)
DeletionOne base is removedFrameshift — alters all downstream codons; usually severe
InsertionOne extra base is addedFrameshift — alters all downstream codons; usually severe
Worked Example: Effect of Point Mutations

Question: The DNA template strand reads: 3'-TAC AAA GGG CCC ATT-5'. A substitution changes the second A in the second codon to G. Predict the effect on the protein. [3 marks]

Answer:

Original mRNA: 5'-AUG UUU CCC GGG UAA-3'
Amino acids: Met — Phe — Pro — Gly — STOP

Mutated mRNA: 5'-AUG UUC CCC GGG UAA-3'
Amino acids: Met — Phe — Pro — Gly — STOP

This is a silent mutation — both UUU and UUC code for phenylalanine, so the protein is unchanged (1). However, if the mutation had created a stop codon, it would be a nonsense mutation with a more severe effect (1).

Last-Minute Summary

  • DNA structure: Double helix, antiparallel strands, A-T (2 H-bonds), G-C (3 H-bonds), sugar-phosphate backbone.
  • Replication: Semi-conservative; helicase unwinds; primase makes RNA primer; DNA polymerase adds nucleotides 5'→3'; leading strand continuous; lagging strand as Okazaki fragments; ligase joins fragments.
  • Transcription: DNA → mRNA; RNA polymerase; splicing removes introns; 5' cap and poly-A tail added.
  • Translation: mRNA → protein at ribosome; tRNA anticodons match mRNA codons; peptide bonds form; stop codon terminates.
  • Genetic code: Triplet, degenerate, universal, non-overlapping.
  • Mutations: Substitution (may be silent/missense/nonsense); deletion/insertion (cause frameshifts).

💡 Exam Tips

  • Always draw DNA with antiparallel strands (5' and 3' ends labelled).
  • DNA replication: leading vs lagging strand — know which enzymes do what.
  • Transcription occurs in the nucleus; translation at ribosomes in cytoplasm.
  • Remember the start codon (AUG = methionine) and stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA).
  • In mutation questions, work out the amino acid sequence before and after to determine the effect.
  • Distinguish exons (expressed) from introns (intervening sequences) — introns are spliced out.
  • Semi-conservative means one old strand + one new strand in each daughter DNA.
