Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are organic molecules containing carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen, typically in the ratio 1:2:1. They serve as the primary source of energy for living organisms and provide structural support in plants and some animals.

Monosaccharide: The simplest carbohydrate unit (monomer) that cannot be hydrolysed into smaller carbohydrate units. General formula: (CH₂O)n.

Monosaccharides

The most common monosaccharides are hexoses (6-carbon sugars) and pentoses (5-carbon sugars):

  • Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆): The main respiratory substrate. Exists as α-glucose and β-glucose, which differ in the position of the —OH group on carbon-1. α-glucose is used to make starch and glycogen; β-glucose is used to make cellulose.
  • Fructose (C₆H₁₂O₆): A ketohexose found in fruits and honey. Sweeter than glucose.
  • Galactose (C₆H₁₂O₆): A component of lactose (milk sugar).
  • Ribose (C₅H₁₀O₅) and Deoxyribose (C₅H₁₀O₄): Pentose sugars found in RNA and DNA respectively.
The difference between α-glucose and β-glucose is critical. In α-glucose, the —OH on carbon-1 is below the ring plane; in β-glucose, it is above. This small difference means enzymes that digest starch cannot digest cellulose.

Disaccharides are formed when two monosaccharides join by a glycosidic bond in a condensation reaction (water is removed).

📝 Annotated Diagram Description: Starch vs Cellulose

Starch (amylose):

  • Unbranched chain of α-glucose molecules joined by α-1,4 glycosidic bonds.
  • The chain coils into a helix, making it compact for storage.
  • Insoluble in water, so it does not affect the osmotic balance of cells.

Cellulose:

  • Straight, unbranched chains of β-glucose joined by β-1,4 glycosidic bonds.
  • Every other β-glucose molecule is inverted, allowing hydrogen bonds to form between adjacent chains.
  • Many chains bond together to form strong microfibrils, providing rigidity to plant cell walls.
Worked Example: Why Starch is Suitable for Energy Storage

Question: Explain why starch is a suitable molecule for energy storage in plants. [3 marks]

Answer:

  • Starch is insoluble in water, so it does not affect the water potential of cells and can be stored without causing osmotic problems (1).
  • It is a large, compact molecule (coiled helix), so a lot of glucose can be stored in a small space (1).
  • The many glycosidic bonds contain chemical potential energy that can be released by hydrolysis when glucose is needed for respiration (1).
Common Misconception: Students often think cellulose is branched like glycogen. Cellulose is actually unbranched. The strength comes from hydrogen bonding between parallel straight chains, not from branching.
🔬 Real-World Application: Dietary Fibre

Humans lack the enzyme cellulase needed to digest cellulose, so cellulose passes through the digestive system as dietary fibre (roughage). Fibre adds bulk to faeces, aiding peristalsis and preventing constipation. It may also reduce the risk of bowel cancer and help regulate blood cholesterol levels.

Lipids

Lipids are a diverse group of hydrophobic (water-repelling) organic molecules. They include triglycerides, phospholipids, and steroids. Lipids are insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents such as ethanol.

Triglycerides

Triglycerides are the main energy storage lipids. They consist of:

  • One glycerol molecule: A 3-carbon alcohol with an —OH group on each carbon.
  • Three fatty acid molecules: Long hydrocarbon chains with a carboxyl group (—COOH) at one end.
Glycerol + 3 Fatty Acids → Triglyceride + 3 H₂O
(Ester bonds form between glycerol and fatty acids in condensation reactions)

A fatty acid with one C=C bond is monounsaturated; with two or more is polyunsaturated. Trans fats are produced by hydrogenation of vegetable oils and are particularly harmful to cardiovascular health.

Phospholipids

Phospholipids are similar to triglycerides but one fatty acid is replaced by a phosphate group (which is often attached to another polar group such as choline).

  • Hydrophilic (water-loving) head: The phosphate group and attached group are polar and interact with water.
  • Hydrophobic (water-fearing) tails: The two fatty acid chains are non-polar and repel water.
📝 Annotated Diagram Description: Phospholipid Bilayer

In an aqueous environment, phospholipids arrange themselves into a bilayer:

  • Hydrophilic heads face outwards towards the water on both sides.
  • Hydrophobic tails face inwards, shielded from water.
  • This forms the basic structure of all cell membranes.
  • The bilayer is fluid — phospholipids can move laterally.
  • It is a mosaic because proteins are scattered throughout.

Steroids

Steroids have a characteristic structure of four fused carbon rings. Examples include:

  • Cholesterol: Found in animal cell membranes, maintaining fluidity at low temperatures and stability at high temperatures. Precursor to steroid hormones.
  • Steroid hormones: Testosterone, oestrogen, progesterone, cortisol — all derived from cholesterol.
Worked Example: Energy Storage — Lipids vs Carbohydrates

Question: Explain why lipids are a more efficient energy storage molecule than carbohydrates. [3 marks]

Answer:

  • Lipids contain more C—H bonds per molecule than carbohydrates, meaning they release more energy per gram when oxidised (about 37 kJ/g compared to 16 kJ/g for carbohydrates) (1).
  • Lipids are insoluble in water and are stored as droplets, so they do not affect the water potential of cells and do not require water for storage (1).
  • When oxidised, lipids produce more water (metabolic water), which is valuable for desert animals such as kangaroo rats and camels (1).
🔬 Real-World Application: Cholesterol and Cardiovascular Health

High levels of LDL (low-density lipoprotein) cholesterol in the blood are associated with atherosclerosis — the buildup of fatty plaques in arteries. HDL (high-density lipoprotein) cholesterol is considered "good" because it transports cholesterol back to the liver for excretion. Dietary choices (reducing saturated fats, increasing unsaturated fats) and exercise can improve the HDL:LDL ratio.

Proteins

Proteins are polymers of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. They have an enormous range of functions in living organisms, from enzymes and hormones to structural components and antibodies.

Amino Acid Structure

All amino acids share a common basic structure:

  • A central alpha carbon (Cα) bonded to:
    • An amino group (—NH₂)
    • A carboxyl group (—COOH)
    • A hydrogen atom
    • A variable R group (side chain) — this differs between the 20 amino acids and determines their properties.
General amino acid structure:
    H
    |
H₂N—C—COOH
    |
    R
Amino acids are amphoteric — they can act as acids (donate H⁺ from —COOH) or bases (accept H⁺ at —NH₂). At physiological pH (~7.4), they exist as zwitterions with both positive and negative charges.

Formation of Peptide Bonds

Amino acids join by condensation reactions between the amino group of one amino acid and the carboxyl group of another, forming a peptide bond (—CO—NH—) and releasing water.

  • Dipeptide: Two amino acids joined by one peptide bond.
  • Polypeptide: Many amino acids joined by peptide bonds.
  • Protein: One or more polypeptide chains folded into a functional 3D structure.

📝 Annotated Diagram Description: Protein Structure Levels

Primary structure: Imagine a string of coloured beads, where each colour represents a different amino acid. The sequence is determined by the gene coding for the protein.

Secondary structure: The string coils into an α-helix (like a spiral staircase) or folds into a β-pleated sheet (like a pleated curtain). Hydrogen bonds form between the backbone atoms.

Tertiary structure: The coiled/folded chain further folds into a complex 3D shape, driven by interactions between R groups. Hydrophobic R groups cluster inside; hydrophilic R groups face outward.

Quaternary structure: Multiple polypeptide chains assemble. Example: Haemoglobin has four subunits (2α + 2β); insulin has two chains (A and B) linked by disulfide bonds.

Types of Proteins

  • Enzymes: Globular proteins that catalyse metabolic reactions. Have a specific active site.
  • Transport proteins: Haemoglobin (transports oxygen), albumin (transports fatty acids), channel/carrier proteins (membrane transport).
  • Structural proteins: Collagen (tendons, ligaments, skin — triple helix, high tensile strength), keratin (hair, nails), elastin (elastic tissues).
  • Defence proteins: Antibodies (immunoglobulins) that recognise and neutralise pathogens.
  • Hormonal proteins: Insulin, glucagon, growth hormone — chemical messengers.
  • Contractile proteins: Actin and myosin in muscle fibres.
Worked Example: Structure-Function in Collagen vs Haemoglobin

Question: Compare the structure and function of collagen and haemoglobin. [4 marks]

Answer:

  • Collagen is a fibrous protein with a triple helix structure of three polypeptide chains, providing high tensile strength for connective tissues such as tendons and ligaments (1).
  • Haemoglobin is a globular protein with four polypeptide subunits (2α + 2β), each containing a haem group with an iron ion that binds oxygen for transport in blood (1).
  • Collagen has a regular, repeating structure with many proline and glycine residues, making it strong but insoluble (1).
  • Haemoglobin has a compact, rounded structure that allows it to dissolve in blood plasma and fit inside red blood cells (1).
Common Misconception: Students often confuse the bonds at each structural level. Remember: peptide bonds ONLY at primary; hydrogen bonds at secondary (backbone); ionic, hydrogen, disulfide, and hydrophobic interactions at tertiary. Disulfide bridges are the strongest of these interactions.

Water & Inorganic Ions

Properties of Water

Water is essential for life due to its unique properties, which arise from its polar nature and hydrogen bonding:

  • Solvent properties: Water is an excellent solvent for polar and ionic substances because its partially charged regions (δ+ on H, δ− on O) surround and separate ions and polar molecules.
  • High specific heat capacity: Water absorbs a lot of heat before its temperature rises, buffering organisms against rapid temperature changes.
  • High latent heat of vaporisation: Water requires a lot of energy to evaporate, making sweating an effective cooling mechanism.
  • Cohesion and adhesion: Water molecules stick to each other (cohesion) and to polar surfaces (adhesion), essential for transpiration in plants.
  • Density anomaly: Ice is less dense than liquid water, so it floats, insulating water bodies below and allowing aquatic life to survive freezing.
  • Transparent: Allows light to penetrate for photosynthesis in aquatic ecosystems.

Biochemical Tests

Biochemical tests are used to identify the presence of specific biological molecules in food samples or biological extracts.

TestSubstance TestedMethodPositive ResultKey Notes
Benedict's testReducing sugars (e.g., glucose, fructose, maltose)Add Benedict's reagent (blue), heat in boiling water bathBrick red precipitate (colour change: blue → green → yellow → orange → brick red)Non-reducing sugars (e.g., sucrose) give negative result unless first hydrolysed with dilute acid
Iodine testStarchAdd iodine in potassium iodide solutionBlue-black colourVery sensitive; even tiny amounts of starch give positive result
Biuret testProteinAdd Biuret reagent (sodium hydroxide + copper sulfate)Mauve / purple / violet colourDetects peptide bonds; dipeptides may not give positive result
Emulsion testLipidShake sample with ethanol, then pour into waterWhite emulsion (milky appearance)Ethanol dissolves lipid; when added to water, lipid forms tiny droplets (emulsion)
Sudan III testLipidAdd Sudan III stainRed-stained droplets float on surfaceAlternative to emulsion test
Testing for Non-Reducing Sugars
  1. First, perform Benedict's test on the sample. If negative (stays blue), a non-reducing sugar may be present.
  2. Add dilute hydrochloric acid and heat gently to hydrolyse the glycosidic bond (e.g., sucrose → glucose + fructose).
  3. Neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate ( Benedict's reagent only works in alkaline conditions).
  4. Perform Benedict's test again. A positive result confirms the presence of a non-reducing sugar.
Worked Example: Interpreting Test Results

Question: A food sample gives a negative result with Benedict's test, a positive blue-black result with iodine, and a positive mauve result with Biuret reagent. Identify the biological molecules present and explain your reasoning. [3 marks]

Answer:

  • Starch is present because iodine test gave a blue-black colour (1).
  • Protein is present because the Biuret test gave a mauve/purple colour, indicating peptide bonds (1).
  • No reducing sugar is present (or reducing sugar concentration is very low) because Benedict's test remained blue (1).
📋 Exam-Style Question

Question: Describe how you would test a sample of unknown food for the presence of reducing sugars, starch, protein, and lipid. Include the expected results for a positive test in each case. [8 marks]

Mark Scheme:
  • Reducing sugar: add Benedict's reagent, heat in water bath; positive = brick red precipitate (2)
  • Starch: add iodine in KI solution; positive = blue-black colour (2)
  • Protein: add Biuret reagent (NaOH then CuSO₄); positive = mauve/purple colour (2)
  • Lipid: shake with ethanol, pour into water; positive = white emulsion (2)

📝 Quick Summary

  • Carbohydrates: monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose), disaccharides (maltose, sucrose, lactose), polysaccharides (starch, glycogen, cellulose)
  • Glucose exists as α-glucose (starch, glycogen) and β-glucose (cellulose)
  • Glycosidic bonds form in condensation; break in hydrolysis
  • Starch (storage): amylose (helix) + amylopectin (branched); glycogen (highly branched); cellulose (straight chains, H-bonds → microfibrils)
  • Lipids: triglycerides (glycerol + 3 fatty acids, ester bonds), phospholipids (glycerol + 2 fatty acids + phosphate), steroids (4 rings)
  • Saturated fats: no C=C, solid at room temp; unsaturated: one or more C=C, liquid at room temp
  • Proteins: amino acids joined by peptide bonds; primary (sequence), secondary (α-helix, β-sheet), tertiary (3D folding), quaternary (multiple subunits)
  • Water: polar, excellent solvent, high specific heat capacity, high latent heat of vaporisation, cohesive and adhesive
  • Tests: Benedict's (reducing sugar → brick red), iodine (starch → blue-black), Biuret (protein → mauve), emulsion (lipid → white)

💡 Exam Tips

  • Remember Benedict's test for reducing sugars — if testing for non-reducing sugars, hydrolyse first with dilute acid, then neutralise before adding Benedict's
  • Iodine test: starch → blue-black (not just "blue")
  • Biuret test: protein → purple/violet/mauve (not "pink" or "red")
  • Emulsion test: lipids → white emulsion (describe the milky appearance)
  • When drawing glucose, show the ring structure for α- and β-glucose, highlighting the position of the —OH on carbon-1
  • For protein structure questions, always name the specific bonds at each level
  • Lipids yield more energy per gram (≈37 kJ/g) than carbohydrates (≈16 kJ/g) due to more reduced carbon atoms
  • Always link structure to function — exam questions frequently ask "explain how the structure is related to its function"
