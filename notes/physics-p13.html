IGCSE AS Level

Thermal Physics

9702 (A-Level) · 0625 (IGCSE)

Detailed Notes

1. Temperature Scales

Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance. Two common scales are used in physics:

  • Celsius (°C): Based on the freezing point (0°C) and boiling point (100°C) of water at standard pressure.
  • Kelvin (K): The absolute thermodynamic scale. T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15.
Absolute zero (0 K = -273.15°C) is the theoretical temperature at which all molecular motion ceases. It is the lowest possible temperature. In calculations, use T(K) = T(°C) + 273 (approximation).

2. Thermal Expansion

Most materials expand when heated due to increased molecular vibrations:

  • Solids: ΔL = αL₀ΔT (linear expansion), where α is the coefficient of linear expansion.
  • Liquids: ΔV = βV₀ΔT (volume expansion), where β is the coefficient of volume expansion.
  • Gases: Expand much more than solids or liquids; obey the ideal gas law.

3. Ideal Gas Law

An ideal gas is a theoretical gas consisting of point particles that do not interact except during elastic collisions. Real gases approximate ideal behaviour at low pressure and high temperature.

pV = nRT = NkT
where n = number of moles, N = number of molecules, R = 8.31 J/(mol·K), k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K

📖 Key Definition

Boltzmann constant (k): k = R/N_A = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K. It relates temperature to energy at the molecular level.

4. Kinetic Theory of Gases

The kinetic theory models a gas as a large number of small particles in random motion. From this model, we derive:

pV = ⅓Nmc²
Average kinetic energy of one molecule: Eₖ = ½mc² = (3/2)kT
Root-mean-square speed: c_rms = √(3kT/m) = √(3RT/M)

where m = mass of one molecule, M = molar mass, and c² is the mean square speed.

The average kinetic energy of an ideal gas molecule depends only on temperature, not on the type of gas. At the same temperature, hydrogen molecules and oxygen molecules have the same average kinetic energy, but hydrogen molecules move faster because they are lighter.

5. Specific Heat Capacity

The specific heat capacity c of a substance is the energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of the substance by 1 K:

Q = mcΔT

Unit: J/(kg·K). Water has a very high specific heat capacity (4200 J/(kg·K)), which is why it is used in heating systems and why coastal areas have milder climates.

📝 Worked Example — Specific Heat Capacity

Question: An electric heater of power 2.0 kW is used to heat 0.50 kg of water from 20°C to 80°C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/(kg·K). Calculate the time required, assuming no heat losses.

Solution:

Q = mcΔT = 0.50 × 4200 × (80 − 20) = 126,000 J

t = Q/P = 126,000/2000 = 63 s

6. Specific Latent Heat

Latent heat is the energy required for a substance to change phase at constant temperature. The specific latent heat L is the energy per unit mass:

Q = mL
  • Specific latent heat of fusion (L_f): Energy to melt 1 kg of solid at its melting point.
  • Specific latent heat of vaporisation (L_v): Energy to vaporise 1 kg of liquid at its boiling point.
During phase change, the temperature remains constant even though energy is being supplied. The energy breaks intermolecular bonds (fusion) or does work against atmospheric pressure while breaking bonds (vaporisation).

📝 Worked Example — Phase Change

Question: Ice at 0°C is added to 0.40 kg of water at 25°C. The final temperature is 5°C. Calculate the mass of ice added. (c_water = 4200 J/(kg·K), L_fusion = 3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg)

Solution:

Heat lost by water = 0.40 × 4200 × (25 − 5) = 33,600 J

Heat gained by ice = m × 3.34 × 10⁵ + m × 4200 × (5 − 0) = m(334,000 + 21,000) = 355,000m

355,000m = 33,600 → m = 0.095 kg (95 g)

7. First Law of Thermodynamics

The first law is a statement of conservation of energy for thermal systems:

ΔU = Q − W

where ΔU = increase in internal energy, Q = heat supplied to the system, W = work done by the system. Note: some textbooks use ΔU = Q + W where W is work done on the system — be careful with sign conventions!

For an ideal gas, internal energy U consists entirely of the kinetic energy of the molecules. U depends only on temperature: ΔU ∝ ΔT. For real gases and other substances, U also includes potential energy from intermolecular forces.

⚠️ Common Misconception

Students often think that temperature always increases when heat is added. This is not true during phase changes — when ice melts at 0°C, heat is absorbed but the temperature stays constant. The energy goes into breaking intermolecular bonds, not increasing kinetic energy.

🎯 Exam-Style Question

Question: (a) State what is meant by absolute zero. (b) A gas cylinder contains 2.0 mol of ideal gas at 27°C and 5.0 × 10⁵ Pa. Calculate the volume of the gas. (c) The temperature is raised to 77°C at constant volume. Calculate the new pressure. [5 marks]

Mark Scheme

• (a) Theoretical temperature at which molecules have minimum/zero kinetic energy / lowest possible temperature / -273°C (1 mark)

• (b) T = 27 + 273 = 300 K. V = nRT/p = (2.0 × 8.31 × 300)/(5.0 × 10⁵) (1 mark)

• V = 9.97 × 10⁻³ m³ (accept 0.010 m³ or 10 L) (1 mark)

• (c) p/T = constant → p₂ = p₁T₂/T₁ = 5.0 × 10⁵ × 350/300 (1 mark)

• p₂ = 5.83 × 10⁵ Pa (accept 5.8 × 10⁵ Pa) (1 mark)

🎯 Exam-Style Question

Question: A metal block of mass 0.80 kg is heated by an electric heater of power 40 W for 5.0 minutes. The temperature rises from 18°C to 42°C. Calculate the specific heat capacity of the metal. [3 marks]

Mark Scheme

• Q = Pt = 40 × 300 = 12,000 J (1 mark)

• c = Q/(mΔT) = 12,000/(0.80 × 24) (1 mark)

• c = 625 J/(kg·K) (accept 620–630) (1 mark)

Last-Minute Summary

  • T(K) = T(°C) + 273; Absolute zero = 0 K = -273°C
  • Ideal gas: pV = nRT = NkT
  • Kinetic theory: pV = ⅓Nmc²; Average Eₖ = (3/2)kT
  • Specific heat: Q = mcΔT
  • Latent heat: Q = mL
  • 1st Law: ΔU = Q − W (increase in internal energy = heat in − work out)
  • Internal energy of ideal gas depends only on temperature

Exam Tips

  • Always convert Celsius to Kelvin before using the ideal gas equation.
  • For gas law calculations, remember: pV/T = constant (for fixed amount of gas).
  • When solving calorimetry problems, assume heat lost by hot body = heat gained by cold body (unless stated otherwise).
  • During phase change, temperature is constant — don't include ΔT for the phase-changing substance.
  • Check whether the question asks for specific heat capacity or specific latent heat.
  • In first law problems, be clear about your sign convention for work done.
