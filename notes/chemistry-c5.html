Enthalpy Changes — Fundamentals

Enthalpy change (ΔH) is the heat energy change measured at constant pressure. It is a state function, meaning it depends only on the initial and final states, not the path taken.

📘 Key Definitions

Exothermic reaction: Heat is released to the surroundings. ΔH < 0. Products have less enthalpy than reactants.

Endothermic reaction: Heat is absorbed from the surroundings. ΔH > 0. Products have more enthalpy than reactants.

Standard enthalpy change of reaction (ΔH°ᵣ): The enthalpy change when the reaction occurs with all reactants and products in their standard states at 298 K and 1 atm.

Standard enthalpy change of formation (ΔH°f): The enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states.

Standard enthalpy change of combustion (ΔH°c): The enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely burned in oxygen under standard conditions.

Standard enthalpy change of neutralisation (ΔH°neut): The enthalpy change when one mole of water is formed from the neutralisation of an acid and a base under standard conditions.

ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants)

For exothermic: H(products) < H(reactants), so ΔH < 0
For endothermic: H(products) > H(reactants), so ΔH > 0

Calorimetry — Experimental Methods

Calorimetry measures heat changes by observing temperature changes in a known mass of water.

q = mcΔT

q = heat energy (J)
m = mass of water (g)
c = specific heat capacity of water = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹
ΔT = temperature change (K or °C)
Worked Example: Combustion Calorimetry

0.500 g of ethanol (C₂H₅OH) was burned, heating 200 g of water from 20.0°C to 45.0°C. Calculate the enthalpy of combustion per mole.

Solution:

q = mcΔT = 200 × 4.18 × 25.0 = 20900 J = 20.9 kJ

n(ethanol) = 0.500 / 46.0 = 0.01087 mol

ΔH°c = −20.9 / 0.01087 = −1923 kJ mol⁻¹ (negative because exothermic)

Note: Experimental value is less than theoretical (−1367 kJ mol⁻¹) due to heat losses.

⚠️ Common Misconceptions

• ΔH°f for elements in their standard states is ZERO — don't try to calculate it!

• Combustion is always exothermic (ΔH < 0); formation can be either.

• In calorimetry, heat lost by reaction = heat gained by water/solution (assuming no heat loss).

• Always divide by moles of the substance specified in the definition.

Hess's Law and Enthalpy Cycles

📘 Key Definition

Hess's Law: The total enthalpy change for a reaction is independent of the route taken, provided the initial and final conditions are the same.

Hess's Law allows us to calculate enthalpy changes that cannot be measured directly by constructing alternative reaction routes using known enthalpy changes.

Method 1: Using formation enthalpies
ΔH°reaction = ΣΔH°f(products) − ΣΔH°f(reactants)

Method 2: Using combustion enthalpies
ΔH°reaction = ΣΔH°c(reactants) − ΣΔH°c(products)

Note the opposite subtraction for combustion data!
Worked Example: Using Formation Enthalpies

Calculate ΔH° for: C₂H₄(g) + H₂(g) → C₂H₆(g)

Given: ΔH°f(C₂H₄) = +52 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔH°f(C₂H₆) = −85 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔH°f(H₂) = 0

Solution:

ΔH° = ΔH°f(products) − ΔH°f(reactants)

ΔH° = (−85) − (+52 + 0) = −137 kJ mol⁻¹

Worked Example: Using Combustion Enthalpies

Calculate ΔH° for: C(s) + 2H₂(g) → CH₄(g)

Given: ΔH°c(C) = −394 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔH°c(H₂) = −286 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔH°c(CH₄) = −890 kJ mol⁻¹

Solution:

ΔH° = ΣΔH°c(reactants) − ΣΔH°c(products)

ΔH° = (−394 + 2(−286)) − (−890) = −966 + 890 = −76 kJ mol⁻¹

Enthalpy Cycle Diagram for Formation Route:

        Reactants ────────→ Products
          │ ΔH°reaction                  │
          ↓ ΣΔH°f(reactants)        ↑ ΣΔH°f(products)
        Elements ──────────────────→
                                                  ΔH°reaction = ΣΔH°f(products) − ΣΔH°f(reactants)

Bond Enthalpies

📘 Key Definitions

Bond dissociation enthalpy: The enthalpy change when one mole of a specific bond is broken in the gas phase.

Average bond enthalpy: The average value of a bond dissociation enthalpy taken over a range of compounds.

ΔH°reaction = Σ(bond enthalpies broken) − Σ(bond enthalpies formed)

Bonds broken = endothermic (+)
Bonds formed = exothermic (−)

Worked Example: Using Bond Enthalpies

Calculate ΔH° for: CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g)

Solution:

Bonds broken:

4 C−H: 4 × 413 = 1652 kJ mol⁻¹

2 O=O: 2 × 498 = 996 kJ mol⁻¹

Total broken = 2648 kJ mol⁻¹

Bonds formed:

2 C=O: 2 × 805 = 1610 kJ mol⁻¹

4 O−H: 4 × 464 = 1856 kJ mol⁻¹

Total formed = 3466 kJ mol⁻¹

ΔH° = 2648 − 3466 = −818 kJ mol⁻¹

Bond enthalpy calculations give approximate values because average bond enthalpies are used, not specific bond dissociation enthalpies. Also, they apply only to gas-phase reactions.

Lattice Energy and Born-Haber Cycles

📘 Key Definitions

Lattice energy (ΔH°latt): The enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic compound is formed from its gaseous ions.

Enthalpy change of atomisation (ΔH°at): The enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms is formed from the element in its standard state.

First ionisation energy (IE₁): Energy required to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms.

First electron affinity (EA₁): The enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms gains one mole of electrons to form one mole of gaseous −1 ions.

Born-Haber Cycle for NaCl
  1. Na(s) → Na(g)     ΔH°at = +107 kJ mol⁻¹
  2. ½Cl₂(g) → Cl(g)     ΔH°at = +122 kJ mol⁻¹
  3. Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻     IE₁ = +496 kJ mol⁻¹
  4. Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g)     EA₁ = −349 kJ mol⁻¹
  5. Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g) → NaCl(s)     ΔH°latt = ?

Using Hess's Law: ΔH°f = ΔH°at(Na) + ΔH°at(Cl) + IE₁ + EA₁ + ΔH°latt

−411 = +107 + 122 + 496 + (−349) + ΔH°latt

ΔH°latt = −787 kJ mol⁻¹

Worked Example: Born-Haber Cycle for MgCl₂

Calculate the lattice energy of MgCl₂ given:

ΔH°f = −642 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔH°at(Mg) = +148 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔH°at(Cl) = +122 kJ mol⁻¹

IE₁(Mg) = +738 kJ mol⁻¹; IE₂(Mg) = +1451 kJ mol⁻¹; EA₁(Cl) = −349 kJ mol⁻¹

Solution:

ΔH°f = ΔH°at(Mg) + 2ΔH°at(Cl) + IE₁ + IE₂ + 2EA₁ + ΔH°latt

−642 = 148 + 2(122) + 738 + 1451 + 2(−349) + ΔH°latt

−642 = 148 + 244 + 738 + 1451 − 698 + ΔH°latt

−642 = 1883 + ΔH°latt

ΔH°latt = −2525 kJ mol⁻¹

Lattice energy becomes more exothermic (more negative) when: • Ionic charge increases (MgO > NaCl) • Ionic radius decreases (NaCl > KCl; NaF > NaCl) This is explained by Coulomb's Law: F ∝ (q₁q₂)/r²

Entropy and Gibbs Free Energy

📘 Key Definitions

Entropy (S): A measure of the disorder or randomness of a system. Units: J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Gibbs free energy (G): The energy available to do useful work. ΔG determines spontaneity.

Gibbs equation: ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°

ΔG° < 0: reaction is spontaneous
ΔG° = 0: reaction is at equilibrium
ΔG° > 0: reaction is non-spontaneous

Entropy change of reaction: ΔS° = ΣS°(products) − ΣS°(reactants)

Factors Affecting Entropy

FactorEffect on Entropy
State of matterS(gas) >> S(liquid) > S(solid)
Number of molesMore moles → higher entropy
ComplexityMore atoms/more complex → higher entropy
TemperatureHigher T → higher entropy
MixingMixing substances → higher entropy
Worked Example: Predicting Spontaneity

For the reaction: 2HgO(s) → 2Hg(l) + O₂(g)

ΔH° = +181 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔS° = +216 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹

Calculate the temperature above which this decomposition becomes spontaneous.

Solution:

For spontaneity: ΔG° < 0

ΔH° − TΔS° < 0

181000 − T(216) < 0

T > 181000 / 216 = 838 K

The reaction becomes spontaneous above 838 K (565°C).

Worked Example: Temperature and Spontaneity

Predict the sign of ΔG° for the following at 298 K:

(a) Combustion of methane: ΔH° < 0, ΔS° < 0 (gas → gas, but fewer moles)

Answer: Spontaneous at low T; non-spontaneous at very high T (|TΔS| > |ΔH|)

(b) Dissolving NH₄Cl in water: ΔH° > 0, ΔS° > 0 (solid → ions in solution)

Answer: Non-spontaneous at low T; spontaneous at high T

The four combinations of ΔH and ΔS: • ΔH < 0, ΔS > 0: Always spontaneous (ΔG < 0 at all T) • ΔH > 0, ΔS < 0: Never spontaneous (ΔG > 0 at all T) • ΔH < 0, ΔS < 0: Spontaneous at low T • ΔH > 0, ΔS > 0: Spontaneous at high T

Exam-Style Questions

📝 Exam Question 1

(a) Define standard enthalpy change of formation. [2]

(b) Calculate ΔH° for: C₂H₆(g) + 3½O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l)

Given: ΔH°f(C₂H₆) = −85 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔH°f(CO₂) = −394 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔH°f(H₂O) = −286 kJ mol⁻¹ [2]

Mark Scheme:
(a) Enthalpy change when one mole of compound is formed (1) from its elements in their standard states (1).
(b) ΔH° = [2(−394) + 3(−286)] − [−85 + 0] (1) = −1560 kJ mol⁻¹ (1).
📝 Exam Question 2

(a) Define lattice energy. [2]

(b) Explain why MgO has a more exothermic lattice energy than NaCl. [2]

Mark Scheme:
(a) Enthalpy change when one mole of ionic compound is formed (1) from its gaseous ions (1).
(b) Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ have higher charges than Na⁺ and Cl⁻ (1). Higher charge → stronger electrostatic attraction / more exothermic lattice energy (1). Also smaller ionic radii (1). Max 2.
📝 Exam Question 3

For a reaction: ΔH° = +120 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔS° = +150 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

(a) Calculate ΔG° at 298 K and state whether the reaction is spontaneous. [2]

(b) Calculate the minimum temperature for spontaneity. [2]

Mark Scheme:
(a) ΔG° = 120000 − 298(150) = 120000 − 44700 = +75300 J mol⁻¹ = +75.3 kJ mol⁻¹ (1). Not spontaneous (1).
(b) T = ΔH°/ΔS° = 120000/150 = 800 K (2). [1 for method, 1 for answer]

📝 Quick Summary

  • Exothermic: ΔH < 0; Endothermic: ΔH > 0
  • Calorimetry: q = mcΔT; ΔH = q / n
  • Hess's Law: ΔH independent of route
  • Using ΔH°f: ΔH° = ΣΔH°f(products) − ΣΔH°f(reactants)
  • Using ΔH°c: ΔH° = ΣΔH°c(reactants) − ΣΔH°c(products)
  • Bond enthalpies: ΔH° = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed)
  • Lattice energy ∝ (charge⁺ × charge⁻) / (r⁺ + r⁻)
  • Born-Haber cycle: ΔH°f = sum of all steps including lattice energy
  • ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°; spontaneous when ΔG° < 0

💡 Exam Tips

  • Always check signs carefully — exothermic = negative
  • Remember ΔH°f(elements) = 0
  • In bond enthalpy calculations, be careful not to miss any bonds
  • When using combustion data, the subtraction is reversed compared to formation data
  • Convert ΔS to kJ before using in ΔG = ΔH − TΔS
  • Spontaneity depends on both enthalpy AND entropy — a reaction can be endothermic but spontaneous if ΔS is large and positive
