Selection & Evolution

9700 (A-Level) · 0610 (IGCSE)

IGCSE Content

1

Variation

  • Genetic variation: Differences in alleles due to mutation, meiosis (crossing over, independent assortment), and random fertilisation.
  • Environmental variation: Differences caused by environment (diet, climate, lifestyle).
  • Most characteristics are influenced by both genes and environment.
2

Natural Selection

Mechanism proposed by Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace:

Steps of Natural Selection
  1. Within a population there is variation (due to mutation, meiosis, etc.).
  2. There is competition for limited resources (struggle for existence).
  3. Individuals with advantageous characteristics are more likely to survive and reproduce (differential survival).
  4. These advantageous alleles are passed to offspring and become more common in the population over generations.
Natural selection acts on phenotypes, but evolution occurs at the genetic level — allele frequencies change over generations.
3

Evolution & Artificial Selection

  • Evolution: Change in allele frequencies in a population over time. Natural selection is the main mechanism driving adaptive evolution.
  • Artificial selection: Humans select individuals with desired characteristics to breed. Over many generations, breeds/cultivars with exaggerated traits are produced. Reduces genetic diversity compared to natural populations.
    • Examples: dairy cows (high milk yield), wheat (disease resistance, high yield), dogs (various breeds).
4

Antibiotic Resistance

  • Bacteria show natural variation in resistance to antibiotics (due to random mutations).
  • Antibiotic use creates a selection pressure.
  • Resistant bacteria survive and reproduce, passing resistance genes (often on plasmids) to offspring and other bacteria via horizontal gene transfer.
  • Overuse and misuse of antibiotics accelerate resistance.
🔬 Real-World Application: Antibiotic Stewardship

The WHO identifies antibiotic resistance as one of the greatest threats to global health. Strategies include: restricting antibiotic use in agriculture, ensuring patients complete full courses, developing new antibiotics, and using combination therapies. Bacteriophage therapy is an emerging alternative to antibiotics.

AS Level Content

5

Speciation

Formation of new species from existing ones:

  • Species definition: Group of organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
  • Isolation mechanisms: Prevent gene flow between populations.
    • Geographic isolation: Physical barrier (river, mountain, ocean) separates populations.
    • Reproductive isolation: Pre-zygotic (temporal, behavioural, mechanical, gamete incompatibility) or post-zygotic (hybrid inviability, hybrid sterility).
  • Allopatric speciation: Speciation due to geographic isolation. Different selection pressures and genetic drift cause divergence.
  • Sympatric speciation: Speciation without geographic separation (e.g., polyploidy in plants).

7

Industrial Melanism

  • Peppered moth (Biston betularia): light form camouflaged on lichen-covered trees; dark form (melanic) rare.
  • Industrial pollution killed lichen and darkened tree bark. Birds preyed more on light moths.
  • Melanic form increased in frequency due to natural selection.
  • After clean air laws, lichen returned, light form increased again.
  • Classic example of directional selection.

A2 Level Content

8

Sources of Variation

  • Mutation: Ultimate source of new alleles. Point mutations, chromosomal mutations (deletion, duplication, inversion, translocation), polyploidy.
  • Meiosis: Crossing over (prophase I) and independent assortment (metaphase I) reshuffle alleles.
  • Random fertilisation: Any gamete can fuse with any other, creating unique combinations.
  • Genetic drift: Random changes in allele frequencies, especially in small populations. Founder effect and bottleneck effect.
9

Types of Selection

TypeEffectExample
StabilisingFavours intermediate phenotypes; reduces variationHuman birth weight
DirectionalFavours one extreme; shifts mean phenotypeAntibiotic resistance, industrial melanism
Disruptive (diversifying)Favours both extremes; can lead to speciationDifferent beak sizes when both seed types abundant
BalancingMaintains multiple allelesSickle cell trait (heterozygote advantage)
Heterozygote advantage (balancing selection): HbA/HbS individuals are resistant to malaria while avoiding severe sickle cell anaemia. This maintains the sickle cell allele in malaria-endemic regions.
10

Molecular Evidence for Evolution

  • DNA hybridisation: More binding = more similar DNA = more closely related.
  • DNA sequencing: Compare base sequences directly. Fewer differences = more recent common ancestor.
  • Protein sequencing: Compare amino acid sequences (cytochrome c, haemoglobin).
  • Molecular clocks: Assume mutation rate is constant; calculate time since divergence.
11

Social & Ethical Issues

  • Genetic modification: Benefits (increased yield, pest resistance, medicines like insulin) vs risks (gene flow to wild relatives, reduction in biodiversity).
  • Conservation: Maintaining genetic diversity is crucial for species' ability to adapt.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Natural selection: variation → competition → differential survival → advantageous alleles increase in frequency.
  • Speciation requires isolation (geographic or reproductive) + divergence.
  • Evidence: fossils, homologous/analogous structures, embryos, DNA/protein sequences, biogeography.
  • Types of selection: stabilising (intermediate favoured), directional (one extreme favoured), disruptive (both extremes favoured), balancing (multiple alleles maintained).
  • Variation sources: mutation (ultimate), meiosis (crossing over, independent assortment), random fertilisation, genetic drift.
  • Artificial selection: humans choose parents; reduces genetic diversity.
  • Antibiotic resistance: selection pressure from antibiotic use favours resistant bacteria.
  • Industrial melanism: classic example of directional selection.

💡 Exam Tips

  • When explaining natural selection, always include all stages: variation exists → selection pressure → differential survival/reproduction → inheritance of advantageous alleles → change in allele frequency over generations.
  • Distinguish homologous (common ancestry, divergent evolution) from analogous (convergent evolution) structures clearly.
  • Speciation: define species first, then explain isolation mechanisms.
  • For selection types, describe the effect on the phenotype distribution curve.
  • Antibiotic resistance is a key example — explain the role of selection pressure clearly.
