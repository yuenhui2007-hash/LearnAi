Collision Theory

📘 Key Definitions

Rate of reaction: The change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. Units: mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.

Activation energy (Eₐ): The minimum energy required for a reaction to occur when reactant molecules collide.

Effective collision: A collision between reactant molecules that results in a reaction. Requires sufficient energy (≥ Eₐ) and correct orientation.

Collision theory states that for a reaction to occur, reactant particles must:

  1. Collide with each other
  2. Have kinetic energy equal to or greater than the activation energy (Eₐ)
  3. Collide with the correct orientation (steric factor)
Rate ∝ frequency of effective collisions
Factors that increase rate do so by increasing either the total collision frequency or the proportion of collisions that are effective.

Maxwell-Boltzmann and Temperature:

At higher temperature (T₂ > T₁):
• The curve shifts right and becomes broader
• The peak is lower
• A significantly larger proportion of molecules have E ≥ Eₐ
• This is the MAIN reason rate increases with temperature (not just faster collisions)
A 10°C increase in temperature approximately doubles the rate of many reactions because the proportion of molecules with E ≥ Eₐ increases exponentially (Arrhenius equation).

Rate Equations and Order of Reaction

📘 Key Definitions

Rate equation: An equation linking rate of reaction to the concentrations of reactants. Rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ

Order of reaction: The power to which the concentration of a reactant is raised in the rate equation. m is the order with respect to A.

Overall order: The sum of the individual orders (m + n).

Rate constant (k): A proportionality constant in the rate equation. Units depend on overall order.

Half-life (t½): The time taken for the concentration of a reactant to fall to half its initial value.

General rate equation: Rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ

Where:
k = rate constant
[A], [B] = concentrations in mol dm⁻³
m, n = orders with respect to A and B

Units of k:
Zero order overall: mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
First order overall: s⁻¹
Second order overall: dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹
Third order overall: dm⁶ mol⁻² s⁻¹
OrderRate vs ConcentrationHalf-lifeExample
ZeroRate is constant; independent of [A]Decreases as [A] decreasesRate = k
FirstRate ∝ [A]ConstantRate = k[A]
SecondRate ∝ [A]²Increases as [A] decreasesRate = k[A]²
Worked Example: Determining Rate Equation from Data

For the reaction A + B → C, the following data was obtained:

Expt[A] (mol dm⁻³)[B] (mol dm⁻³)Initial rate (mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹)
10.100.100.005
20.200.100.010
30.100.200.020

Solution:

Compare expts 1 and 2: [A] doubles, rate doubles → first order in A

Compare expts 1 and 3: [B] doubles, rate quadruples → second order in B

Rate = k[A][B]²

Using expt 1: 0.005 = k(0.10)(0.10)² → k = 0.005 / 0.001 = 5 dm⁶ mol⁻² s⁻¹

Worked Example: Half-Life Method

For a first-order reaction, the half-life is constant. If [A] falls from 1.0 to 0.5 mol dm⁻³ in 10 minutes, and from 0.5 to 0.25 in another 10 minutes, the reaction is first order.

First order: t½ = ln(2) / k = 0.693 / k

Clock reactions (e.g., iodine clock) are useful for measuring initial rates. The time taken for a visible change is inversely proportional to the rate (1/t ∝ rate for initial stages).

Reaction Mechanisms

📘 Key Definitions

Elementary step: A single step in a reaction mechanism involving a small number of molecules.

Molecularity: The number of reactant molecules involved in an elementary step.

Rate-determining step (RDS): The slowest step in a reaction mechanism, which determines the overall rate.

Intermediate: A species produced in one step and consumed in a subsequent step. It does not appear in the overall equation.

Worked Example: Deducing Mechanism from Rate Equation

The reaction: NO₂(g) + CO(g) → NO(g) + CO₂(g)

Experimentally: Rate = k[NO₂]²

Proposed mechanism:

Step 1 (slow, RDS): 2NO₂ → NO + NO₃

Step 2 (fast): NO₃ + CO → NO₂ + CO₂

Overall: NO₂ + CO → NO + CO₂

The rate equation matches the stoichiometry of the slow step — second order in NO₂, zero order in CO.

The rate equation can only be determined experimentally — it cannot be predicted from the stoichiometric equation. The stoichiometry only matches the rate equation if the reaction occurs in a single step.
⚠️ Common Misconceptions

• The order with respect to a reactant does NOT necessarily equal its stoichiometric coefficient.

• Intermediates appear in the mechanism but NOT in the overall equation or rate equation.

• Catalysts may appear in the mechanism but cancel out in the overall equation.

Catalysis

📘 Key Definitions

Homogeneous catalysis: The catalyst is in the same phase as the reactants.

Heterogeneous catalysis: The catalyst is in a different phase from the reactants (usually solid catalyst, gas/liquid reactants).

Heterogeneous Catalysis — Mechanism

  1. Adsorption: Reactant molecules bind to the catalyst surface.
  2. Reaction: Bonds in reactants weaken; new bonds form.
  3. Desorption: Product molecules leave the catalyst surface.

Examples of Catalysis

ReactionCatalystType
Haber process: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃Iron (with promoters)Heterogeneous
Contact process: 2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃V₂O₅Heterogeneous
Hydrogenation of alkenesNi or PtHeterogeneous
Fe³⁺/I⁻ reactionFe²⁺/Fe³⁺Homogeneous
Ozone decompositionNOₓ, CFCsHomogeneous
Catalysts increase rate by providing an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy. They are not consumed in the reaction and do not affect the position of equilibrium — they speed up both forward and reverse reactions equally.

Exam-Style Questions

📝 Exam Question 1

The rate equation for a reaction is: Rate = k[X][Y].

(a) State the overall order of the reaction. [1]

(b) When [X] = 0.20 mol dm⁻³ and [Y] = 0.30 mol dm⁻³, the rate is 0.012 mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. Calculate k and state its units. [2]

Mark Scheme:
(a) Second order (1).
(b) 0.012 = k(0.20)(0.30) → k = 0.012 / 0.06 = 0.20 (1). Units: dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ (1).
📝 Exam Question 2

(a) Explain why increasing temperature increases reaction rate. [3]

(b) Explain how a catalyst increases reaction rate without being consumed. [2]

Mark Scheme:
(a) Particles have more kinetic energy / move faster (1). More frequent collisions (1). Greater proportion of molecules have E ≥ Eₐ / Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution shifts right (1).
(b) Catalyst provides alternative reaction pathway with lower Eₐ (1). More molecules have E ≥ Eₐ at same temperature (1).

📝 Quick Summary

  • Collision theory: collide + sufficient energy + correct orientation
  • Rate increases with: concentration, pressure, temperature, surface area, catalyst
  • Rate equation: Rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ; order must be found experimentally
  • Zero order: rate constant; first order: constant half-life; second order: half-life increases
  • Rate-determining step is the slowest step in the mechanism
  • Homogeneous catalyst: same phase; Heterogeneous: different phase
  • Catalysts lower Eₐ but do not change ΔH or equilibrium position

💡 Exam Tips

  • Orders cannot be deduced from the balanced equation alone
  • Always show your working when determining orders from data
  • For half-life: constant = first order; increasing = second order; decreasing = zero order
  • When proposing mechanisms, the RDS must match the experimental rate equation
  • Remember: catalysts are regenerated, intermediates are consumed
