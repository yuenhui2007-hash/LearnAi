📘 H1: The Origins of World War I, 1871–1914

Long-Term Causes (1871–1914)

  • 1871 German Unification: Franco-Prussian War; Alsace-Lorraine annexed — France wanted revenge
  • 1879 Dual Alliance: Germany + Austria-Hungary (defensive)
  • 1882 Triple Alliance: Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy
  • 1887–90 Reinsurance Treaty: Bismarck kept Russia neutral; Wilhelm II refused to renew (1890)
  • 1894 Franco-Russian Alliance: Ended French isolation; encircled Germany
  • 1904 Entente Cordiale: Britain + France (colonial agreement, not military alliance)
  • 1907 Triple Entente: Britain, France, Russia

Militarism & Arms Race

  • German Naval Laws (1898, 1900): Tirpitz Plan challenged British naval supremacy
  • Dreadnought crisis (1909): Britain launched HMS Dreadnought; Germany followed; accelerated arms race
  • Conscription: All major powers had large standing armies by 1914 (Germany: 4.9m; Russia: 5.9m)
  • Schlieffen Plan: German strategy for two-front war — attack France through Belgium first, then Russia

Imperialism & Crises

  • 1905–06 First Moroccan Crisis: Germany challenged French control; conference at Algeciras — Germany isolated
  • 1908–09 Bosnian Crisis: Austria annexed Bosnia-Herzegovina; Russia backed down (not ready for war)
  • 1911 Second Moroccan Crisis (Agadir): Germany sent gunboat; Britain threatened war; Germany gained small territory but relations worsened
  • 1912–13 Balkan Wars: Serbia doubled territory; Austria determined to crush Serbia

The July Crisis 1914

  • 28 June Assassination: Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip (Black Hand, Serbian nationalist)
  • 5 July Blank Cheque: Germany promised full support to Austria
  • 23 July Austrian Ultimatum: 10 demands designed to be rejected
  • 25 July Serbian Response: Accepted most demands; Austria rejected it
  • 28 July Austria declared war on Serbia
  • 1 Aug Germany declared war on Russia (mobilisation)
  • 3 Aug Germany declared war on France; invaded Belgium
  • 4 Aug Britain declared war on Germany (1839 Treaty of London guaranteeing Belgian neutrality)

📚 Historiography — Who Was Responsible?

  • Fischer Thesis (1961): Germany deliberately planned war. "War Council" Dec 1912; September Programme (annexationist war aims) proves intent. Critique: Other powers had war aims too.
  • Clark — The Sleepwalkers (2012): No single power planned war. All powers stumbled into it through miscalculation, rigid alliance systems, and mobilisation timetables.
  • Structural vs Intentionalist: Were long-term forces (alliances, militarism) or short-term decisions (July Crisis) more important?
  • Counter to Fischer: Bethmann-Hollweg was reactive, not planning war. Russian mobilisation (31 July) forced German hand.

✍️ Essay Framework: "To what extent was Germany responsible for WWI?"

FOR Germany: Blank cheque to Austria; Schlieffen Plan (pre-war offensive strategy); naval expansion provoking Britain; Fischer thesis evidence (War Council, September Programme); Weltpolitik destabilised Europe.

AGAINST sole blame: Austrian ultimatum was autonomous; Russia chose mobilisation; France supported Russia; Britain's ambiguous position; alliance system made war likely regardless.

CONCLUSION: Germany bears significant responsibility — blank cheque and offensive strategy were enabling factors — but the alliance system and decisions of all powers made war a collective failure.

💡 Exam Tip: Always use SPECIFIC dates and evidence. "On 28 June 1914" not "in 1914". Include names (Bethmann-Hollweg, Grey, Sazonov) to demonstrate detailed knowledge.

📘 H2: The Holocaust

Stages of Persecution

  • 1933–35 Discrimination: Boycott of Jewish businesses (April 1933); Nuremberg Laws (Sept 1935) — stripped citizenship, banned intermarriage, defined "Jew" by race not religion
  • 1938 Violence: Kristallnacht (9–10 Nov) — state-organised pogrom; 267 synagogues destroyed; 30,000 sent to concentration camps; Jewish community fined 1 billion marks
  • 1939–41 Ghettoisation: Warsaw (400,000), Lodz, Krakow; forced labour; Judenrat (Jewish councils) administered Nazi orders
  • 1941–45 Final Solution: Einsatzgruppen (mobile killing squads) mass shootings in USSR; extermination camps (Auschwitz-Birkenau, Treblinka, Sobibor, Chelmno, Belzec, Majdanek)

Key Events

  • 20 Jan 1942 Wannsee Conference: Heydrich coordinated implementation of Final Solution across Nazi bureaucracy
  • 1942–44 Auschwitz-Birkenau: Largest death camp; 1.1 million killed (90% Jews); Zyklon B gas chambers
  • 1943 Warsaw Ghetto Uprising: Armed resistance crushed (13,000 killed)
  • 1944 Hungarian Deportations: 437,000 Jews sent to Auschwitz in 8 weeks

Ideological Roots

  • Anti-Semitism: Historical European prejudice + 19th century racial "science"
  • Volksgemeinschaft: Racial community excluding Jews and other "undesirables"
  • Lebensraum: Need for living space in East; Jews seen as obstacle to German expansion
  • Eugenics: "Purification" of German race — also targeted Roma, disabled, homosexuals, political opponents

Collaboration & Resistance

  • Collaboration: Vichy France (deported 76,000 Jews); Hungary (deported 437,000); Romania (massacred 280,000 independently)
  • Resistance: Denmark saved most Jews (ferried to Sweden); Bulgaria refused deportations; some individuals (Schindler, Wallenberg)

📚 Historiography — How Did the Holocaust Happen?

  • Intentionalists (Dawidowicz, Hillberg): Hitler planned genocide from early. Mein Kampf (1925) outlined intent. Holocaust was logical outcome of Nazi ideology.
  • Functionalists (Broszat, Browning): No master plan. Holocaust emerged from wartime radicalisation, bureaucratic momentum, and "cumulative radicalisation." Ordinary Men (Browning) — Reserve Police Battalion 101 participated in mass shootings; not just fanatics but ordinary Germans.
  • Goldhagen — Hitler's Willing Executioners (1996): "Eliminationist anti-Semitism" was widespread in German society. Controversial — overstated?
  • Structuralist: Nazi state was polycratic (competing agencies); Holocaust resulted from initiative from below + approval from above.

✍️ Essay Framework: "Was the Holocaust the result of a long-term plan or wartime radicalisation?"

INTENTIONALIST: Mein Kampf; Nuremberg Laws (1935) show racial intent; T-4 Euthanasia Programme (1939) as trial run; Madagascar Plan (1940) shows systematic planning.

FUNCTIONALIST: No written order from Hitler for Final Solution; Wannsee Conference (Jan 1942) came AFTER mass killings had begun; shifting policies (emigration → ghettoisation → extermination); local initiative in occupied territories.

CONCLUSION: Ideological intent existed from 1933, but the method and timing emerged from circumstances. Intention + opportunity = genocide.

📘 H3: The Cold War in Europe, 1941–1995

Origins (1941–1947)

  • 1941 Grand Alliance formed against Hitler; tensions over Second Front (Stalin wanted earlier opening)
  • Feb 1945 Yalta: Agreed on division of Germany; disagreement over Poland (Stalin wanted pro-Soviet government)
  • July 1945 Potsdam: Truman (knew about atomic bomb), Attlee (post-election), Stalin — increased suspicion; agreed on demilitarisation, denazification, democratisation of Germany
  • March 1946 Iron Curtain Speech: Churchill — "From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic, an iron curtain has descended"

Containment (1947–1955)

  • March 1947 Truman Doctrine: US committed to support free peoples resisting subjugation — Greece and Turkey first recipients
  • June 1947 Marshall Plan: $13 billion economic aid to rebuild Western Europe; 16 countries accepted; USSR rejected it for Eastern Europe (Cominform response)
  • June 1948–May 1949 Berlin Blockade: Stalin cut land access to West Berlin; Western powers airlifted supplies (277,000 flights); Stalin lifted blockade; NATO formed in response
  • April 1949 NATO: 12 founding members; Article 5 — attack on one = attack on all
  • 1949 COMECON: Soviet economic bloc in response to Marshall Plan

Confrontation (1955–1962)

  • 1955 Warsaw Pact: Soviet military alliance in response to West German rearmament and NATO
  • 1956 Hungarian Uprising: Nagy announced withdrawal from Warsaw Pact; Soviet tanks crushed rebellion (3,000 killed); West did not intervene (Suez distraction)
  • 1961 Berlin Wall: Stopped refugee flow (3.5 million had fled East); symbolic division of Cold War
  • 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis: Closest to nuclear war; Khrushchev placed missiles in Cuba; US naval blockade; secret deal (missiles removed from Cuba + Turkey); Khrushchev weakened

Détente (1969–1979)

  • 1972 SALT I: Limited anti-ballistic missiles; froze ICBM numbers
  • 1975 Helsinki Accords: Recognised post-war borders; committed to human rights (used by dissidents like Charter 77)
  • 1979 SALT II: Further arms limitations (never ratified due to Afghanistan)
  • Reasons for détente: Nuclear parity (MAD); economic costs of arms race; Sino-Soviet split (US could play China against USSR); Ostpolitik (Brandt's Eastern diplomacy)

Second Cold War & End (1979–1991)

  • 1979 Afghanistan Invasion: USSR intervened; US boycotted 1980 Moscow Olympics; funded mujahideen (Stinger missiles)
  • 1983 Strategic Defence Initiative (SDI): Reagan's "Star Wars" — threatened to make MAD obsolete
  • 1985 Gorbachev: Glasnost (openness) + Perestroika (restructuring); sought to reform USSR, not end it
  • 1989 Fall of Berlin Wall (9 Nov): Symbolic end of division; Hungary opened border; mass demonstrations in East Germany
  • 1990 German Reunification: Kohl's 10-point plan; "2+4 Treaty" — NATO membership resolved
  • Dec 1991 USSR Dissolved: Gorbachev resigned; Commonwealth of Independent States formed

📚 Historiography — Why Did the Cold War End?

  • Reagan Victory School: Military buildup (SDI) bankrupted USSR; pressure forced reform.
  • Gorbachev Factor: His choices mattered — he could have used force (like in Hungary 1956). Personal agency was decisive.
  • Structural Weakness: Soviet economy couldn't compete; Afghanistan drain; Eastern European satellites unsustainable; nationalism in republics.
  • People Power: Mass movements in Eastern Europe (Solidarity in Poland, demonstrations in East Germany) made Soviet control impossible.

✍️ Essay Framework: "To what extent was Gorbachev responsible for the end of the Cold War?"

FOR: Glasnost and Perestroika undermined Communist control; refused to use force in Eastern Europe (unlike Brezhnev Doctrine); withdrew from Afghanistan; dealt with Reagan on arms reduction (INF Treaty 1987).

AGAINST: Reagan's military pressure forced Soviet concessions; structural economic problems made status quo unsustainable; Eastern European populations rejected communism independently; Afghanistan weakened USSR before Gorbachev.

CONCLUSION: Gorbachev was the catalyst — his choices (not using force, seeking reform) made collapse possible. But long-term structural factors created conditions. He accelerated inevitable change.

📘 H4: The Cold War in Asia, 1945–1991

Chinese Communist Revolution

  • 1945–49 Civil War: CCP (Mao) vs GMD (Chiang Kai-shek)
  • CCP strengths: Land reform won peasant support; guerrilla warfare; disciplined organisation; Manchurian industrial base
  • GMD weaknesses: Corruption; inflation; reliance on US aid; alienated intellectuals and middle class
  • Oct 1949 People's Republic of China proclaimed; Chiang fled to Taiwan
  • 1950 Sino-Soviet Treaty of Friendship; USSR provided loans and advisors

Korean War (1950–1953)

  • June 1950 North Korea invaded South (Kim Il-sung, authorised by Stalin)
  • Sept 1950 Inchon Landing — MacArthur turned the war; advanced to Yalu River
  • Oct 1950 China entered war (300,000 "volunteers") — feared US on border
  • 1951–53 Stalemate around 38th parallel; armistice signed (no peace treaty)
  • Consequences: Korea permanently divided; US containment extended to Asia; militarised Cold War; US-Japan alliance strengthened; China isolated from West until 1970s

Vietnam War (1954–1975)

  • 1954 Geneva Accords: Vietnam divided at 17th parallel; elections promised (never held)
  • 1954–63 Diem Regime: South Vietnam — corrupt, Catholic minority rule; Buddhist monks self-immolated
  • 1964 Gulf of Tonkin Incident: US claimed North Vietnamese attacked destroyers (disputed); Congress authorised escalation
  • 1965 US combat troops deployed; 500,000 by 1968
  • 1968 Tet Offensive: Viet Cong attacked 100 cities; military defeat but psychological victory — turned US public opinion
  • 1969–72 Vietnamisation — US withdrew troops; bombing of Cambodia
  • 1973 Paris Peace Accords — US withdrew
  • 1975 North Vietnamese victory; Saigon fell (30 April)

Other Asian Cold Wars

  • Malayan Emergency (1948–60): British defeated communist insurgents; hearts and minds strategy
  • Indonesian Confrontation (1963–66): Sukarno opposed Malaysia; Suharto coup (1965) — anti-communist purge (500,000 killed)
  • Afghanistan (1979–89): USSR invasion to support communist government; US funded mujahideen; USSR withdrew (1989)

✍️ Essay Framework: "Why did the USA fail in Vietnam?"

MILITARY: Guerrilla warfare — Viet Cong controlled countryside; Ho Chi Minh Trail sustained supplies; US superior firepower ineffective against insurgency.

POLITICAL: South Vietnamese governments were unpopular and corrupt; lack of legitimacy; Vietnamisation failed to build capable ARVN.

DOMESTIC: Tet Offensive (1968) shattered credibility; media coverage ("living room war"); anti-war movement; My Lai massacre (1968).

STRATEGIC: Limited war — US feared Chinese intervention (like Korea); couldn't invade North; bombing ineffective.

CONCLUSION: Military power could not overcome political legitimacy deficit. US underestimated Vietnamese nationalism.

📘 H5: Civil Rights in the USA, 1863–1968

Reconstruction & Jim Crow (1863–1954)

  • 1863 Emancipation Proclamation; 13th Amendment (1865) abolished slavery
  • 1865–77 Reconstruction: 14th Amendment (citizenship); 15th Amendment (voting rights); Freedmen's Bureau
  • 1877 End of Reconstruction — federal troops withdrawn from South
  • 1896 Plessy v Ferguson: "Separate but equal" legalised segregation
  • Jim Crow laws: Segregated schools, transport, facilities; literacy tests, poll taxes, grandfather clauses disenfranchised Black voters
  • NAACP founded 1909: Legal strategy to challenge segregation

The Civil Rights Movement (1954–1968)

  • 1954 Brown v Board of Education: Supreme Court ruled school segregation unconstitutional; overturned Plessy
  • 1955–56 Montgomery Bus Boycott: Rosa Parks arrested; MLK emerged as leader; 381-day boycott; Supreme Court banned bus segregation
  • 1957 Little Rock Nine: Eisenhower sent federal troops to enforce school desegregation
  • 1960 Sit-ins: Greensboro, NC — students protested segregated lunch counters
  • 1961 Freedom Rides: Testing interstate bus segregation (illegal since 1946 Morgan v Virginia); riders attacked in Alabama
  • 1963 Birmingham Campaign: "Letter from Birmingham Jail"; police dogs and fire hoses on children — televised; Kennedy proposed Civil Rights Act
  • 28 Aug 1963 March on Washington: 250,000 people; MLK's "I Have a Dream" speech
  • 1964 Civil Rights Act: Banned discrimination in employment and public accommodations
  • 1965 Selma to Montgomery March: "Bloody Sunday" (7 March) — police attacked marchers; Voting Rights Act passed
  • 1968 MLK assassinated (Memphis)

Black Power & Radicalisation

  • Malcolm X: Nation of Islam; advocated self-defence and Black separatism; assassinated 1965
  • Stokely Carmichael: Coined "Black Power" (1966); SNCC shifted from integration to separatism
  • Black Panthers (1966): Huey Newton and Bobby Seale; armed self-defence; community programmes (free breakfast, health clinics)
  • Impact: Shifted movement from integration to empowerment; frightened white moderates; FBI COINTELPRO targeted activists

✍️ Essay Framework: "How successful was the Civil Rights Movement by 1968?"

SUCCESSES: Civil Rights Act 1964 (ended legal segregation); Voting Rights Act 1965 (enfranchisement); 14th/15th Amendments finally enforced; shift in public opinion; Supreme Court support.

LIMITATIONS: Economic inequality persisted (de facto segregation in housing, schools); Northern ghettos remained impoverished; riots in Watts (1965), Detroit (1967); Black Power alienated white support; assassinations (MLK, Malcolm X).

CONCLUSION: Legal and political rights were achieved but social and economic equality remained elusive. Movement succeeded in dismantling Jim Crow but not structural racism.

📘 H6: International History, 1945–1991

Decolonisation

  • India (1947): Mountbatten Plan — partition into India and Pakistan; 1 million died in communal violence
  • Palestine (1948): UN Partition Plan; Israel declared independence; Arab-Israeli War; 700,000 Palestinian refugees
  • Suez Crisis (1956): Nasser nationalised canal; Britain, France, Israel invaded; US and USSR forced withdrawal — end of British imperial power
  • Congo (1960): Lumumba assassinated; UN intervention; civil war and secession of Katanga
  • Algeria (1954–62): Brutal war of independence; de Gaulle granted independence; 1 million European settlers fled

Non-Aligned Movement

  • 1955 Bandung Conference: 29 Asian-African nations; Nehru, Nasser, Tito, Sukarno
  • 1961 Belgrade Summit: Formal founding of NAM
  • Principles: Non-alignment with either bloc; anti-colonialism; peaceful coexistence; economic development

Arab-Israeli Conflicts

  • 1948 First Arab-Israeli War — Israel victorious; expanded territory
  • 1956 Suez Crisis — Israel invaded Sinai; withdrew under superpower pressure
  • 1967 Six-Day War: Israel launched preemptive strike; captured Sinai, Gaza, West Bank, Golan Heights; 250,000 more Palestinian refugees
  • 1973 Yom Kippur War: Egypt and Syria attacked on Jewish holy day; initial Arab successes; Israel recovered; led to oil crisis
  • 1979 Camp David Accords: Egypt-Israel peace; Sadat and Begin; Egypt expelled from Arab League

Iran-Iraq War (1980–1988)

  • Saddam Hussein invaded Iran (Sept 1980) — feared Iranian Revolution spreading to Shia majority in Iraq
  • Stalemated trench warfare; chemical weapons used; 1 million deaths
  • UN ceasefire (1988) — no territorial changes

📘 H7: The French Revolution, 1774–1794

Ancien Régime (1774–1789)

  • Louis XVI: Absolute monarchy; divine right; no constitution
  • Three Estates: First (clergy, 0.5%), Second (nobility, 1.5%), Third (commoners, 98% — bourgeoisie, urban workers, peasants)
  • Privilege: First and Second exempt from taille (main tax); Third carried tax burden
  • Financial crisis: Wars (Seven Years' War, American Revolution) = massive debt; Calonne's reforms failed (1787)
  • Social crisis: Bad harvests 1787–88; bread prices soared; population pressure

1789: Revolution Begins

  • May 1789 Estates-General called — first since 1614; dispute over voting (by estate vs by head)
  • 17 June Third Estate declared National Assembly
  • 20 June Tennis Court Oath: Pledged not to disband until constitution written
  • 14 July Storming of the Bastille: Popular uprising; symbol of royal tyranny destroyed
  • July–Aug Great Fear: Peasant uprisings; châteaux attacked
  • 4 Aug Abolition of Feudalism: National Assembly ended feudal dues and privileges
  • 26 Aug Declaration of Rights of Man: Liberty, property, security, resistance to oppression; equality before law; freedom of speech and religion

Constitutional Monarchy (1789–1792)

  • 1791 Constitution: Constitutional monarchy; separation of powers; active vs passive citizenship (property qualification)
  • Civil Constitution of the Clergy (July 1790): Bishops elected by citizens; priests salaried by state; Pope condemned it — divided France
  • June 1791 Flight to Varennes: Louis XVI attempted to flee; captured; discredited monarchy
  • April 1792 France declared war on Austria — fear of counter-revolution

Republic & Terror (1792–1794)

  • 10 Aug 1792 Insurrection of sans-culottes; stormed Tuileries; royal family imprisoned
  • Sept 1792 September Massacres: Prisoners killed by Paris mobs
  • Sept 1792 National Convention abolished monarchy; declared republic
  • 21 Jan 1793 Louis XVI executed
  • 1793 Committee of Public Safety: Robespierre dominant; Terror to defend revolution
  • 1793–94 The Terror: 17,000 executed (guillotine); Law of Suspects; sans-culottes controlled Paris
  • 27 July 1794 Thermidorian Reaction: Robespierre arrested and executed; Terror ended
  • 1795 Directory: Five-man executive; corrupt and unstable; paved way for Napoleon

📚 Historiography — What Caused the Revolution?

  • Marxist: Bourgeoisie revolution — rising capitalist class overthrew feudal aristocracy. Critique: Many bourgeois were nobles; social mobility existed.
  • Cobban (1960s): Not a social revolution but a political one — dispute over power, not class conflict.
  • Tocqueville: Centralisation under absolute monarchy weakened local institutions; when monarchy fell, nothing replaced it — chaos.
  • Recent: Cultural/ideological factors — Enlightenment ideas, public opinion, print culture created revolutionary mentality.

✍️ Essay Framework: "How revolutionary was the French Revolution?"

REVOLUTIONARY: Abolished feudalism and privilege; Declaration of Rights; constitutional government; metric system; secular state; citizen army (levée en masse); popular sovereignty.

LIMITED: Property still dominated; women gained little (Olympe de Gouges executed); slavery reintroduced in colonies (Napoleon); Napoleon restored authoritarian rule (1804).

CONCLUSION: Politically revolutionary — destroyed Ancien Régime and established principles of modern democracy. Socially less transformative — new elite replaced old.

📘 H8: Hitler's Germany, 1929–1947

Weimar Weakness (1929–1933)

  • Treaty of Versailles: War guilt clause (Article 231); reparations; territorial losses; military restrictions — deeply resented
  • Wall Street Crash (1929): US loans recalled; German economy collapsed; unemployment reached 6 million (1932)
  • Political instability: Coalition governments collapsed; extremist parties grew
  • Nazi rise: 2.6% (1928) → 18.3% (1930) → 37.3% (July 1932) → 33.1% (Nov 1932) — largest party but never majority

Hitler's Appointment & Consolidation

  • 30 Jan 1933 Hitler appointed Chancellor (von Papen believed he could control Hitler)
  • 27 Feb 1933 Reichstag Fire: Communist blamed; Decree for Protection of People and State — suspended civil liberties
  • March 1933 Enabling Act: Gave Hitler dictatorial powers for 4 years; passed with Centre Party support
  • June 1934 Night of the Long Knives: SA leaders (Röhm) purged; SS (Himmler) became dominant
  • Aug 1934 Hindenburg died; Hitler combined Chancellor and President — Führer

Nazi State (1933–1945)

  • Gleichschaltung: Coordination of all aspects of society — trade unions banned (May 1933); political parties banned; civil service purged of Jews and critics
  • Propaganda: Goebbels; control of press, radio, cinema; rallies; Hitler myth
  • Terror: Gestapo (secret police); SS; concentration camps (Dachau 1933); informants
  • Education: Nazi curriculum; Hitler Youth (compulsory 1936); indoctrination
  • Church: Concordat with Vatican (1933); later attacked (Confessing Church opposition)
  • Women: Kinder, Küche, Kirche; loans for staying home; abortion restricted

Economic Policy

  • Public works: Autobahns; rearmament (concealed from 1933; open from 1935)
  • Rearmament: Key to recovery; military spending created jobs
  • Four Year Plan (1936): Göring; autarky (self-sufficiency); preparation for war
  • Strengths: Unemployment fell; public morale improved; military power restored
  • Weaknesses: Debt; dependence on plunder ( Austria, Czechoslovakia); wartime economy unsustainable

Foreign Policy (1933–1939)

  • 1933 Withdrew from League of Nations and Disarmament Conference
  • 1935 Rearmament announced; Saar returned (plebiscite)
  • 1936 Remilitarised Rhineland (broken Versailles); Rome-Berlin Axis
  • 1938 Anschluss: Austria annexed
  • Sept 1938 Munich Agreement: Sudetenland to Germany; Chamberlain's "peace for our time"
  • March 1939 Occupied rest of Czechoslovakia — broke Munich; Britain guaranteed Poland
  • Aug 1939 Nazi-Soviet Pact: Non-aggression + secret protocol dividing Poland and Baltics
  • 1 Sept 1939 Invaded Poland — WWII began

✍️ Essay Framework: "How did Hitler consolidate power by 1934?"

LEGAL METHODS: Enabling Act (used constitution to end democracy); Reichstag elections (largest party); presidential decree powers.

TERROR: Reichstag Fire → emergency powers; SA violence against opponents; concentration camps; Night of Long Knives (eliminated internal threat).

POPULAR SUPPORT: Economic recovery promised; Hitler myth (propaganda); fear of communism; nationalist appeal.

ELITE SUPPORT: Industrialists funded Nazis; army supported (after SA purge); von Papen and conservatives believed they could control Hitler.

CONCLUSION: Combination of legal manoeuvring, terror against opponents, and support from elites and population. Hitler used the system to destroy it.

📘 H9: Stalin's Russia, 1924–1953

Power Struggle (1924–1929)

  • Lenin died (Jan 1924): No clear successor; Testament criticised Stalin but was suppressed
  • Trotsky: Brilliant orator; hero of Civil War; advocated permanent revolution; arrogant; Jewish background alienated some
  • Stalin: General Secretary (appointed 1922) — controlled party apparatus; built alliances; "Socialism in One Country" appealed to nationalism
  • Zinoviev and Kamenev: Initially allied with Stalin against Trotsky; later expelled
  • Bukharin: Right-wing; supported NEP; opposed by Stalin
  • 1929 Stalin expelled Trotsky; undisputed leader

Collectivisation (1929–1940)

  • Goal: Grain for export (to buy machinery); eliminate kulaks (prosperous peasants); socialist agriculture
  • Process: Forced merger of farms into collective farms (kolkhoz); state Machine Tractor Stations controlled equipment
  • Kulaks: "Dekulakisation" — 5 million deported to labour camps; many died
  • Resistance: Peasants slaughtered livestock rather than surrender; grain destroyed
  • Famine (1932–33): Ukraine (Holodomor) — estimated 4–7 million died; Kazakhstan — 1.5 million died (38% of population)
  • Results: Grain production fell initially; recovered by late 1930s; peasants reduced to rural proletariat

Five Year Plans (1928–1941)

  • First Plan (1928–32): Heavy industry — coal, steel, electricity, machinery; Magnitogorsk and Dnieper Dam
  • Second Plan (1933–37): Continued industrialisation; transport; some consumer goods
  • Third Plan (1938–41): Defence industry — interrupted by war
  • Results: Industrial output quadrupled; USSR became 2nd largest industrial power; but quality poor; targets met by falsifying figures

The Great Terror (1936–38)

  • Show Trials: Zinoviev, Kamenev, Bukharin, Trotsky (in absentia) confessed to absurd charges (sabotage, spying) — forced by torture/threats to family
  • Yezhovshchina: Secret police (NKVD) under Yezhov arrested 1.5 million; 700,000 executed
  • Red Army purged: 75% of officers arrested/executed; weakened USSR for WWII
  • Causes: Stalin's paranoia; need for scapegoats for hardships; elimination of Old Bolsheviks; securing total control before war

WWII & Post-War

  • 1941 Operation Barbarossa — Germany invaded; Stalin initially disbelieved intelligence
  • Great Patriotic War: Scorched earth; relocation of industry east; Leningrad siege (900 days; 1 million dead)
  • 1942–43 Stalingrad — turning point; 2 million casualties
  • 1945 USSR reached Berlin; 27 million Soviet deaths in WWII
  • Post-war: Rebuilt industry; tightened control; new purges (Leningrad Affair, Doctors' Plot); Cold War began

✍️ Essay Framework: "How did Stalin transform the USSR?"

ECONOMIC: Collectivisation destroyed peasant independence but secured grain; Five Year Plans created heavy industry from nothing; USSR became superpower — but at enormous human cost (famine, labour camps).

POLITICAL: One-party totalitarian state; cult of personality; Terror eliminated all opposition; Constitution 1936 was façade — real power with Stalin and NKVD.

SOCIAL: Education and literacy improved; women entered workforce; social mobility (workers could rise); but fear permeated society; religion suppressed; culture controlled.

CONCLUSION: Stalin transformed USSR from agricultural backwardness to industrial superpower in one generation — but transformation was achieved through terror, coercion, and mass death. Progress at an inhuman cost.

📘 H10: Britain, 1918–1951

Post-WW1 Challenges (1918–1924)

  • Lloyd George: "Man who won the war"; Coalition government; promises of "homes fit for heroes"
  • Economy: War debt to USA; return to Gold Standard (1925) at pre-war parity — overvalued pound hurt exports
  • Unemployment: 1 million by 1921; staple industries (coal, textiles, shipbuilding) in decline
  • General Strike (1926): Miners' strike spread to transport, printing, steel; TUC called it off after 9 days; miners held out for 6 months but lost
  • Trade Disputes Act (1927): Made general strikes illegal; restricted trade union funding of Labour Party

Interwar Politics

  • Ramsay MacDonald: First Labour PM (1924, 1929–31); National Government (1931–35) — coalition with Conservatives; expelled from Labour Party
  • National Government (1931–40): Dominance of Conservatives; austerity policies; abandoned Gold Standard (1931)
  • Appeasement: Hoare-Laval Pact (1935); Rhineland remilitarisation (1936) — no action; Anschluss (1938); Munich (1938)

WWII & Post-War

  • Churchill (1940–45): Coalition war leader; "blood, toil, tears and sweat"; Battle of Britain; Blitz
  • Labour Landslide (1945): Attlee; promised welfare state, full employment, nationalisation
  • Beveridge Report (1942): Blueprint for welfare state — attack on "Five Giants" (want, disease, ignorance, squalor, idleness)
  • Key reforms: NHS (1948) — Bevan; National Insurance; Education Act 1944 (Butler); nationalisation of coal, rail, steel, Bank of England

✍️ Essay Framework: "Why did Labour win the 1945 election?"

WAR EXPERIENCE: Evacuation exposed poverty; Beveridge Report raised expectations; people wanted change not return to 1930s.

LABOUR STRENGTHS: Effective in coalition; credible on reconstruction; grassroots organisation; Attlee's quiet competence vs Churchill's wartime rhetoric.

CONSERVATIVE WEAKNESSES: Churchill's "Gestapo" comment alienated voters; association with interwar unemployment and appeasement; weak organisation.

CONCLUSION: War transformed attitudes; voters wanted social security and planning — Labour offered this. Churchill was admired as war leader but not trusted for peace.

📘 H11: The Origins of the Civil War in the USA

Slavery & Sectional Tension (1820–1850)

  • 1820 Missouri Compromise: Slavery banned north of 36°30′ (except Missouri); maintained balance (11 free, 11 slave states)
  • 1831 Nat Turner's Rebellion: Virginia; 55 whites killed; harsh slave codes tightened
  • 1845 Texas annexed; Mexican-American War (1846–48)
  • 1850 Compromise: California free state; Fugitive Slave Act strengthened; popular sovereignty in Utah/New Mexico territories

Escalation (1850–1860)

  • 1852 Uncle Tom's Cabin — galvanised Northern opinion
  • 1854 Kansas-Nebraska Act: Popular sovereignty → "Bleeding Kansas"; repealed Missouri Compromise
  • 1857 Dred Scott Decision: Supreme Court ruled Congress could not ban slavery in territories; African Americans could not be citizens
  • 1859 John Brown's Raid: Harper's Ferry; executed as martyr by North, terrorist by South
  • 1860 Lincoln elected (no Southern states); South Carolina seceded (Dec 1860)

Secession & War (1860–1861)

  • 1861 7 states seceded before Lincoln's inauguration; Confederate States formed (Jefferson Davis)
  • Fort Sumter (April 1861): Confederate attack began war; 4 more states seceded
  • North advantages: Population (22m vs 9m); industry; railroads; navy; established government
  • South advantages: Defensive war; military tradition (Lee, Jackson); cotton (hoped for British support)

✍️ Essay Framework: "Was slavery the main cause of the Civil War?"

YES: All disputes (Missouri Compromise, Kansas-Nebraska, Dred Scott) concerned slavery's expansion; Confederate constitution explicitly protected slavery; Lincoln's opposition to expansion triggered secession; slavery was the cornerstone of Southern economy and society.

NO / OTHER FACTORS: States' rights (but states' rights to do what? = protect slavery); economic differences (industrial North vs agricultural South); tariff disputes; Southern honour and culture; failure of political compromise.

CONCLUSION: Slavery was the fundamental cause — all other factors were connected to it. Without slavery, there would have been no secession.

📘 H12: International Relations, 1871–1945

Bismarck's System (1871–1890)

  • Goal: Isolate France; maintain peace favourable to Germany
  • 1873 Three Emperors' League: Germany, Austria-Hungary, Russia
  • 1879 Dual Alliance: Germany + Austria-Hungary
  • 1881 Three Emperors' League renewed: Secret agreements on Balkans
  • 1882 Triple Alliance: + Italy
  • 1887 Reinsurance Treaty: Germany + Russia — neutrality if other attacked
  • 1890 Wilhelm II dismissed Bismarck; refused to renew Reinsurance Treaty

New Imperialism (1880s–1914)

  • 1884–85 Berlin Conference: Divided Africa; no African representatives; "effective occupation" principle
  • Motives: Economic (raw materials, markets); strategic (naval bases); national prestige; Social Darwinism; missionary
  • Scramble for Africa: Belgium (Congo Free State — Leopold II's brutal rule); Britain (Egypt, Sudan, South Africa); France (West Africa); Germany (East and Southwest Africa)
  • Asia: British India expanded; French Indochina; Dutch East Indies; US Philippines (1898)

Interwar Period (1919–1939)

  • 1919 League of Nations: Wilson's ideal; US never joined; collective security principle
  • 1920s Successes: Aaland Islands (1920); Upper Silesia (1921); Greece-Bulgaria (1925); Mandates system
  • 1930s Failures: Manchuria (1931–33) — Japan invaded; Lytton Commission; Japan left League; Abyssinia (1935–36) — Italy invaded; Hoare-Laval Pact betrayal; both left League discredited
  • Why League failed: No US; no armed force; unanimity required; great powers put interests above collective security; appeasement (Munich 1938 — dealt without League)

Path to WWII

  • 1933 Hitler withdrew from League and Disarmament Conference
  • 1935 Rearmament; Stresa Front (Britain, France, Italy) — weak response
  • 1936 Rhineland remilitarised — no action
  • 1938 Anschluss; Munich Agreement (appeasement)
  • March 1939 Czechoslovakia occupied — Britain guaranteed Poland
  • Aug 1939 Nazi-Soviet Pact
  • 1 Sept 1939 Invasion of Poland — WWII

✍️ Essay Framework: "Why did the League of Nations fail?"

STRUCTURAL WEAKNESSES: No US membership; no armed force of its own; decisions required unanimity; no enforcement mechanism; Covenant was vague on sanctions.

GREAT POWER FAILURE: Britain and France prioritised national interest over collective security; appeasement (Manchuria, Abyssinia, Munich); unwilling to use force; Depression diverted attention to domestic issues.

AGGRESSOR POWERS: Japan, Italy, Germany left League and ignored it; no deterrent effect; aggressors coordinated (Rome-Berlin Axis, Anti-Comintern Pact).

CONCLUSION: League failed because great powers never committed to collective security. It was designed as a forum but expected to act as a policeman without a police force.

📝 Universal A-Level History Exam Skills

Essay Structure (45 minutes)

  1. Introduction (5 min): Define key terms; state your line of argument; outline the main factors you'll discuss
  2. Paragraphs (30 min): PEEL structure — Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link back to question
  3. Conclusion (5 min): Summarise argument; make a judgement; explain significance; avoid introducing new material
  4. Balance: At least 2 factors compared; don't just list — explain WHY each factor matters

Source Analysis (30 minutes)

  • Content: What does the source say? What information does it provide?
  • Provenance: WHO wrote it? WHEN? WHY? What is their perspective/position?
  • Reliability: Is it trustworthy? What biases? Compare with other sources/your knowledge.
  • Utility: How useful for a specific enquiry? What can it tell us? What are its limits?

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • ❌ Narrative instead of analysis — don't just tell the story; explain CAUSATION and SIGNIFICANCE
  • ❌ Vague generalisations — use SPECIFIC dates, names, statistics
  • ❌ One-sided argument — address counter-arguments
  • ❌ No conclusion — always make a judgement
  • ❌ Ignoring the question — answer what is ASKED, not what you know
