📘 Subject Context

The French Revolution, 1774–1794 is a significant area of study in IGCSE History. Understanding the causes, events, and consequences of historical developments is essential for constructing analytical essays and source-based answers.

IGCSE History examines knowledge, source analysis, and essay-writing skills. Paper 1 tests depth studies through structured questions; Paper 2 tests breadth studies through source analysis and essays. Always use specific evidence.

The French Revolution, 1774–1794

Cambridge 9389 A-Level / 0470 IGCSE

📘 IGCSE Content

France in 1774: Ancien Régime

  • Absolute monarchy under Louis XVI (r. 1774–92). Divine right; no constitution.
  • Three Estates: First (clergy, 0.5%), Second (nobility, 1.5%), Third (commoners, 98%). Third Estate included bourgeoisie, urban workers, and peasants.
  • Privilege system: First and Second Estates exempt from most taxation (taille).
  • Financial crisis: wars (Seven Years' War, American Revolution) left massive debt. Calonne's reforms failed (1787).
  • Bad harvests 1787–88; bread prices soared; population pressure.

From Estates-General to National Assembly (1789)

  • Estates-General called (May 1789) — first since 1614. Dispute over voting: by estate (favours First/Second) or by head (favours Third).
  • Third Estate declared itself National Assembly (17 June 1789); Tennis Court Oath (20 June) — pledged not to disband until constitution written.
  • Storming of the Bastille (14 July 1789): popular uprising in Paris; symbol of royal tyranny destroyed.
  • Great Fear (July–Aug 1789): peasant uprisings across France; châteaux attacked.
  • Abolition of feudalism (4 Aug 1789): National Assembly ended feudal dues and privileges.

The Constitutional Monarchy (1789–92)

  • Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (26 Aug 1789): liberty, property, security, resistance to oppression; equality before law; freedom of speech and religion.
  • Constitution of 1791: constitutional monarchy; separation of powers; active vs. passive citizenship (property qualification).
  • Financial reforms: confiscation and sale of Church lands (assignats as currency); Civil Constitution of the Clergy (July 1790) — bishops elected by citizens, priests salaried by state. Pope condemned it; divided France.
  • Flight to Varennes (June 1791): Louis XVI attempted to flee; captured; discredited monarchy.
  • Champ de Mars massacre (July 1791): National Guard fired on republican petitioners.

War and Radicalisation (1792–93)

  • April 1792: France declared war on Austria (and soon Prussia). Fear of counter-revolution and émigré armies.
  • Brunswick Manifesto (July 1792): Allied commander threatened destruction of Paris if royal family harmed. Inflamed radicals.
  • 10 Aug 1792: Insurrection of sans-culottes; stormed Tuileries Palace; royal family imprisoned.
  • September Massacres (Sept 1792): prisoners killed by Paris mobs.
  • National Convention (Sept 1792): abolished monarchy; declared republic.
  • Battle of Valmy (20 Sept 1792): French victory; saved revolution.
  • Trial and execution of Louis XVI (Jan 1793): guillotined 21 Jan 1793.

The Terror (1793–94)

  • Crisis: war (Britain, Spain, Netherlands joined coalition), Vendée revolt (royalist/Catholic uprising), federalist revolts, economic chaos (assignats depreciated).
  • Committee of Public Safety (established April 1793): dominated by Robespierre, Saint-Just, Couthon.
  • Reign of Terror (Sept 1793–July 1794): Revolutionary Tribunal; Law of Suspects; 17,000–40,000 executed.
  • Dechristianisation: churches closed, Gregorian calendar replaced, Cult of Reason/Cult of the Supreme Being.
  • Thermidorian Reaction (27 July 1794): Robespierre arrested and executed (9 Thermidor Year II). End of Terror.

📗 AS Level Content

Causes of the Revolution: Long and Short Term

  • Political: Absolute monarchy increasingly dysfunctional; parlements (especially Paris) resisted royal edicts. Louis XVI indecisive; Marie Antoinette unpopular ("Madame Déficit").
  • Social: Rise of bourgeoisie — wealthy, educated, but excluded from political power and noble status. Noble reaction: some nobles sought to claw back privileges, creating tension.
  • Economic: Debt from wars; inefficient tax system; physiocratic ideas; subsistence crises.
  • Intellectual: Enlightenment — Voltaire (toleration), Rousseau (popular sovereignty, social contract), Montesquieu (separation of powers). Salons and pamphlets spread ideas.
  • Immediate triggers: Bankruptcy, calling of Estates-General, bad harvests.

The Role of Key Groups

  • Bourgeoisie: Led early revolution (Sieyès, What is the Third Estate? 1789). Dominated National Assembly and Convention. Sought legal equality and constitutional government.
  • Sans-culottes: Urban workers and artisans. Demanded price controls, direct democracy, and violent action against counter-revolution. Influenced radical phases (1792–94).
  • Peasants: Drove abolition of feudalism (Great Fear). Supported revolution when it brought land; turned against it when Church attacked and conscription imposed.
  • Women: October Days (1789) — march to Versailles; Olympe de Gouges (Declaration of the Rights of Woman, 1791); excluded from political rights in 1791 Constitution; Republic of Virtue gave them some legal equality but no vote.

From Reform to Terror: Why Did Radicalisation Occur?

  • External war: revolutionaries felt surrounded by enemies; created siege mentality.
  • Internal opposition: Vendée, federalist revolts, royalist plots. Convinced revolutionaries that only terror could save the republic.
  • Economic crisis: assignat inflation, food shortages. Sans-culottes demanded Maximum (price controls, Sept 1793).
  • Ideological: Rousseau's concept of general will justified coercion in name of virtue. Robespierre believed terror was necessary to establish virtue.
  • Political conflict: factional struggle — Girondins vs. Montagnards; purge of Girondins (June 1793); Hébertists and Indulgents also purged.

📙 A2 Level Content

Historiography of the Revolution

  • Marxist (Georges Lefebvre, Albert Soboul): Revolution was bourgeois revolution — transition from feudalism to capitalism. Third Estate led by bourgeoisie overthrew feudal aristocracy. Class struggle central.
  • Revisionist (Alfred Cobban, François Furet): Revolution was not bourgeois in modern sense. Many leaders were nobles or office-holders (robins). Bourgeoisie already economically dominant before 1789. Revolution was political, not social, in origin.
  • Cultural/linguistic turn (Furet, Mona Ozouf): Revolution created modern political culture — nationalism, citizenship, ideology. Language and symbolism were as important as material interests.
  • Tocquevillian (Alexis de Tocqueville): Revolution did not break with past but accelerated centralising trends begun under absolute monarchy. Old regime already modernising.

The Terror: Necessity or Ideological Excess?

  • Defence: Republic was in mortal danger; terror saved revolution from internal and external enemies. War justified emergency measures.
  • Critique: Terror was counter-productive — alienated peasantry (dechristianisation), provoked more resistance, consumed revolutionaries themselves. Robespierre's virtue became tyranny.
  • Comparative: Similar patterns in 20th-century revolutions (Russian, Chinese) — radicalisation under siege, purges of original revolutionaries.

Women and the Revolution

  • Olympe de Gouges challenged male-centric rights. Revolutionary clubs for women (Society of Revolutionary Republican Women, 1793) — banned Oct 1793.
  • Paradox: revolution proclaimed universal rights but excluded women from political participation. Rousseau's domestic ideology influenced republican model.
  • Legacy: Napoleon Code (1804) reinforced patriarchal family structure. Women's political rights not won until 1944 in France.

Essay Frameworks

  • "The French Revolution was caused by the bourgeoisie": Use Marxist interpretation, rise of capitalist economy, Third Estate demands. Counter with Cobban/Furet — bourgeoisie not a class, nobles led revolution.
  • "War caused the Terror": External threats, internal revolt, siege mentality. Counter: terror also driven by ideology (Rousseau, virtue) and internal factionalism.
  • "The Revolution achieved its aims by 1791": Constitutional monarchy, Declaration of Rights, end of feudalism. Counter: monarchy fell, Terror followed, instability until Napoleon.

📜 Worked Example

Question: To what extent was Germany responsible for WWI?

Essay Plan:
FOR: Blank cheque, Schlieffen Plan, naval expansion, Fischer thesis
AGAINST: Austrian ultimatum, Russian mobilisation, alliance system, Clark "sleepwalkers"
Conclusion: Significant responsibility but not sole blame
Writing narrative instead of analysis. History essays need CAUSATION — explain WHY and SIGNIFICANCE.
Always use SPECIFIC EVIDENCE — dates, names, statistics. "On 28 June 1914" not "in 1914".

⚡ Quick Recall

  • Long-term: Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism
  • Short-term: Assassination of Franz Ferdinand
  • Treaty of Versailles: War guilt, reparations, territorial losses
  • Cold War: Containment, Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan

Source Analysis Framework

  • Content: What does the source say? What information does it provide?
  • Provenance: Who wrote it? When? Why? What is their perspective?
  • Reliability: Is it trustworthy? What biases might exist?
  • Utility: How useful is it for a specific enquiry?

Essay Structure

  • Introduction: Define key terms, set out argument/line of reasoning
  • Paragraphs: Point → Evidence → Explanation → Link back to question
  • Synthesis: Compare factors, weigh importance, show interconnections
  • Conclusion: Judgment based on evidence, avoid introducing new points
Always answer the EXACT question asked. Underline key words ("To what extent", "How far", "Most important") and address them directly.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Failing to reach a clear judgement in essay questions — your conclusion should answer the question directly.
  • Forgetting to link back to the question in each paragraph — every point should support your argument.
  • Forgetting to evaluate sources for reliability and usefulness in Paper 2 questions.
  • Writing narrative instead of analysis — history essays need causation and significance, not just chronology.
  • Describing a source instead of analysing it — explain what it shows and why it matters.

✅ Key Points to Remember

  • Practise writing timed essays — time management is crucial in the examination.
  • Understand the difference between causes (why it happened) and consequences (what resulted).
  • Know the difference between short-term and long-term causes and consequences.
  • Always plan your essay before writing — a clear structure improves coherence and argumentation.
  • Understand the concept of historical significance — why does this event matter?

Historical Analysis: h7

History is not about memorising dates and events — it is about understanding causation, change, continuity, and significance. Examiners reward analysis over narrative.

Essay Structure
  1. Introduction: Define key terms and state your line of argument.
  2. Paragraphs: Each should make one point, supported by specific evidence.
  3. Analysis: Explain why events happened and why they matter.
  4. Conclusion: Directly answer the question with a clear judgement.
Evidence and Analysis

Poor: "The Treaty of Versailles was signed in 1919 and Germany had to pay reparations."

Better: "The Treaty of Versailles imposed reparations of £6.6 billion on Germany because the war guilt clause (Article 231) held Germany responsible for WWI. This meant that the German economy was crippled by debt, leading to hyperinflation in 1923 and resentment that fuelled Nazi support."

Specific evidence earns marks. "On 28 June 1914, Franz Ferdinand was assassinated in Sarajevo" is far better than "in 1914 something happened". Always use names, dates, and statistics.

Worked Example 1: Analysing a Source

Problem

Analyse the usefulness of a government propaganda poster from 1916 encouraging men to enlist in the army.

Solution

Content: The poster shows a smiling soldier with the caption "Your Country Needs You." It portrays military service as honourable and patriotic.

Provenance: Created by the British government in 1916, after conscription was introduced. The purpose was to encourage voluntary enlistment and maintain public support for the war.

Usefulness: Useful for showing how governments used emotional appeals to recruit soldiers. However, limited by bias — it does not show the realities of trench warfare. It should be compared with letters from soldiers or casualty figures for a balanced view.

Worked Example 2: Causation Essay Plan

Problem

To what extent was the Treaty of Versailles responsible for the outbreak of World War II?

Essay Plan

Introduction: Define the Treaty of Versailles (1919) and state that while it contributed to WWII, it was not the sole cause.

FOR (Treaty was responsible):

  • War guilt clause (Article 231) humiliated Germany
  • Reparations crippled the German economy (hyperinflation 1923)
  • Territorial losses (13% of land, colonies, Alsace-Lorraine) created resentment
  • Demilitarisation was seen as unfair and weak

AGAINST (Other factors):

  • Failure of the League of Nations to stop aggression (Manchuria 1931, Abyssinia 1935)
  • Appeasement policy of Britain and France (Munich Agreement 1938)
  • Great Depression created economic instability worldwide
  • Hitler's expansionist ambitions and rearmament

Conclusion: The Treaty created conditions for resentment but did not make war inevitable. The failures of the League, appeasement, and Hitler's actions were equally or more important.

Worked Example 3: Comparing Interpretations

Problem

Historian A argues that the Cold War was caused by Soviet aggression. Historian B argues it was caused by American policy. Evaluate these interpretations.

Solution

Historian A (Soviet aggression): Supported by Soviet expansion into Eastern Europe (1945–48), creation of puppet states, Berlin Blockade (1948), and invasion of Hungary (1956).

Historian B (American policy): Supported by Truman Doctrine (1947), Marshall Plan (1948), NATO (1949), and US support for anti-communist regimes worldwide.

Evaluation: Both interpretations have merit. Soviet actions in Eastern Europe were aggressive, but US policies like containment were also confrontational. The orthodox view (Soviet blame) dominated until the 1960s; the revisionist view (US blame) emerged with the Vietnam War. Post-revisionist historians (like Gaddis) argue both sides share responsibility.

Practice Questions

Question 1

Analyse the reliability of a diary entry from a soldier on the Western Front in 1916.

Answer: Consider proximity to events (likely reliable on details), personal perspective (may be emotional or biased), purpose (private vs published), compare with official records or other soldiers' accounts.

Question 2

Explain why the League of Nations failed to stop Japanese aggression in Manchuria (1931).

Answer: Economic sanctions not applied (USA not member, major trading partner); no military force available; Britain and France unwilling to act (focused on domestic issues); Japan simply left the League; slow decision-making process.

Question 3

"The Cuban Missile Crisis was a victory for the USA." How far do you agree?

Answer: FOR: missiles removed, Khrushchev backed down, USA appeared strong. AGAINST: USA had to remove missiles from Turkey secretly, crisis showed dangers of brinkmanship, hotline established afterwards. CONCLUSION: Short-term victory for USA but long-term recognition of need for détente.

🎯 Examination Tips

  • For source questions, always comment on both content and provenance — this is the key to high marks.
  • Leave time to review — check that you have answered the question asked, not the one you expected.
  • Structure essays with an introduction, developed paragraphs, and a clear conclusion.
  • Each paragraph should make one main point, supported by evidence and explanation.
  • In essay questions, use specific evidence — "On 28 June 1914" not "in 1914".
