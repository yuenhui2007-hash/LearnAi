IBC1: Stoichiometry

Overview

Stoichiometric Relationships (Topic 1) is the quantitative foundation of chemistry. It covers mole calculations, gas laws, and solution chemistry. This topic is tested across all papers and is essential for the IA, where accurate quantitative technique is assessed in Criterion B (methodology).

1.1 The Mole Concept

The mole is the amount of substance containing the same number of particles as there are atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12.

Avogadro's constant: N_A = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹
n = N / N_A (number of moles from number of particles)
n = m / M (number of moles from mass)
where M = molar mass (g mol⁻¹)

Worked Example 1: Mole Calculation

Question: Calculate the number of moles in 25.0 g of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃).

Solution:

M(CaCO₃) = 40.08 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 100.09 g mol⁻¹
n = m/M = 25.0 / 100.09 = 0.250 mol (3 sf)

1.2 Empirical and Molecular Formulas

  • Empirical formula: simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound.
  • Molecular formula: actual number of atoms of each element in a molecule.

Worked Example 2: Empirical Formula

Question: A compound contains 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen, and 53.3% oxygen by mass. Find the empirical formula.

Solution:

Assume 100 g sample:
C: 40.0/12.01 = 3.33 mol
H: 6.7/1.008 = 6.65 mol
O: 53.3/16.00 = 3.33 mol

Divide by smallest (3.33): C₁H₂O₁
Empirical formula = CH₂O

1.3 Chemical Equations & Limiting Reactants

A balanced chemical equation shows the mole ratios of reactants and products. The limiting reactant is completely consumed and determines the maximum yield.

Worked Example 3: Limiting Reactant

Question: 5.0 g H₂ reacts with 20.0 g O₂. Which is limiting and what mass of water forms?

Solution:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
n(H₂) = 5.0/2.02 = 2.48 mol
n(O₂) = 20.0/32.00 = 0.625 mol

H₂ needed for 0.625 mol O₂ = 1.25 mol
H₂ available = 2.48 mol → O₂ is limiting
n(H₂O) = 2 × 0.625 = 1.25 mol
m(H₂O) = 1.25 × 18.02 = 22.5 g

1.4 Solutions

Concentration (mol dm⁻³) = n / V
where V is volume in dm³ (1 dm³ = 1000 cm³ = 1 L)

Dilution

c₁V₁ = c₂V₂

1.5 Gas Laws

Ideal Gas Equation: pV = nRT
R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ (use with Pa and m³)
or R = 0.0821 atm dm³ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹

At STP (273 K, 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa): 1 mol occupies 22.7 dm³
At RTP (298 K, 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa): 1 mol occupies 24.5 dm³

Worked Example 4: Gas Volume

Question: Calculate the volume occupied by 0.50 mol of gas at 25°C and 100 kPa.

Solution:

V = nRT/p = (0.50)(8.31)(298)/(1.00 × 10⁵) = 1.24 × 10⁻² m³ = 12.4 dm³

Common Misconceptions

  • Empirical vs. molecular: Do not confuse them — molecular formula = (empirical formula)ₙ where n = M_molecular / M_empirical.
  • Volume units: pV = nRT requires SI units (Pa, m³) or consistent units with your chosen R.
  • Limiting reactant: Compare mole ratios, not masses.
  • Concentration: mol dm⁻³ is the same as molarity (M).

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: 3–4 MCQs on mole calculations, gas volume, and limiting reactants.
  • Paper 2: Structured questions on empirical formula, titration calculations, and stoichiometry.
  • Paper 3: Data analysis of titration or gas collection experiments.
  • IA: Titrations, enthalpy experiments, and gas collection require accurate stoichiometric calculations.

IBC2: Atomic Structure

Overview

Atomic Structure (Topic 2) explores the composition of atoms, isotopes, and the arrangement of electrons. It provides the foundation for understanding periodicity, bonding, and chemical reactivity. This topic is heavily tested in Paper 1 and Paper 2, and connects directly to the IA through spectroscopy experiments.

2.1 Subatomic Particles

  • Proton: charge +1, mass ≈ 1 u, found in nucleus.
  • Neutron: charge 0, mass ≈ 1 u, found in nucleus.
  • Electron: charge −1, mass ≈ 1/1836 u, found in orbitals.
Atomic number (Z) = number of protons
Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons
Number of electrons = number of protons (in a neutral atom)
Isotopes: same Z, different A (different neutron number)

2.2 Mass Spectrometry

Mass spectrometry determines relative atomic mass and isotopic abundance.

  • Ionisation: sample is bombarded with high-energy electrons.
  • Acceleration: ions are accelerated by an electric field.
  • Deflection: ions are deflected by a magnetic field — lighter ions deflect more.
  • Detection: ions strike a detector and produce a mass spectrum.
Relative atomic mass (A_r) = Σ(isotope mass × fractional abundance)

Worked Example 1: Relative Atomic Mass

Question: Chlorine has two isotopes: ³⁵Cl (75.8%) and ³⁷Cl (24.2%). Calculate A_r.

Solution:

A_r = (35 × 0.758) + (37 × 0.242) = 26.53 + 8.95 = 35.48

2.3 Electron Configuration

Electrons occupy energy levels (shells), sublevels (s, p, d, f), and orbitals.

s: 1 orbital, 2 electrons
p: 3 orbitals, 6 electrons
d: 5 orbitals, 10 electrons
f: 7 orbitals, 14 electrons

Maximum electrons per shell n: 2n²

Aufbau Principle

Electrons fill orbitals from lowest to highest energy: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p...

Special Cases

  • Chromium (Cr): [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ (not 3d⁴ 4s²) — half-filled d subshell is more stable.
  • Copper (Cu): [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ (not 3d⁹ 4s²) — full d subshell is more stable.

HL only

HL students write electron configurations using the full s, p, d, f notation and explain anomalies using stability arguments.

2.4 Ionisation Energy

First ionisation energy (IE₁): energy required to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms.

X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻

Factors Affecting Ionisation Energy

  • Nuclear charge: higher Z → stronger attraction → higher IE.
  • Atomic radius: larger radius → weaker attraction → lower IE.
  • Electron shielding: more inner electrons → more shielding → lower IE.

Trends in Successive Ionisation Energies

Large jumps in successive IE values indicate a change in electron shell. For example, Na shows a large jump after the 1st IE (removing a core electron).

2.5 Emission Spectra

  • Electrons emit photons when falling from higher to lower energy levels.
  • Each element has a unique emission spectrum (fingerprint).
ΔE = hf = hc/λ
where h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹

Common Misconceptions

  • Mass number: It is not the exact mass of an atom — it is the total number of nucleons.
  • Isotopes: Isotopes have the same chemical properties (same electron configuration) but different physical properties (different mass).
  • Electron shells: Electrons do not orbit like planets — they exist in probability clouds (orbitals).
  • Ionisation energy: Always refers to gaseous atoms. Removing an electron from a solid or liquid requires different energy.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: 3–4 MCQs on subatomic particles, electron configuration, and ionisation energy trends.
  • Paper 2: Structured questions on mass spectra, electron configuration of ions, and ionisation energy data.
  • Paper 3: Spectroscopy experiments and data analysis.
  • IA: Flame tests, spectroscopy, and atomic emission experiments are common.

IBC3: Periodicity

Overview

Periodicity (Topic 3) examines trends in the periodic table and the properties of elements in groups and periods. Understanding these patterns allows you to predict chemical behaviour and is tested across all papers. Group 1, Group 17, and transition metals are particularly important.

3.1 The Periodic Table

  • Arranged by increasing atomic number.
  • Groups: vertical columns — elements have similar chemical properties due to same number of valence electrons.
  • Periods: horizontal rows — correspond to the outermost electron shell being filled.
  • Blocks: s-block (groups 1–2), p-block (groups 13–18), d-block (transition metals), f-block (lanthanides/actinides).

3.2 Periodic Trends

Atomic Radius

  • Across a period: decreases — increasing nuclear charge pulls electrons closer.
  • Down a group: increases — additional electron shells outweigh increased nuclear charge.

Ionic Radius

  • Cations are smaller than their parent atoms (lost electron shell or increased effective nuclear charge).
  • Anions are larger than their parent atoms (increased electron–electron repulsion).

Ionisation Energy

  • Across a period: generally increases (higher nuclear charge, smaller radius).
  • Down a group: decreases (larger radius, more shielding).
  • Dips: Group 2 → Group 13 (p electron is higher in energy and slightly shielded); Group 15 → Group 16 (electron pairing repulsion in p subshell).

Electronegativity

Ability of an atom to attract bonding electrons in a covalent bond.

  • Across a period: increases (higher nuclear charge, smaller atomic radius).
  • Down a group: decreases (larger radius, more shielding).
  • Fluorine is the most electronegative element (Pauling scale = 4.0).

Electron Affinity

Energy change when an electron is added to a gaseous atom. More negative = more exothermic = more favourable.

3.3 Properties of Period 3 Elements

Oxides

  • Na₂O, MgO: basic oxides — react with acids to form salts and water.
  • Al₂O₃: amphoteric oxide — reacts with both acids and bases.
  • SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₃: acidic oxides — react with bases to form salts and water.

Chlorides

  • NaCl, MgCl₂: ionic chlorides — conduct when molten/aqueous, neutral pH in solution.
  • AlCl₃: covalent network (dimer Al₂Cl₆) — reacts with water to form acidic solution.
  • SiCl₄, PCl₅: covalent molecular — hydrolyse in water to form acidic solutions and white fumes (HCl).

3.4 Group 1: Alkali Metals

  • Low density, low melting points, soft metals.
  • Reactivity increases down the group — outer electron is further from nucleus and more shielded.
  • Reaction with water: 2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂ (vigorous, exothermic).

3.5 Group 17: Halogens

  • Reactivity decreases down the group — harder to gain an electron as atomic radius increases.
  • Displacement reactions: more reactive halogen displaces less reactive halogen from its salt solution.
  • Colours: F₂ (pale yellow gas), Cl₂ (green-yellow gas), Br₂ (red-brown liquid), I₂ (grey solid / purple vapour).

3.6 Transition Metals [HL only]

  • Variable oxidation states (e.g., Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺, Cu⁺/Cu²⁺).
  • Coloured compounds due to d–d transitions.
  • Catalytic activity (e.g., Fe in Haber process, V₂O₅ in Contact process, MnO₂ in decomposition of H₂O₂).
  • Formation of complex ions with ligands (e.g., [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ is blue).

HL Exam Focus

May 2024 HL Paper 2: Coloured complex ions and explanation using d-orbital splitting in octahedral fields.

Common Misconceptions

  • Atomic radius: Do not confuse atomic radius with ionic radius — trends differ for cations and anions.
  • Electronegativity: It applies to atoms in bonds, not isolated atoms.
  • Group trends: Reactivity of metals increases down a group; reactivity of non-metals decreases down a group.
  • Amphoteric: Al₂O₃ and Al(OH)₃ are amphoteric — they react with both acids and bases.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: 3–4 MCQs on periodic trends, group reactions, and oxide/acid-base character.
  • Paper 2: Structured questions on trend explanations, group chemistry, and HL transition metal chemistry.
  • Paper 3: Data analysis of trend data (atomic radius, IE, electronegativity).
  • IA: Trends in reactivity, halogen displacement, and transition metal complexes are common.

IBC4: Chemical Bonding

Overview

Chemical Bonding (Topic 4) explains how atoms combine to form compounds. Understanding the nature of ionic, covalent, and metallic bonding, as well as intermolecular forces, is essential for predicting physical properties and explaining reactivity. This topic is heavily tested across all papers.

4.1 Ionic Bonding

  • Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
  • Formed between metals (lose electrons → cations) and non-metals (gain electrons → anions).
  • High melting/boiling points due to strong electrostatic forces.
  • Conduct electricity when molten or aqueous (ions are free to move).
  • Brittle — ion layers can shift, causing repulsion between like charges.
Lattice energy: energy required to separate one mole of solid ionic compound into gaseous ions.
Higher lattice energy → stronger ionic bonding → higher melting point.

4.2 Covalent Bonding

  • Sharing of electron pairs between atoms.
  • Occurs between non-metal atoms with similar electronegativities.
  • Can be single (σ), double (1σ + 1π), or triple (1σ + 2π) bonds.

Lewis Structures

Show valence electrons as dots or crosses. Follow the octet rule (duet for H).

VSEPR Theory

Predicts molecular geometry based on electron pair repulsion:

  • 2 electron domains → linear (180°)
  • 3 electron domains → trigonal planar (120°)
  • 4 electron domains → tetrahedral (109.5°)
  • 4 domains (1 lone pair) → trigonal pyramidal (~107°)
  • 4 domains (2 lone pairs) → bent (~104.5°)
  • 5 domains → trigonal bipyramidal
  • 6 domains → octahedral

HL only

HL students explain molecular shapes using hybridisation (sp, sp², sp³) and understand that lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs, compressing bond angles.

Resonance

Some molecules cannot be represented by a single Lewis structure (e.g., ozone O₃, carbonate CO₃²⁻). The actual structure is a resonance hybrid, with bond lengths intermediate between single and double bonds.

4.3 Metallic Bonding

  • Electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and a "sea" of delocalised electrons.
  • Explains electrical and thermal conductivity (delocalised electrons move freely).
  • Malleability and ductility (ions can slide without breaking bonds).
  • High melting points (strong metallic bonds).

4.4 Intermolecular Forces (IMFs)

Weaker than covalent/ionic bonds; determine physical properties of molecular substances.

London Dispersion Forces (LDF)

  • Present in all molecules.
  • Caused by temporary dipoles inducing dipoles in neighbouring molecules.
  • Strength increases with number of electrons (larger molecule → stronger LDF).

Dipole–Dipole Forces

  • Present in polar molecules (permanent dipoles).
  • Positive end of one molecule attracts negative end of another.

Hydrogen Bonding

  • Special strong dipole–dipole force.
  • Occurs when H is bonded to N, O, or F.
  • Explains high boiling points of H₂O, NH₃, HF, and biological structures (DNA base pairing, protein folding).

4.5 Polarity

  • Polar bond: electronegativity difference > 0.4 but < 1.7.
  • Ionic bond: electronegativity difference ≥ 1.7.
  • Non-polar bond: electronegativity difference < 0.4.

A molecule can have polar bonds but be non-polar overall if the dipoles cancel due to symmetry (e.g., CO₂, CCl₄).

Common Misconceptions

  • Intermolecular vs. intramolecular: IMFs are between molecules; covalent bonds are within molecules. Breaking IMFs changes state; breaking covalent bonds causes chemical change.
  • Hydrogen bonds: These are NOT covalent bonds — they are intermolecular forces involving H bonded to N, O, or F.
  • VSEPR: Count all electron domains (bonding + lone pairs), not just atoms.
  • Metallic bonding: The "sea of electrons" is not free to leave the metal — it is delocalised within the structure.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: 4–5 MCQs on bonding types, VSEPR shapes, polarity, and IMFs.
  • Paper 2: Structured questions on Lewis structures, shapes, polarity explanations, and IMF comparisons.
  • Paper 3: Data analysis of melting point or solubility trends.
  • IA: Enthalpy of combustion, solubility, and viscosity experiments relate to IMFs.

IBC5: Energetics

Overview

Energetics and Thermochemistry (Topic 5) deals with the energy changes in chemical reactions. It is essential for predicting reaction feasibility and is tested across all papers. Calorimetry experiments are popular IA topics (Criterion A–D).

5.1 Enthalpy Changes

Enthalpy change (ΔH): heat energy change at constant pressure.

  • Exothermic: ΔH < 0 — heat released to surroundings (products lower in energy than reactants).
  • Endothermic: ΔH > 0 — heat absorbed from surroundings (products higher in energy than reactants).

Types of Enthalpy Change

  • Standard enthalpy of formation (ΔH°f): enthalpy change when 1 mol of compound forms from its elements in standard states.
  • Standard enthalpy of combustion (ΔH°c): enthalpy change when 1 mol of substance burns completely in oxygen.
  • Standard enthalpy of neutralisation: enthalpy change when 1 mol of water is formed from acid–base neutralisation.
  • Standard enthalpy of atomisation: enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous atoms forms from the element in its standard state.

5.2 Calorimetry

q = mcΔT
q = heat energy (J), m = mass of solution (g), c = specific heat capacity (4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ for water), ΔT = temperature change (K or °C)

For reactions in solution, assume the solution behaves like water.

Worked Example 1: Calorimetry

Question: 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl is mixed with 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. The temperature rises from 20.0°C to 26.5°C. Calculate ΔH for neutralisation.

Solution:

Total volume = 100.0 cm³ → mass ≈ 100.0 g
q = (100.0)(4.18)(6.5) = 2717 J
n(H₂O) = n(HCl) = (1.00)(0.0500) = 0.0500 mol
ΔH = −q/n = −2717/0.0500 = −54 340 J mol⁻¹ = −54.3 kJ mol⁻¹

5.3 Hess's Law

The enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the pathway taken.

ΔH_reaction = ΣΔH°f(products) − ΣΔH°f(reactants)
ΔH_reaction = ΣΔH°c(reactants) − ΣΔH°c(products)

Worked Example 2: Hess's Law

Question: Calculate ΔH for C(s) + ½O₂(g) → CO(g) given:
C + O₂ → CO₂, ΔH = −394 kJ mol⁻¹
CO + ½O₂ → CO₂, ΔH = −283 kJ mol⁻¹

Solution:

Reverse second equation: CO₂ → CO + ½O₂, ΔH = +283 kJ mol⁻¹
Add to first: C + O₂ + CO₂ → CO₂ + CO + ½O₂
Simplifies to: C + ½O₂ → CO, ΔH = −394 + 283 = −111 kJ mol⁻¹

5.4 Bond Enthalpies

Average energy required to break 1 mol of a bond in the gaseous state.

ΔH = Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds broken) − Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds formed)
Bonds broken = endothermic (+); bonds formed = exothermic (−)

Bond enthalpies are average values and less accurate than Hess's law calculations using formation/combustion data.

5.5 Entropy & Gibbs Free Energy [HL only]

Entropy (S): measure of disorder or number of ways energy can be distributed.

ΔS° = ΣS°(products) − ΣS°(reactants)
Gibbs free energy: ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°
If ΔG° < 0: reaction is spontaneous
If ΔG° > 0: reaction is non-spontaneous

Temperature Dependence

  • ΔH < 0, ΔS > 0: spontaneous at all temperatures.
  • ΔH > 0, ΔS < 0: non-spontaneous at all temperatures.
  • ΔH < 0, ΔS < 0: spontaneous at low T.
  • ΔH > 0, ΔS > 0: spontaneous at high T.

HL Exam Focus

May 2024 HL Paper 2: Calculate ΔG° and predict spontaneity at different temperatures; explain the decomposition of calcium carbonate.

Common Misconceptions

  • Sign of ΔH: Exothermic = negative ΔH. Students often confuse the sign with temperature change.
  • Hess's law: Remember the direction of arrows — reverse a reaction, reverse the sign of ΔH.
  • Bond enthalpies: These are average values for gaseous molecules only — they cannot be used for solids or liquids directly.
  • Entropy: A negative ΔS° does not mean a reaction cannot occur — check ΔG° at the given temperature.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: 2–3 MCQs on enthalpy profile diagrams, Hess's law, and bond enthalpies.
  • Paper 2: Structured questions on calorimetry, Hess's law cycles, and HL Gibbs free energy.
  • Paper 3: Data analysis of calorimetry experiments.
  • IA: Enthalpy of combustion, enthalpy of neutralisation, and enthalpy of solution are very popular.

IBC6: Kinetics

Overview

Chemical Kinetics (Topic 6) studies the rates of chemical reactions and the factors that influence them. Understanding collision theory, rate laws, and activation energy is essential for predicting how reactions proceed. This topic is tested across all papers and connects to equilibrium (Topic 7) and organic reaction mechanisms (Topic 10).

6.1 Collision Theory

For a reaction to occur, particles must:

  • Collide with each other.
  • Collide with sufficient energy (≥ activation energy, Eₐ).
  • Collide with the correct orientation.

Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution

Shows the distribution of molecular kinetic energies at a given temperature:

  • The area under the curve is constant.
  • At higher temperature, the curve shifts right and flattens — more molecules have E ≥ Eₐ.
  • A catalyst lowers Eₐ, shifting the activation energy mark to the left — more molecules react.

6.2 Factors Affecting Rate

  • Concentration/pressure: more particles per unit volume → more frequent collisions.
  • Temperature: increases average kinetic energy → more molecules exceed Eₐ.
  • Surface area: more exposed particles available for collision.
  • Catalyst: provides alternative pathway with lower Eₐ.

6.3 Rate Expressions

Rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ
where k = rate constant, m and n = orders with respect to A and B
Overall order = m + n

Determining Order from Initial Rates

  • Compare experiments where one reactant concentration changes and others are constant.
  • If rate doubles when [A] doubles → first order in A.
  • If rate quadruples when [A] doubles → second order in A.
  • If rate unchanged when [A] changes → zero order in A.

Worked Example 1: Initial Rates

Question: Determine the rate expression from:

[A] (mol dm⁻³)[B] (mol dm⁻³)Initial rate
0.100.102.0 × 10⁻³
0.200.104.0 × 10⁻³
0.100.208.0 × 10⁻³

Solution:

[A] doubles → rate doubles → first order in A
[B] doubles → rate quadruples → second order in B
Rate = k[A][B]²

6.4 Arrhenius Equation [HL only]

k = Ae^(−Eₐ/RT)
where A = frequency factor, Eₐ = activation energy, R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, T = temperature (K)

Taking natural logs:

ln k = ln A − Eₐ/RT
Plot ln k vs 1/T → straight line with gradient = −Eₐ/R and y-intercept = ln A

6.5 Reaction Mechanisms [HL only]

  • Elementary step: a single collision event in a mechanism.
  • Molecularity: number of reactant particles in an elementary step (unimolecular, bimolecular, termolecular).
  • Rate-determining step (RDS): the slowest step — determines the overall rate expression.

HL Exam Focus

May 2024 HL Paper 2: Proposed mechanism given; deduce rate expression and identify intermediates.

Common Misconceptions

  • Rate vs. equilibrium: A catalyst speeds up both forward and reverse reactions equally — it does not change the position of equilibrium.
  • Order vs. stoichiometry: Reaction order must be determined experimentally — it is not necessarily the stoichiometric coefficient.
  • Temperature: Increasing temperature increases the rate constant k — the activation energy itself does not change.
  • Maxwell–Boltzmann: The peak of the curve decreases when temperature increases because the same total area must be maintained.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: 3–4 MCQs on collision theory, Maxwell–Boltzmann distributions, and rate factors.
  • Paper 2: Structured questions on rate expressions, initial rate methods, and HL Arrhenius plots.
  • Paper 3: Data analysis of rate experiments (clock reactions, iodine clock).
  • IA: Iodine clock, reaction of marble chips with acid, and catalysed decomposition of H₂O₂ are popular.

IBC7: Equilibrium

Detailed Notes

1. Dynamic Equilibrium

  • Forward rate = reverse rate; concentrations constant
  • Kc = [products]/[reactants] (stoichiometric powers)

2. Le Chatelier

  • System opposes the change
  • Conc: add reactant → shifts right
  • Pressure: increase → fewer gas moles
  • Temperature: increase → endothermic direction
  • Catalyst: no effect on position

3. Kp

  • Kp = (partial pressure products)/(partial pressure reactants)
  • Kp = Kc(RT)^Δn

Last-Minute Summary

  • Kc only changes with temperature
  • Le Chatelier: system opposes change
  • Catalyst: speeds both directions equally
  • Kp = Kc(RT)^Δn

Exam Tips

  • Pure solids and liquids are NOT included in Kc expression
  • When writing Kc, use stoichiometric coefficients as powers
  • For pressure: only count GASEOUS moles when determining shift direction
  • Equilibrium position and equilibrium constant are different — constant only changes with T

IBC8: Acids & Bases

Overview

Acids and Bases (Topic 8) covers the Brønsted–Lowry theory, pH calculations, buffers, and titrations. This topic is heavily calculation-based and appears across all papers. Titration experiments are among the most popular IA topics.

8.1 Brønsted–Lowry Theory

  • Acid: proton (H⁺) donor.
  • Base: proton (H⁺) acceptor.
  • Conjugate acid–base pair: differ by one H⁺ (e.g., NH₄⁺/NH₃, H₂O/OH⁻).
HA + B ⇌ A⁻ + HB⁺
Acid + Base ⇌ Conjugate base + Conjugate acid

8.2 pH and pOH

pH = −log[H⁺]
pOH = −log[OH⁻]
pH + pOH = 14.00 (at 25°C)
[H⁺] = 10^(−pH)
K_w = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ (at 25°C)

Worked Example 1: pH Calculation

Question: Calculate the pH of 0.050 mol dm⁻³ HCl.

Solution:

HCl is strong → [H⁺] = 0.050 mol dm⁻³
pH = −log(0.050) = 1.30

8.3 Strong and Weak Acids/Bases

  • Strong acid: completely dissociates (e.g., HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄).
  • Weak acid: partially dissociates (e.g., CH₃COOH, H₂CO₃).
  • Strong base: completely dissociates (e.g., NaOH, KOH).
  • Weak base: partially dissociates (e.g., NH₃).

Acid Dissociation Constant (Kₐ)

Kₐ = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA]
pKₐ = −log Kₐ
Lower pKₐ → stronger acid

Worked Example 2: Weak Acid pH

Question: Calculate the pH of 0.10 mol dm⁻³ CH₃COOH (Kₐ = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵).

Solution:

Kₐ = [H⁺]² / [HA] (assuming [H⁺] << [HA])
[H⁺] = √(Kₐ × [HA]) = √(1.8 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.10) = 1.34 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³
pH = −log(1.34 × 10⁻³) = 2.87

8.4 Titrations

  • Equivalence point: moles of acid = moles of base.
  • End point: indicator changes colour (should match equivalence point).

Indicators

IndicatorpH rangeAcid colourBase colour
Methyl orange3.1–4.4RedYellow
Bromothymol blue6.0–7.6YellowBlue
Phenolphthalein8.2–10.0ColourlessPink

Titration Curves

  • Strong acid + strong base: equivalence at pH 7; any indicator works.
  • Weak acid + strong base: equivalence at pH > 7; phenolphthalein suitable.
  • Strong acid + weak base: equivalence at pH < 7; methyl orange suitable.

8.5 Buffers [HL only]

A buffer resists pH change when small amounts of acid or base are added.

Acidic buffer: weak acid + its conjugate base salt (e.g., CH₃COOH + CH₃COONa)
Basic buffer: weak base + its conjugate acid salt (e.g., NH₃ + NH₄Cl)

Henderson–Hasselbalch equation:
pH = pKₐ + log([A⁻]/[HA])

Common Misconceptions

  • pH scale: A change of 1 pH unit means a 10-fold change in [H⁺].
  • Strong vs. concentrated: "Strong" refers to degree of dissociation; "concentrated" refers to amount per volume. A dilute strong acid can have higher pH than a concentrated weak acid.
  • Equivalence point pH: Not always 7 — depends on the acid and base strengths.
  • Water autoionisation: In pure water, [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³ at 25°C.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: 3–4 MCQs on pH calculations, strong/weak acid identification, and indicator choice.
  • Paper 2: Structured questions on titration calculations, buffer pH, and acid-base theory.
  • Paper 3: Data analysis of titration curves and buffer experiments.
  • IA: Acid-base titrations and buffer preparation are extremely popular IA topics.

IBC9: Redox

Overview

Redox Processes (Topic 9) covers oxidation and reduction reactions, electrochemical cells, and electrolysis. This topic combines conceptual understanding with significant calculation requirements and appears across all papers. Electrochemical cells are a popular IA topic.

9.1 Oxidation & Reduction

  • Oxidation: loss of electrons, increase in oxidation state.
  • Reduction: gain of electrons, decrease in oxidation state.
  • Oxidising agent: accepts electrons (itself reduced).
  • Reducing agent: donates electrons (itself oxidised).
OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons)

9.2 Oxidation States

Rules for assigning oxidation states:

  • Element in standard state: 0.
  • Monatomic ion: equal to charge.
  • Oxygen: usually −2 (except peroxides: −1, OF₂: +2).
  • Hydrogen: usually +1 (except metal hydrides: −1).
  • Sum of oxidation states in a compound = 0; in a polyatomic ion = charge of ion.

Worked Example 1: Oxidation State

Question: Determine the oxidation state of Mn in KMnO₄.

Solution:

K = +1, O = −2 (×4 = −8)
+1 + Mn + (−8) = 0 → Mn = +7

9.3 Redox Equations

Balance using half-equations:

  1. Write separate oxidation and reduction half-equations.
  2. Balance atoms (except O and H).
  3. Balance O with H₂O, H with H⁺ (acidic) or OH⁻ (basic).
  4. Balance charge with electrons.
  5. Multiply half-equations so electrons cancel.
  6. Add and simplify.

Worked Example 2: Redox Equation

Question: Balance: MnO₄⁻ + Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + Fe³⁺ (acidic)

Solution:

Reduction: MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O
Oxidation: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻

Multiply oxidation by 5 and add:
MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O + 5Fe³⁺

9.4 Voltaic (Galvanic) Cells

  • Spontaneous redox reaction produces electrical energy.
  • Anode: oxidation occurs (negative electrode).
  • Cathode: reduction occurs (positive electrode).
  • Electrons flow from anode to cathode through external circuit.
  • Salt bridge allows ion migration to maintain charge neutrality.
Cell potential: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode
E°cell > 0 → spontaneous reaction

Standard Electrode Potentials

  • Measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), E° = 0 V.
  • More positive E° → stronger oxidising agent.
  • More negative E° → stronger reducing agent.

9.5 Electrolysis

  • Non-spontaneous redox driven by external electrical energy.
  • Anode: oxidation (positive electrode).
  • Cathode: reduction (negative electrode).

Electrolysis of Molten Salts

  • Cation reduced at cathode, anion oxidised at anode.

Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions

  • At cathode: compare reduction potentials of metal ion and H⁺/H₂O.
  • At anode: compare oxidation potentials of anion and H₂O/OH⁻.
Faraday's laws: Q = It = nF
Q = charge (C), I = current (A), t = time (s), n = moles of electrons, F = 96 500 C mol⁻¹

Common Misconceptions

  • Anode/cathode signs: In voltaic cells, anode is negative and cathode is positive. In electrolysis, anode is positive and cathode is negative.
  • Oxidation state: Oxidation state is a bookkeeping tool, not the actual charge on an atom.
  • Electrolysis products: In aqueous solutions, water can be oxidised or reduced depending on relative electrode potentials.
  • E°cell: A positive E°cell indicates spontaneity under standard conditions only.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: 3–4 MCQs on oxidation states, cell potentials, and electrolysis products.
  • Paper 2: Structured questions on redox equations, cell diagrams, and electrolysis calculations.
  • Paper 3: Data analysis of voltaic cell experiments.
  • IA: Electrochemical cells, electroplating, and electrolysis experiments are common.

IBC10: Organic

Overview

Organic Chemistry (Topic 10) is the largest topic in IB Chemistry, covering functional groups, nomenclature, reaction mechanisms, and synthetic pathways. It is tested across all papers and is particularly important for Paper 2. Mechanism drawing and retrosynthetic analysis are key HL skills.

10.2 Isomerism

  • Structural isomers: same molecular formula, different structural arrangement (chain, positional, functional group).
  • Stereoisomers: same structural formula, different spatial arrangement.
    • Cis–trans (geometric): restricted rotation around C=C; different groups on each carbon.
    • Optical: chiral centre (carbon with 4 different groups) → non-superimposable mirror images (enantiomers).

HL only

HL students use Cahn-Ingold-Prelog (CIP) rules to assign R/S configuration to chiral centres and E/Z notation to alkenes.

10.3 Reaction Mechanisms [HL only]

SN1 and SN2 Nucleophilic Substitution

  • SN1: two-step, carbocation intermediate, rate = k[substrate], favoured by tertiary substrates and polar protic solvents.
  • SN2: one-step, backside attack, rate = k[substrate][nucleophile], favoured by primary substrates and polar aprotic solvents.

Electrophilic Addition to Alkenes

  • Alkene π electrons attack electrophile (e.g., Br₂, HBr).
  • Carbocation intermediate formed; nucleophile attacks.
  • Markovnikov's rule: H adds to carbon with more H atoms already.

Electrophilic Substitution (Benzene)

  • Benzene's stable π system undergoes substitution rather than addition.
  • Catalyst required: FeBr₃ for bromination, H₂SO₄ for nitration.

10.4 Key Reactions Summary

  • Alkanes: free radical substitution (Cl₂/UV).
  • Alkenes: addition (H₂/Ni, Br₂, HBr, H₂O/H⁺).
  • Alcohols: oxidation (primary → aldehyde → carboxylic acid; secondary → ketone); dehydration (conc. H₂SO₄/heat); substitution (PCl₅, NaBr/H₂SO₄).
  • Aldehydes/ketones: oxidation (aldehyde only, Tollens'/Fehling's); reduction (NaBH₄).
  • Carboxylic acids: neutralisation; esterification (alcohol + conc. H₂SO₄).

10.5 Spectroscopy [HL only]

  • ¹H NMR: number of peaks = number of proton environments; integration = relative number of protons; splitting (n+1 rule) = number of adjacent protons.
  • IR: identify functional groups by characteristic absorption frequencies (e.g., C=O at ~1700 cm⁻¹, O–H broad at 3200–3600 cm⁻¹).
  • Mass spectrometry: molecular ion peak gives molar mass; fragmentation patterns help identify structure.

HL Exam Focus

May 2024 HL Paper 2: Combined IR and ¹H NMR data used to deduce an unknown organic structure.

Common Misconceptions

  • Aldehyde vs. ketone oxidation: Only aldehydes are oxidised by Tollens' reagent and Fehling's solution. Ketones resist oxidation under mild conditions.
  • Optical activity: A racemic mixture contains equal amounts of both enantiomers and is optically inactive.
  • SN1/SN2: Tertiary halogenoalkanes favour SN1; primary favour SN2.
  • Benzene: Undergoes substitution, not addition, due to aromatic stability.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: 4–5 MCQs on functional group identification, nomenclature, and reaction products.
  • Paper 2: Structured questions on reaction mechanisms, synthesis pathways, and HL spectroscopy.
  • Paper 3: Data analysis of organic synthesis experiments (yield, purity).
  • IA: Organic synthesis, esterification, and identification of unknown compounds are popular.
