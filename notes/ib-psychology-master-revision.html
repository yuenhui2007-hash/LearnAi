IBPS2: Cognitive

Overview

The Cognitive Approach (Approach 2) examines internal mental processes such as memory, perception, thinking, and decision-making. It uses models and theories to explain behaviour and is assessed in Paper 1. Understanding memory models and cognitive biases is essential for both exam success and the IA.

2.1 Memory Models

Multi-Store Model (Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1968)

  • Sensory memory: brief storage of sensory information (iconic ~0.5s, echoic ~3–4s).
  • Short-term memory (STM): limited capacity (7±2 items), limited duration (~18–30s), acoustic encoding.
  • Long-term memory (LTM): theoretically unlimited capacity and duration, semantic encoding.

Information flows from sensory → STM → LTM through attention and rehearsal.

Working Memory Model (Baddeley & Hitch, 1974)

  • Central executive: controls attention, coordinates subsystems.
  • Phonological loop: verbal/acoustic information (articulatory control process + phonological store).
  • Visuospatial sketchpad: visual and spatial information.
  • Episodic buffer (added 2000): integrates information across modalities and links to LTM.

2.2 Thinking & Decision-Making

Schema Theory

  • Schemas are cognitive frameworks that organise knowledge.
  • They help process information efficiently but can lead to distortion (e.g., Bartlett's "War of the Ghosts" — memory reconstruction).

Heuristics & Biases

  • Availability heuristic: judge probability by ease of recall (e.g., fear of flying vs. driving).
  • Representativeness heuristic: judge by stereotype (e.g., assuming a shy person is a librarian).
  • Anchoring bias: over-rely on first piece of information.
  • Confirmation bias: seek information that supports existing beliefs.

Dual Process Theory

  • System 1: fast, automatic, intuitive, prone to biases.
  • System 2: slow, effortful, logical, analytical.
  • Kahneman's research shows most everyday decisions use System 1.

2.3 Cognitive Processing

  • Bottom-up processing: perception driven by sensory input (data-driven).
  • Top-down processing: perception influenced by expectations and schemas (concept-driven).
  • Interactive processing: both occur simultaneously.

2.4 Reliability of Cognitive Processes

  • Reconstructive memory: memories are not exact recordings but reconstructions influenced by schemas, expectations, and post-event information (Loftus's eyewitness testimony research).
  • Flashbulb memories: vivid, detailed memories of emotionally significant events — but research shows they are not necessarily more accurate than ordinary memories (Neisser).

Common Misconceptions

  • Memory as video recording: Memory is reconstructive, not reproductive — it changes over time.
  • STM capacity: 7±2 refers to chunks, not individual items — chunking increases capacity.
  • Schemas: They are not "errors" — they are adaptive but can lead to distortion.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Multi-store model: sensory → STM → LTM via attention and rehearsal.
  • Working memory: central executive, phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, episodic buffer.
  • Schema theory: cognitive frameworks that organise knowledge but can distort memory.
  • Heuristics: availability, representativeness, anchoring, confirmation bias.

  • Memory is reconstructive, not a perfect recording.

IBPS4: Abnormal

Overview

Abnormal Psychology (Option 1) examines psychological disorders, their causes, and treatments. It is a popular option topic examined in Paper 2. Students must understand etiological explanations from biological, cognitive, and sociocultural perspectives, as well as treatment efficacy and ethical considerations.

4.1 Defining Abnormality

  • Statistical infrequency: behaviour rare in population. Limitation: some rare behaviours are not disorders (e.g., high IQ).
  • Deviation from social norms: behaviour violating cultural expectations. Limitation: norms change over time and across cultures.
  • Failure to function adequately: inability to cope with daily life. Limitation: some functional people may still have disorders.
  • Deviation from ideal mental health (Jahoda): failure to meet criteria for positive well-being. Limitation: criteria are culturally biased toward Western individualism.

4.2 Depression

Biological Etiology

  • Serotonin and norepinephrine imbalances.
  • Genetic predisposition (twin studies show moderate heritability).
  • HPA axis dysregulation — elevated cortisol.

Cognitive Etiology

  • Beck's negative triad: negative views of self, world, and future.
  • Learned helplessness (Seligman): attributing negative events to internal, stable, global causes.

Sociocultural Etiology

  • Social support deficits.
  • Gender differences (women report depression at higher rates — possible due to reporting bias or social stressors).
  • SES and life stressors.

Treatments

  • Biological: SSRIs (e.g., fluoxetine/Prozac), SNRIs. ECT for severe, treatment-resistant cases.
  • Cognitive: CBT — challenge negative thoughts, behavioural activation.
  • Sociocultural: group therapy, family therapy, community-based interventions.

4.3 PTSD

  • Develops after exposure to traumatic event.
  • Symptoms: re-experiencing (flashbacks), avoidance, negative cognitions, hyperarousal.
  • Etiology: biological (amygdala hyperactivity, hippocampus shrinkage), cognitive (memory fragmentation), sociocultural (social support buffers risk).
  • Treatments: CBT with exposure therapy, EMDR, SSRIs.

4.4 Stigma

  • Social disapproval and discrimination toward those with mental disorders.
  • Reduces help-seeking behaviour.
  • Anti-stigma campaigns (e.g., Time to Change) aim to improve attitudes through education and contact.

Common Misconceptions

  • Depression = sadness: Clinical depression involves persistent symptoms affecting functioning, not just feeling sad.
  • Biological determinism: Biological factors increase risk but do not guarantee disorder — diathesis-stress model applies.
  • ECT: Modern ECT is safe and effective for severe depression — misconceptions come from outdated portrayals.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Definitions of abnormality: statistical, social norms, failure to function, ideal mental health.
  • Depression: biological (serotonin, genetics), cognitive (Beck's triad, learned helplessness), sociocultural (SES, gender).
  • PTSD: trauma-related; biological, cognitive, and sociocultural factors.
  • Treatments: SSRIs, CBT, ECT, EMDR, group/family therapy.
  • Stigma reduces help-seeking; anti-stigma campaigns use education and contact.

Exam Tips

  • Always consider the interaction of biological, cognitive, and sociocultural factors.
  • For treatment questions, evaluate efficacy and ethical issues.
  • Include cross-cultural evidence where relevant.

IBPS5: Research Methods

Overview

Developmental Psychology (Option 2) examines how behaviour changes across the lifespan, with a focus on childhood and adolescence. Key topics include attachment, cognitive development, identity formation, and the effects of childhood adversity. This option is examined in Paper 2.

5.1 Attachment

Bowlby's Attachment Theory

  • Attachment is an innate biological system promoting survival.
  • Monotropy: primary attachment to one caregiver (usually mother).
  • Critical period: first 2–3 years for attachment formation.
  • Internal working model: template for future relationships based on early attachment.

Ainsworth's Strange Situation

  • Secure: distressed when mother leaves, easily comforted (~65%).
  • Insecure-avoidant: little distress, avoids mother on return (~20%).
  • Insecure-resistant/ambivalent: very distressed, difficult to comfort (~15%).
  • Disorganised: inconsistent behaviour (~5–10%, often linked to abuse/neglect).

Long-Term Effects of Attachment

  • Secure attachment linked to better social competence, emotional regulation, and academic success.
  • Disrupted attachment (e.g., institutional care) linked to developmental delays and attachment disorders.

5.2 Cognitive Development

Piaget's Theory

  • Sensorimotor (0–2): object permanence develops.
  • Preoperational (2–7): egocentrism, animism, lack of conservation.
  • Concrete operational (7–11): conservation, classification, reversibility.
  • Formal operational (11+): abstract reasoning, hypothetical-deductive thinking.

Limitations: underestimated children's abilities; cultural bias; stage model too rigid.

Vygotsky's Sociocultural Theory

  • Cognitive development occurs through social interaction.
  • Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD): gap between what a child can do alone and with guidance.
  • Scaffolding: temporary support from a more knowledgeable person.

5.3 Identity Formation

  • Erikson's psychosocial stages: adolescence = identity vs. role confusion.
  • Marcia's identity statuses: achievement, moratorium, foreclosure, diffusion.
  • Identity influenced by peers, family, culture, and social media.

5.4 Childhood Adversity

  • ACEs (Adverse Childhood Experiences): abuse, neglect, household dysfunction.
  • Linked to increased risk of mental health disorders, substance abuse, and physical health problems in adulthood.
  • Resilience factors: secure attachment, social support, cognitive flexibility.

Common Misconceptions

  • Attachment styles: They are not fixed for life — experiences in adolescence and adulthood can modify internal working models.
  • Piaget: Children are not "stupid" in preoperational stage — they simply think differently.
  • ACEs: High ACE scores increase risk but do not determine outcomes — resilience factors matter.

  • Ainsworth: secure, avoidant, resistant, disorganised attachment styles.
  • Piaget: sensorimotor → preoperational → concrete operational → formal operational.
  • Vygotsky: social interaction drives development; ZPD and scaffolding.
  • Identity: Erikson (identity vs. role confusion); Marcia (achievement, moratorium, foreclosure, diffusion).
  • Childhood adversity (ACEs) increases risk but resilience factors can mitigate effects.

Exam Tips

  • Use named studies (Bowlby, Ainsworth, Piaget, Vygotsky) with details.
  • For evaluation, consider methodological issues and cultural bias.
  • Apply theories to real-world contexts (education, parenting, policy).
  • In essays, integrate biological, cognitive, and sociocultural levels of analysis.
