C9: Hydrocarbons

0620 (IGCSE)

IGCSE Content

Alkanes

Saturated hydrocarbons with general formula CnH₂n₊₂. They contain only C-C single bonds and C-H bonds. Alkanes are relatively unreactive but undergo:

  • Combustion: Complete combustion produces CO₂ + H₂O; incomplete produces CO or C (soot)
  • Substitution with halogens: UV light required. Free radical mechanism.
  • Cracking: Breaking long-chain alkanes into shorter alkanes + alkenes
CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O (complete combustion)
C₈H₁₈ → C₄H₁₀ + C₄H₈ (cracking)

Alkenes

Unsaturated hydrocarbons with general formula CnH₂n. They contain a C=C double bond and are more reactive than alkanes.

  • Combustion: Burns with sooty flame (higher C:H ratio)
  • Addition reactions: Characteristic reaction of alkenes
Addition of H₂ (hydrogenation): C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆ (Ni catalyst, 150°C)
Addition of Br₂ (bromination): C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂ (red-brown → colourless)
Addition of HBr: C₂H₄ + HBr → C₂H₅Br (Markovnikov: H to C with more H)
Addition of H₂O (hydration): C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH (H₃PO₄ catalyst, 300°C, 60 atm)

Tests for Unsaturation

Bromine water test: red-brown bromine water decolourises with alkenes (addition reaction). Alkanes do not react (no change, or very slow under UV).

Fossil Fuels and Environmental Impact

  • Crude oil is separated by fractional distillation
  • Combustion of fossil fuels produces CO₂ (greenhouse gas) and SO₂ (acid rain)
  • Catalytic converters remove CO, NOₓ, and unburnt hydrocarbons from exhaust

AS Level Content

Free Radical Substitution Mechanism

Mechanism for reaction of methane with chlorine (UV light):

Initiation: Cl₂ → 2Cl• (homolytic fission by UV)
Propagation: CH₄ + Cl• → CH₃• + HCl
                CH₃• + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•
Termination: Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂
                CH₃• + Cl• → CH₃Cl
                CH₃• + CH₃• → C₂H₆

Further substitution can occur: CH₃Cl → CH₂Cl₂ → CHCl₃ → CCl₄

Electrophilic Addition to Alkenes

The electron-rich C=C double bond attacks electrophiles.

Mechanism (bromination):
1. Br₂ polarised by C=C; Brδ+ attacked by π electrons → cyclic bromonium ion
2. Br⁻ attacks from opposite side (anti-addition) → dibromoalkane

Markovnikov's Rule

In addition of HX to unsymmetrical alkenes, H attaches to the carbon with more H atoms already (forming more stable carbocation).

CH₃CH=CH₂ + HBr → CH₃CHBrCH₃ (major product)
Not CH₃CH₂CH₂Br (minor product)

Ozonolysis of Alkenes

Alkenes react with ozone, then reductive workup (Zn/H₂O) gives carbonyl compounds. Used to locate position of double bond.

A2 Level Content

Arene Chemistry - Benzene

Benzene (C₆H₆) has a planar hexagonal structure with delocalised π electrons. It undergoes electrophilic substitution (not addition) because addition would destroy the stable aromatic system.

Electrophilic Substitution of Benzene

General mechanism:
1. Electrophile (E⁺) generated by catalyst
2. E⁺ attacked by π electrons of benzene → carbocation intermediate (arenium ion)
3. Loss of H⁺ restores aromaticity → substituted benzene

Nitration

Reagents: concentrated HNO₃ + concentrated H₂SO₄ (catalyst), 50°C
Electrophile: NO₂⁺ (nitronium ion)
C₆H₆ + HNO₃ → C₆H₅NO₂ + H₂O

Halogenation

Reagents: Br₂ + FeBr₃ catalyst (or AlBr₃)
Electrophile: Br⁺
C₆H₆ + Br₂ → C₆H₅Br + HBr

Chlorination uses Cl₂ + FeCl₃/AlCl₃

Friedel-Crafts Reactions

Alkylation: C₆H₆ + RCl → C₆H₅R + HCl (AlCl₃ catalyst)
Acylation: C₆H₆ + RCOCl → C₆H₅COR + HCl (AlCl₃ catalyst)

Directing Effects of Substituents

  • 2,4-directing (activating): -OH, -NH₂, -NHR, -NR₂, -R, -X — donate electrons, activate ring
  • 3-directing (deactivating): -NO₂, -COOH, -CHO, -COR, -CN, -SO₃H — withdraw electrons, deactivate ring

Phenol

Phenol (C₆H₅OH) has an -OH group directly attached to benzene. The lone pairs on oxygen delocalise into the ring, making it more reactive than benzene:

  • Reacts with bromine water without catalyst → white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol
  • Weakly acidic (pKa ~10) — more acidic than alcohols, less than carboxylic acids

Methylbenzene (Toluene)

Methyl group is 2,4-directing and activating. Methylbenzene is more reactive than benzene and undergoes substitution more readily.

🧪 Worked Example

Question: Calculate the mass of NaOH needed to make 500 cm³ of 0.2 mol/dm³ solution. (Mᵣ NaOH = 40)

Solution:
n = c × V = 0.2 × (500/1000) = 0.1 mol
mass = n × Mᵣ = 0.1 × 40 = 4.0 g
Answer: 4.0 g
Forgetting to convert cm³ to dm³ (÷1000) when using n = c × V. Always check your units!
In a balanced equation, mole ratios are the stoichiometric coefficients. Use these to convert between substances.

⚡ Quick Recall

  • n = mass / Mᵣ
  • n = concentration × volume (dm³)
  • 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³
  • % yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100

Last-Minute Summary

🎯 Key Points to Remember

  • Alkanes: saturated, substitution only (free radical, UV), combustion, cracking
  • Alkenes: addition reactions (H₂, Br₂, HX, H₂O); test with bromine water
  • Free radical substitution: initiation, propagation, termination
  • Markovnikov: H adds to C with more H; forms more stable carbocation
  • Benzene: electrophilic substitution (not addition); delocalised electrons
  • Nitration: HNO₃/H₂SO₄, 50°C; Halogenation: X₂ + FeX₃
  • Friedel-Crafts: Alkylation/acylation with RCl/RCOCl + AlCl₃
  • 2,4-directors: -OH, -NH₂, -R, -X (activating); 3-directors: -NO₂, -COOH, -CHO (deactivating)
  • Phenol: very reactive, decolourises bromine water, weakly acidic

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Saying alkanes undergo addition (they don't — only substitution/combustion)
  • Not showing homolytic fission correctly in radical mechanisms
  • Forgetting benzene undergoes substitution, not addition
  • Confusing directing effects of substituents
  • Not recognising that phenol is more acidic than alcohols due to resonance stabilisation of phenoxide ion
