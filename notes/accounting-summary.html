📒 Unit 1 — Financial Accounting

▼

🔄 The Accounting Cycle

Every transaction follows a journey: Source Documents → Books of Prime Entry → Ledgers → Trial Balance → Financial Statements. Miss one step and the whole system breaks.

⚖️ Double Entry — The Golden Rule

Every debit has an equal credit. DEAD CLIC: Debits increase Expenses, Assets, Drawings. Credits increase Liabilities, Income, Capital. If your trial balance doesn't balance, something's wrong.

Mnemonic: DEAD CLIC

📚 Books of Prime Entry

Sales Day Book (credit sales), Purchases Day Book (credit purchases), Cash Book (all cash/bank), Journal (non-routine), Petty Cash Book (small expenses). These are the first place transactions are recorded before posting to ledgers.

🔍 Bank Reconciliation

Why doesn't your cash book match your bank statement? Unpresented cheques (you recorded, bank hasn't), uncredited deposits (you paid in, bank hasn't processed), bank charges, standing orders, dishonoured cheques. Always update the cash book first, then reconcile.

⚠️ Case Study: The Suspense Account

A trial balance is $500 out. Investigation finds: a $250 sales invoice was only credited to the customer (no debit), and a $250 purchase was debited twice. Both errors go through the suspense account — a temporary holding account that must balance to zero once all errors are found.

📊 Unit 2 — Financial Statements

▼

👤 Sole Trader Accounts

Income Statement: Revenue − Cost of Sales = Gross Profit. Gross Profit − Expenses = Net Profit. Adjust for accruals, prepayments, depreciation, bad debts, and provision for doubtful debts.

🤝 Partnership Accounts

Partners share profits according to the partnership agreement. The appropriation account shows: Interest on capital (reward for investment), Salaries (reward for work), Residual profit (shared in profit-sharing ratio). Current accounts track each partner's drawings and share.

Exam tip: Always check for guaranteed minimum profit for new partners

🏢 Limited Companies

Share capital: Authorised (max allowed), Issued (actually sold), Called-up (asked for), Paid-up (received). Types of shares: Ordinary (voting rights, variable dividends) vs Preference (fixed dividend, no voting). Reserves: Share premium, revaluation, general, retained earnings.

🏭 Manufacturing Accounts

Prime Cost = Direct Materials + Direct Labour + Direct Expenses. Factory Overheads = Indirect costs (rent, power, depreciation, supervisor salaries). Total Manufacturing Cost = Prime Cost + Factory Overheads. Add opening WIP, deduct closing WIP = Cost of Production transferred to Income Statement.

💡
Did you know? Enron (2001) used off-balance-sheet entities to hide $38bn in debt. They exploited accounting loopholes in Special Purpose Entities (SPEs). The scandal led to the Sarbanes-Oxley Act and the collapse of Arthur Andersen — one of the "Big Five" accounting firms.

📈 Unit 3 — Analysis & Communication

▼

💰 Profitability Ratios

Gross Profit Margin: How much of each sale is gross profit? Net Profit Margin: How much of each sale is net profit? ROCE: Return on capital employed — the ultimate measure of efficiency. Higher is better, but compare against industry averages.

💧 Liquidity Ratios

Current ratio (ideal 1.5–2.0): Can we pay short-term debts? Acid test (ideal 1.0+): Same, but excluding inventory (might not sell). Below 1.0 = danger zone. Above 2.0 = might be holding too much idle cash.

Warning: A high current ratio can hide slow-moving stock

⚙️ Efficiency Ratios

Inventory days: How long stock sits in the warehouse? Receivables days: How long customers take to pay? Payables days: How long we take to pay suppliers? The goal: collect fast, pay slow (but not too slow — damages relationships).

📊 Cash Flow Statements (IAS 7)

Three sections: Operating (day-to-day business), Investing (buying/selling assets), Financing (loans, shares, dividends). A company can be profitable but have negative cash flow — this is how businesses fail despite profit.

📉 Case Study: Comet Electricals (2012)

Comet was profitable on paper but had negative operating cash flow — customers weren't paying, and suppliers demanded cash. The business collapsed within months. Profit ≠ Cash.

🧮 Unit 4 — Cost & Management Accounting

▼

🏷️ Cost Classification

By element: Materials, Labour, Overheads. By behaviour: Fixed (rent, salaries), Variable (raw materials), Semi-variable (utilities). By traceability: Direct (traceable to product) vs Indirect (must be apportioned).

📐 Overhead Absorption

Three stages: Allocation (direct assignment) → Apportionment (fair sharing by floor area, machine hours, employees) → Absorption (charge to products using OAR). Under-absorption = not enough overhead charged. Over-absorption = too much charged.

📉 Marginal Costing & Break-even

Contribution = Selling Price − Variable Cost. Break-even = Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution per unit. Margin of safety = how much sales can fall before loss. C/S ratio = Contribution ÷ Sales. Marginal costing is for decisions; absorption costing is for financial reporting.

📋 Standard Costing & Variances

Set a standard cost, compare with actual, investigate differences. Material price variance: Did we pay more/less than expected? Material usage variance: Did we use more/less than expected? Same for labour (rate and efficiency). Favourable = good for profit. Adverse = bad.

Eval tip: Don't blame workers for adverse variances — check material quality first

💹 Investment Appraisal

Payback: How long to recover investment? Simple but ignores later cash flows. NPV: Discount future cash flows — if positive, accept. IRR: The discount rate where NPV = 0. If IRR > cost of capital, accept. ARR: Average profit ÷ average investment.

💡
Did you know? The Concorde fallacy (sunk cost fallacy) is named after the failed supersonic jet. British and French governments kept investing because they'd already spent so much — ignoring that future costs exceeded future benefits. In accounting, sunk costs are irrelevant to future decisions.
