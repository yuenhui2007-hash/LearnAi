IBE1: Microeconomics

Overview

Introduction to Economics (Topic 1) establishes the foundational concepts of scarcity, choice, and opportunity cost. It also introduces the methodological framework of economics, including the distinction between positive and normative statements. This topic underpins all subsequent economic analysis and is tested in Paper 1 (essays) and Paper 2 (data response).

1.1 Scarcity, Choice & Opportunity Cost

Economics is the study of how individuals, firms, and societies allocate scarce resources to satisfy unlimited wants.

Key Definitions

  • Scarcity: the fundamental economic problem that resources are limited relative to unlimited wants.
  • Choice: because of scarcity, decisions must be made about how to allocate resources.
  • Opportunity cost: the value of the next best alternative foregone when a choice is made.
  • Free good: unlimited in supply with no opportunity cost (e.g., air).
  • Economic good: scarce and has an opportunity cost.

Worked Example 1: Opportunity Cost

Question: A government has $1 billion to spend. It can build a hospital (estimated benefit: 800 QALYs) or a highway (estimated benefit: 600 QALYs). What is the opportunity cost of building the hospital?

Solution:

Opportunity cost = 600 QALYs (the benefit of the next best alternative, the highway).

1.2 Factors of Production

  • Land: natural resources (e.g., oil, forests, water).
  • Labor: human effort, both physical and mental.
  • Capital: manufactured goods used to produce other goods (e.g., machinery, factories).
  • Enterprise (Entrepreneurship): the ability to combine the other factors and take risks.

Each factor earns a corresponding reward: land → rent, labor → wages, capital → interest, enterprise → profit.

1.3 Production Possibility Curve (PPC)

The PPC shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce using all resources efficiently.

  • Points on the curve: productive efficiency.
  • Points inside the curve: inefficiency or unemployment.
  • Points outside the curve: unattainable with current resources.
Opportunity cost of producing more of one good = quantity of the other good given up.
Law of increasing opportunity cost: PPC is bowed outwards because resources are not perfectly transferable.

Shifts in the PPC

  • Outward shift: economic growth (more resources, better technology, improved labour productivity).
  • Inward shift: economic decline (war, natural disaster, emigration).

1.4 Economic Systems

  • Free market economy: resources allocated by price mechanism and private ownership. Advantages: efficiency, consumer sovereignty, innovation. Disadvantages: inequality, public goods underprovided, externalities.
  • Planned (command) economy: government makes all economic decisions. Advantages: reduced inequality, provision of public goods. Disadvantages: inefficiency, lack of innovation, shortages/surpluses.
  • Mixed economy: combines market and government intervention. Most modern economies are mixed.

1.5 Positive vs. Normative Statements

  • Positive: objective, fact-based, testable (e.g., "An increase in the minimum wage reduces employment by 2%").
  • Normative: subjective, value-based, prescriptive (e.g., "The minimum wage should be increased to reduce poverty").

IB examiners expect students to distinguish between these in evaluation questions.

Common Misconceptions

  • Scarcity: Scarcity is not the same as shortage. Scarcity is permanent; shortages are temporary market conditions.
  • Opportunity cost: It is the next best alternative, not all alternatives foregone.
  • Capital: In economics, capital means physical capital (machinery), not financial capital (money).
  • PPC: Movement along the PPC shows a reallocation of resources; a shift shows a change in productive capacity.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1 (Part a): 10-mark questions often ask for definitions and diagrams (e.g., "Explain the concept of opportunity cost using a PPC diagram").
  • Paper 1 (Part b): 15-mark evaluation questions may ask whether market or planned economies are preferable.
  • Paper 2: Data response questions may require calculation of opportunity cost from production data.
  • IA: Internal Assessment commentary often involves identifying opportunity costs in real-world policy decisions.

  • Opportunity cost = next best alternative foregone.

IBE2: Macroeconomics

Overview

Microeconomics (Topic 2) examines how markets function, how prices are determined, and why markets sometimes fail. It is the largest topic in IB Economics and is tested across all papers. Diagrammatic analysis is essential — examiners award marks for correctly drawn and labelled supply and demand diagrams.

2.1 Demand & Supply

Demand

  • Law of demand: as price falls, quantity demanded rises (ceteris paribus).
  • Determinants of demand: income, tastes, price of substitutes/complements, expectations, number of buyers.
Movement along the demand curve: caused by price change of the good itself.
Shift of the demand curve: caused by non-price determinants.

Supply

  • Law of supply: as price rises, quantity supplied rises (ceteris paribus).
  • Determinants of supply: input prices, technology, taxes/subsidies, number of sellers, expectations.

Market Equilibrium

Equilibrium occurs where demand equals supply. At this price, there is no surplus or shortage.

2.2 Elasticity

Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

PED = (% change in quantity demanded) / (% change in price)
PED > 1: elastic (quantity responsive to price)
PED < 1: inelastic (quantity unresponsive to price)
PED = 1: unit elastic
PED = 0: perfectly inelastic; PED = ∞: perfectly elastic

Determinants of PED: availability of substitutes, necessity vs. luxury, proportion of income, time period.

Income Elasticity of Demand (YED)

YED = (% change in quantity demanded) / (% change in income)
YED > 0: normal good; YED < 0: inferior good
YED > 1: luxury good; 0 < YED < 1: necessity

Cross Elasticity of Demand (XED)

XED = (% change in Qd of good A) / (% change in price of good B)
XED > 0: substitutes; XED < 0: complements

Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)

PES = (% change in quantity supplied) / (% change in price)
Determinants: spare capacity, mobility of factors, time period, ease of storage

2.3 Consumer & Producer Surplus

  • Consumer surplus: difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay (area below demand, above price).
  • Producer surplus: difference between what producers receive and their minimum acceptable price (area above supply, below price).
  • Total surplus: consumer surplus + producer surplus — maximised at competitive equilibrium.

2.4 Market Failure

Market failure occurs when the free market allocates resources inefficiently.

Externalities

  • Negative externality: social cost > private cost (e.g., pollution). Leads to overproduction.
  • Positive externality: social benefit > private benefit (e.g., education). Leads to underproduction.

Public Goods

  • Non-excludable: cannot prevent anyone from consuming.
  • Non-rivalrous: one person's consumption does not reduce availability for others.
  • Examples: national defence, street lighting. Free-rider problem means private markets underprovide.

Common Access Resources

  • Rivalrous but non-excludable (e.g., fish stocks, clean air).
  • Tragedy of the commons: overuse due to lack of property rights.

Asymmetric Information

  • One party has more information than another.
  • Moral hazard: hidden action (e.g., insurance encouraging risky behaviour).
  • Adverse selection: hidden characteristics (e.g., sick people buying more insurance).

2.5 Government Intervention

  • Price ceilings: maximum price (e.g., rent control). Causes shortages if set below equilibrium.
  • Price floors: minimum price (e.g., minimum wage). Causes surpluses if set above equilibrium.
  • Indirect taxes: shift supply left; reduce quantity, increase price.
  • Subsidies: shift supply right; increase quantity, decrease price.
  • Regulation: direct control of behaviour (e.g., emissions standards).
  • Tradable permits: market-based solution to negative externalities.

Common Misconceptions

  • Demand vs. quantity demanded: A change in price causes movement along the curve (Qd); other factors shift the curve (D).
  • PED sign: PED is usually stated as an absolute value (positive), even though the calculation gives a negative.
  • Externalities: The existence of an externality means market output is inefficient, not that the market fails completely.
  • Price controls: A price ceiling above equilibrium has no effect; a price floor below equilibrium has no effect.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1 (Part a): 10-mark questions on elasticity calculations, consumer/producer surplus, and market failure diagrams.
  • Paper 1 (Part b): 15-mark evaluations of government intervention policies.
  • Paper 2: Data response with elasticity calculations and policy recommendations.
  • IA: Microeconomics commentaries on market failure and government intervention are very common.

Past Paper Trends (2019–2024)

November 2023: Evaluation of carbon taxes vs. tradable permits.
May 2023: PED calculation and its relevance to business revenue.
November 2022: Negative externalities of production with welfare loss diagram.
May 2022: Price floor analysis in agricultural markets.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Demand: inverse price–quantity relationship; shift = non-price factors.
  • Supply: direct price–quantity relationship.
  • PED: responsiveness of Qd to price; |PED| > 1 = elastic.
  • Externalities: social cost/benefit differs from private → market failure.

  • Government tools: taxes, subsidies, price controls, regulation, tradable permits.

IBE3: International

Overview

Macroeconomics (Topic 3) studies the economy as a whole, focusing on national income, inflation, unemployment, and government policies. It is heavily diagrammatic and is tested across all papers. Understanding the AD/AS model and policy evaluation is critical for high marks.

3.1 Measuring National Income

GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)
C = consumption, I = investment, G = government spending, X = exports, M = imports

Nominal GDP: measured at current prices
Real GDP: measured at constant prices (adjusted for inflation)
GDP deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100

Limitations of GDP

  • Does not measure income distribution.
  • Ignores environmental degradation.
  • Excludes informal/non-market activities.
  • Does not account for leisure time or volunteer work.

3.2 Aggregate Demand & Supply

Aggregate Demand (AD)

AD = C + I + G + (X − M)
AD shifts right: increased consumption, investment, government spending, or net exports.
AD shifts left: decreased components.

Aggregate Supply (AS)

  • Short-run AS (SRAS): upward sloping — as price level rises, firms produce more (wages and input prices sticky).
  • Long-run AS (LRAS): vertical at full employment output — determined by factors of production and technology.

Macroeconomic Equilibrium

Equilibrium occurs where AD = SRAS. In the long run, equilibrium is where AD = SRAS = LRAS (full employment).

3.3 Inflation

Inflation: sustained increase in the general price level.

Inflation rate = [(CPI_current − CPI_previous) / CPI_previous] × 100%
CPI = (cost of basket in current year / cost of basket in base year) × 100

Causes of Inflation

  • Demand-pull: AD increases faster than AS ("too much money chasing too few goods").
  • Cost-push: SRAS shifts left due to rising input costs (e.g., oil prices, wages).

Consequences of Inflation

  • Reduces purchasing power of money.
  • Redistributes income (hurt: fixed-income earners, savers; helped: borrowers).
  • Menu costs and shoe-leather costs.
  • Uncertainty reduces investment.

3.4 Unemployment

  • Frictional: temporary unemployment between jobs.
  • Structural: skills/location mismatch; caused by technological change or deindustrialisation.
  • Cyclical (demand-deficient): caused by recession (AD < AS).
  • Seasonal: predictable seasonal patterns (e.g., tourism, agriculture).
Unemployment rate = (number unemployed / labour force) × 100%

3.5 Fiscal Policy

  • Expansionary: decrease taxes, increase government spending → AD shifts right.
  • Contractionary: increase taxes, decrease government spending → AD shifts left.

Limitations: time lags, crowding out, political constraints, reliance on multiplier size.

3.6 Monetary Policy

  • Controlled by the central bank.
  • Interest rates: lower rates stimulate borrowing and investment (AD shifts right).
  • Quantitative easing: central bank buys bonds to increase money supply.

Limitations: liquidity trap, ineffective if banks won't lend, time lags.

3.7 Supply-Side Policies

  • Policies to increase LRAS (productive capacity).
  • Examples: education/training, privatisation, deregulation, infrastructure investment, R&D subsidies.
  • Advantage: non-inflationary growth. Disadvantage: long time lags.

Common Misconceptions

  • Nominal vs. real GDP: Real GDP adjusts for inflation; nominal does not.
  • AD shift: A change in the price level causes movement along AD, not a shift.
  • Full employment: Does not mean zero unemployment — frictional and structural unemployment still exist.
  • Fiscal vs. monetary: Fiscal = government; Monetary = central bank.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1 (Part a): 10-mark questions on AD/AS diagrams, inflation causes, and unemployment types.
  • Paper 1 (Part b): 15-mark evaluations of macroeconomic policies.
  • Paper 2: Data response with GDP calculations, inflation rate, and policy recommendations.
  • IA: Macroeconomics commentaries on monetary/fiscal policy are very common.

Past Paper Trends (2019–2024)

November 2023: Evaluation of supply-side policies for reducing unemployment.
May 2023: AD/AS analysis of post-pandemic inflation.
November 2022: Monetary policy effectiveness in a liquidity trap.
May 2022: GDP calculation and limitations as a welfare measure.

  • Explain the difference between nominal GDP and real GDP.
  • Draw an AD/AS diagram showing demand-pull inflation and explain the process.

Last-Minute Summary

  • GDP = C + I + G + (X − M); real GDP adjusts for inflation.
  • AD = C + I + G + (X − M); SRAS upward sloping; LRAS vertical.
  • Demand-pull inflation: AD increases; cost-push: SRAS decreases.
  • Unemployment types: frictional, structural, cyclical, seasonal.
  • Fiscal: taxes and government spending. Monetary: interest rates and money supply.
  • Supply-side: increase productive capacity (education, infrastructure, deregulation).

  • Draw accurate AD/AS diagrams with correct axis labels (Price Level, Real GDP).
  • Show shifts with arrows and label new equilibrium clearly.
  • In evaluation, mention time lags, crowding out, and political constraints.
  • Use real-world examples (e.g., US Fed, ECB, COVID-19 stimulus).

IBE4: Development

Overview

The Global Economy (Topic 4) examines international trade, exchange rates, the balance of payments, and development economics. It connects micro and macro concepts to the global context and is tested across all papers. Understanding exchange rate mechanisms and trade theory is essential.

4.1 International Trade

Comparative Advantage

A country should specialise in producing goods where it has a lower opportunity cost.

Opportunity cost of good X = quantity of good Y given up / quantity of good X gained
Countries gain from trade by specialising and trading at a rate between their opportunity costs.

Trade Protection

  • Tariffs: taxes on imports → higher domestic price, reduced quantity imported.
  • Quotas: physical limit on imports → higher price, reduced quantity.
  • Subsidies: domestic producers receive payments → lower costs, more competitive.

Arguments for protection: infant industries, national security, diversification, retaliation against dumping.

Arguments against: higher prices for consumers, reduced efficiency, risk of retaliation.

4.2 Exchange Rates

Determination of Exchange Rates

  • Floating: determined by demand and supply in foreign exchange markets.
  • Fixed: government/pegged to another currency or basket.
  • Managed float: mostly market-determined with occasional government intervention.

Factors Affecting Exchange Rates

  • Interest rate differentials.
  • Inflation differentials.
  • Current account balance.
  • Speculation and confidence.

Effects of Exchange Rate Changes

  • Appreciation: exports more expensive, imports cheaper → current account may worsen (Marshall-Lerner condition).
  • Depreciation: exports cheaper, imports more expensive → current account may improve.

4.3 Balance of Payments

Current account = trade in goods + trade in services + income + transfers
Capital account + financial account = capital transfers + investment flows
Current account + capital account + financial account = 0 (with statistical discrepancy)

Causes of Current Account Deficits

  • Loss of competitiveness (high inflation, low productivity).
  • Strong currency.
  • High marginal propensity to import.
  • Reliance on imports for energy/raw materials.

Policies to Correct Deficits

  • Expenditure-reducing (contractionary fiscal/monetary).
  • Expenditure-switching (depreciation, protectionism, supply-side policies).

4.4 Economic Development

  • Economic growth: increase in real GDP.
  • Economic development: broader improvement in living standards, including health, education, and equality.

Development Indicators

  • GDP per capita (PPP adjusted).
  • Human Development Index (HDI) — combines income, education, life expectancy.
  • Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI).
  • Gini coefficient — income inequality (0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality).

Barriers to Development

  • Poor infrastructure, education, and healthcare.
  • Political instability and corruption.
  • Geographic disadvantages (landlocked, tropical diseases).
  • Debt burdens and unequal trade terms.

Development Strategies

  • Import substitution vs. export-led growth.
  • Microfinance and fair trade.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI).
  • Structural adjustment programmes (controversial).

4.5 Sustainability

  • Sustainable development: meeting present needs without compromising future generations.
  • Trade-off between economic growth and environmental protection.
  • Renewable energy, carbon pricing, and international agreements (Paris Agreement).

Common Misconceptions

  • Absolute vs. comparative advantage: Comparative advantage is about opportunity cost, not absolute productivity.
  • Appreciation and exports: Currency appreciation makes exports more expensive in foreign currency terms, not domestic terms.
  • Current account deficit: A deficit is not always bad — it may reflect investment and growth.
  • Growth vs. development: Growth is quantitative (GDP); development is qualitative (HDI, living standards).

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1 (Part a): 10-mark questions on comparative advantage, exchange rates, and trade protection.
  • Paper 1 (Part b): 15-mark evaluations of development strategies or trade policies.
  • Paper 2: Data response with exchange rate calculations, balance of payments data, and development indicators.
  • IA: Global economy commentaries on exchange rates, trade, or development are common.

Past Paper Trends (2019–2024)

May 2023: Exchange rate depreciation and the J-curve effect.
November 2022: HDI and GDP per capita as development measures.
May 2022: Comparative advantage calculation with opportunity costs.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Comparative advantage: specialise where opportunity cost is lowest.
  • Trade protection: tariffs, quotas, subsidies — know effects on welfare.
  • Exchange rates: floating (market), fixed (pegged), managed.
  • Current account = trade + services + income + transfers.
  • Development: broader than growth; measured by HDI, MPI, Gini.
  • Sustainability: balancing present and future needs.

Exam Tips

  • Draw accurate diagrams for tariffs and exchange rates.
  • Use real-world examples (e.g., China's export-led growth, EU tariffs).
  • In evaluation, consider short-run vs. long-run and different stakeholders.
  • For development questions, use data from the World Bank or UN.
