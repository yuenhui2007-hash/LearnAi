IGCSE A2 Level

Electromagnetism

9702 (A-Level) · 0625 (IGCSE)

Detailed Notes

1. Magnetic Fields

A magnetic field is a region of space where a moving charge or magnetic material experiences a force. Magnetic field lines show the direction a free north pole would move.

  • Field around a straight wire: Concentric circles. Direction given by the right-hand grip rule (thumb in direction of current, fingers curl in direction of field).
  • Field around a solenoid: Similar to a bar magnet — uniform inside, diverging outside. North pole is the end where field lines emerge.
  • Field around a flat coil: Like a short solenoid; strongest at the centre.

📖 Key Definition

Magnetic Flux Density (B): A measure of the strength of a magnetic field. Defined as the force per unit current per unit length on a current-carrying conductor placed perpendicular to the field. Unit: tesla (T).

2. Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force due to the interaction between the magnetic field of the current and the external field.

F = BIL sin θ

where B = magnetic flux density, I = current, L = length of conductor in the field, θ = angle between current direction and field direction. Maximum force occurs when θ = 90° (conductor perpendicular to field).

The direction of the force is given by Fleming's left-hand rule: First finger = Field, seCond finger = Current, thuMb = Motion (force).

3. Force on a Moving Charge

A charged particle moving in a magnetic field experiences a force. This is the basis of particle accelerators, mass spectrometers, and cathode ray tubes.

F = BQv sin θ

For a charged particle moving perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field (θ = 90°), the force is always perpendicular to the velocity, causing circular motion:

r = mv/(BQ) (radius of circular path)
T = 2πm/(BQ) (period, independent of velocity!)

📝 Worked Example — Circular Motion in B-field

Question: An electron (mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, charge 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) moves at 2.0 × 10⁷ m/s perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of 0.010 T. Calculate the radius of its path.

Solution:

r = mv/(Be) = (9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 2.0 × 10⁷)/(0.010 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)

r = 1.822 × 10⁻²³ / 1.6 × 10⁻²¹ = 1.14 × 10⁻² m (1.14 cm)

4. Magnetic Flux and Flux Linkage

Magnetic flux Φ through an area A perpendicular to a uniform field B:

Φ = BA (unit: weber, Wb)

For a coil of N turns, the flux linkage is:

Flux linkage = NΦ = BAN (unit: Wb turns)

5. Electromagnetic Induction

An e.m.f. is induced in a conductor when there is a change in magnetic flux linkage. This can occur by:

  • Moving a conductor through a magnetic field (motional e.m.f.)
  • Changing the magnetic field through a stationary coil
  • Changing the area or orientation of a coil in a magnetic field
Faraday's Law: ε = -N(dΦ/dt) = -d(NΦ)/dt
Lenz's Law: The induced current flows in a direction to oppose the change causing it.
The negative sign in Faraday's Law represents Lenz's Law. If the magnetic flux through a coil is increasing, the induced e.m.f. drives a current that creates a field opposing the increase. Lenz's Law is a manifestation of conservation of energy.

6. Transformer

A transformer changes alternating voltage levels using electromagnetic induction. It consists of two coils (primary and secondary) wound on a soft iron core.

V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p
For an ideal transformer: V_p I_p = V_s I_s

Step-up transformer: N_s > N_p → V_s > V_p (increases voltage, decreases current). Step-down: N_s < N_p → V_s < V_p.

Transformers only work with alternating current (a.c.) because they require a changing magnetic flux. In a real transformer, energy losses occur due to: resistance of windings (I²R heating), eddy currents in the core, and hysteresis losses. Laminating the core reduces eddy currents.

📝 Worked Example — Transformer

Question: A step-down transformer has 1200 turns on the primary coil and 60 turns on the secondary. The primary is connected to 240 V a.c. and the secondary delivers 2.0 A to a load. Assuming 100% efficiency, calculate: (a) the secondary voltage, (b) the primary current.

Solution:

(a) V_s = V_p × N_s/N_p = 240 × 60/1200 = 12 V

(b) I_p = I_s × V_s/V_p = 2.0 × 12/240 = 0.10 A

⚠️ Common Misconception

Students often think that transformers can work with d.c. They cannot! A constant current in the primary produces a constant magnetic field, so there is no changing flux and no induced e.m.f. in the secondary. Transformers require a.c. or pulsed d.c. to function.

🎯 Exam-Style Question

Question: A straight wire of length 25 cm carries a current of 4.0 A. It is placed perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field and experiences a force of 0.15 N. Calculate the magnetic flux density. [2 marks]

Mark Scheme

• F = BIL → B = F/(IL) (1 mark)

• B = 0.15/(4.0 × 0.25) = 0.15 T (1 mark)

🎯 Exam-Style Question

Question: (a) State Lenz's law. (b) A coil of 80 turns and cross-sectional area 5.0 cm² is placed perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field. The field changes from 0.20 T to 0.80 T in 0.40 s. Calculate the magnitude of the induced e.m.f. [4 marks]

Mark Scheme

• (a) The direction of the induced e.m.f. (or current) is such as to oppose the change producing it (1 mark)

• (b) ΔΦ = ΔB × A = (0.80 − 0.20) × 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ Wb (1 mark)

• ε = N × ΔΦ/Δt = 80 × 3.0 × 10⁻⁴/0.40 (1 mark)

• ε = 0.060 V (accept 60 mV) (1 mark)

Last-Minute Summary

  • Force on wire: F = BIL sin θ (Fleming's left-hand rule)
  • Force on charge: F = BQv sin θ
  • B = F/IL (T) — defined from force on current-carrying conductor
  • Circular motion in B-field: r = mv/(BQ), T = 2πm/(BQ)
  • Magnetic flux: Φ = BA (Wb); Flux linkage = NΦ
  • Faraday's Law: ε = -N(dΦ/dt)
  • Lenz's Law: induced effect opposes the change
  • Transformer: V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p; ideal: V_p I_p = V_s I_s
  • Step-up: N_s>N_p; Step-down: N_s

Exam Tips

  • Always use Fleming's left-hand rule for force/motor effect, and Fleming's right-hand rule for induction/generator effect.
  • For circular motion in a magnetic field, the magnetic force provides the centripetal force: BQv = mv²/r.
  • Remember that the period T of circular motion in a B-field is independent of velocity — this is crucial in cyclotrons.
  • In transformer calculations, use the turns ratio for voltage and the inverse ratio for current (for ideal transformers).
  • When calculating induced e.m.f., use the magnitude of change and add the direction based on Lenz's Law separately.
  • Transformers require a.c. — never say they work with d.c.
