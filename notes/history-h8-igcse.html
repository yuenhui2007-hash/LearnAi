📘 Subject Context

Hitler's Germany, 1929–1947 is a significant area of study in IGCSE History. Understanding the causes, events, and consequences of historical developments is essential for constructing analytical essays and source-based answers.

IGCSE History examines knowledge, source analysis, and essay-writing skills. Paper 1 tests depth studies through structured questions; Paper 2 tests breadth studies through source analysis and essays. Always use specific evidence.

Hitler's Germany, 1929–1947

Cambridge 9389 A-Level / 0470 IGCSE

📘 IGCSE Content

The Rise of the Nazis (1929–33)

  • Wall Street Crash (Oct 1929) triggered global depression. German economy collapsed: unemployment reached 6 million (1932), GDP fell 40%.
  • Weimar Republic fragile: proportional representation created unstable coalitions; Article 48 allowed presidential rule by decree.
  • Nazi appeal: promised economic recovery, national revival, anti-communism, anti-Semitism. Hitler's oratory and propaganda (Goebbels).
  • SA (Sturmabteilung): paramilitary force; street battles with Communists. SS (Schutzstaffel): originally Hitler's bodyguard, later elite force under Himmler.
  • 1930 election: Nazis won 107 seats (18.3%) — up from 12 (2.6%) in 1928.
  • 1932: Nazis peaked — 230 seats (37.3%) in July election. Hitler ran for president against Hindenburg (lost).
  • 30 Jan 1933: Hitler appointed Chancellor by Hindenburg. Only two Nazis in cabinet (Hitler and Frick). Conservatives (Papen, Schleicher) believed they could control him.

Consolidation of Power (1933–34)

  • Reichstag Fire (27 Feb 1933): Dutch communist van der Lubbe arrested. Decree for the Protection of People and State (28 Feb): suspended civil liberties, allowed arrest without trial.
  • March 1933 election: Nazis won 288 seats (43.9%) with coalition partners (DNVP) giving majority. Enabling Act (23 March): gave Hitler power to rule by decree for 4 years. Passed with Centre Party support.
  • Gleichschaltung (coordination): All institutions brought under Nazi control. Trade unions abolished (2 May 1933); replaced by DAF (German Labour Front). Political parties banned (July 1933).
  • Night of the Long Knives (30 June 1934): Hitler purged SA leadership (Röhm and others) — 85–200 killed. SS gained power. Army approved.
  • Hindenburg's death (2 Aug 1934): Hitler merged offices of president and chancellor — became Führer. Army swore personal oath of loyalty.

Nazi Germany 1933–39: Society and Economy

  • Economy: Public works (Autobahns), rearmament (conscription 1935), Mefo bills (off-budget financing). Unemployment fell from 6 million (1932) to under 1 million (1936). However, wages stagnated; working hours increased.
  • Propaganda: Goebbels as Minister of Propaganda. Control of press, radio, cinema, rallies (Nuremberg rallies), Olympics (1936).
  • Education and Youth: Curriculum Nazified; teachers purged. Hitler Youth (compulsory 1936): indoctrination in racial ideology and militarism. League of German Girls.
  • Women: Encouraged to be mothers and homemakers. Honour Cross of the German Mother (bronze for 4 children, silver for 6, gold for 8). Professional women and university students reduced.
  • Religion: Reich Church (pro-Nazi) vs Confessing Church (opposition, e.g., Pastor Niemöller). Concordat with Vatican (1933) violated.
  • Racial policy: Nuremberg Laws (1935); persecution of Jews intensified (see Holocaust notes). Also persecution of Roma, disabled, homosexuals, Jehovah's Witnesses.

Foreign Policy and Road to War

  • Withdrawal from League of Nations (1933). Rearmament announced (1935).
  • Rhineland remilitarised (March 1936): troops entered demilitarised zone. France and Britain did not act.
  • Anschluss with Austria (March 1938): union forbidden by Versailles. Achieved without war.
  • Munich Agreement (Sept 1938): Hitler demanded Sudetenland (German-speaking part of Czechoslovakia). Britain (Chamberlain) and France agreed. "Peace for our time."
  • March 1939: Germany occupied rest of Czechoslovakia — broke Munich promises. Britain guaranteed Poland.
  • Nazi-Soviet Pact (Aug 1939): non-aggression treaty with secret protocol dividing Eastern Europe.
  • 1 Sept 1939: Germany invaded Poland. Britain and France declared war (3 Sept).

War and Defeat (1939–45)

  • Blitzkrieg victories: Poland (Sept 1939), Denmark and Norway (April 1940), Netherlands, Belgium, France (May–June 1940). Dunkirk evacuation (May–June 1940).
  • Battle of Britain (July–Oct 1940): Luftwaffe failed to gain air superiority; invasion of Britain cancelled.
  • Operation Barbarossa (June 1941): invasion of USSR. Initial gains; stalled at Moscow (Dec 1941). Declaration of war on USA (Dec 1941) after Pearl Harbor.
  • Turning points: Stalingrad (Feb 1943), El Alamein (Nov 1942), Kursk (July 1943).
  • D-Day (6 June 1944): Allied invasion of Normandy. Soviet advance from East.
  • Hitler's suicide (30 April 1945); Germany surrendered (7–8 May 1945).

Occupation and Nuremberg (1945–47)

  • Germany divided into four zones (USA, UK, France, USSR). Berlin also divided.
  • Denazification: trials of war criminals. Nuremberg Trials (Nov 1945–Oct 1946): 22 major Nazis tried; 12 sentenced to death (including Göring, Ribbentrop, Kaltenbrunner). Hess life imprisonment.
  • Morgenthau Plan (initial) vs. policy of reconstruction. By 1947, Cold War tensions led to creation of West German state.

📗 AS Level Content

Why Did Hitler Come to Power?

  • Great Depression: economic catastrophe discredited Weimar democracy. Unemployed turned to extremes (Nazis and Communists).
  • Weimar weaknesses: proportional representation, presidential cabinets (Brüning, Papen, Schleicher), lack of democratic culture in some sectors.
  • Conservative elite miscalculation: Papen and Hindenburg believed they could use Hitler's mass support while controlling him. "We've hired him" (Papen).
  • Nazi strengths: effective propaganda, Hitler's charisma, organisation (Gauleiters), appeal across classes (lower middle class, farmers, some workers).
  • Not inevitable: Nazis lost 2 million votes between July and Nov 1932. Hitler appointed by elite manoeuvring, not popular majority.

The Nazi State: Totalitarian or Polycratic?

  • Totalitarian model: Hitler had absolute power; ideology permeated all aspects of life; terror (Gestapo, SS, camps) enforced conformity.
  • Polycratic model (historians like Martin Broszat, Ian Kershaw): Nazi state was chaotic, with competing agencies and personal rivalries. Hitler was a "weak dictator" who often avoided decisions, allowing subordinates to compete.
  • Source evaluation: use of decrees, Hitler's daily routine (late mornings, aversion to paperwork), power of Himmler, Goebbels, Göring.

Consensus and Coercion

  • How much did Germans support the regime? Some historians emphasise popular consent (economic recovery, national pride, effective propaganda). Others emphasise fear and surveillance.
  • Gestapo relatively small (approx. 32,000 for 66 million people). Relied on denunciation by citizens.
  • Opposition: isolated and fragmented. Communist and Socialist undergrounds. White Rose (Munich students, 1942–43). July 20 Plot (1944): Stauffenberg's bomb failed to kill Hitler. Army officers, some clergy (Niemöller, Bonhoeffer).

📙 A2 Level Content

The Intentionalist vs Structuralist Debate on Nazi Foreign Policy

  • Intentionalist (e.g., Klaus Hildebrand, Andreas Hillgruber): Hitler had a clear programme from Mein Kampf (1925): destroy Versailles, expand eastward (Lebensraum), destroy Jews. War was inevitable because Hitler planned it.
  • Structuralist/Functionalist (e.g., Hans Mommsen, Timothy Mason): Nazi foreign policy was more improvised. Economic pressures (need for resources) and internal dynamics drove expansion. Hitler was an opportunist who exploited situations.
  • Mason's "domestic crisis" thesis: expansion was driven by need to solve economic problems and prevent social unrest. War in 1939 was premature because Germany was not ready.
  • A.J.P. Taylor, The Origins of the Second World War (1961): Hitler was a traditional German statesman pursuing traditional aims; other powers mishandled him. Highly controversial.

Hitler as War Leader

  • Initial successes due to boldness and Allied unpreparedness. But strategic errors: invasion of USSR, declaration of war on USA, no retreat orders (Stalingrad).
  • "No surrender" policy wasted German lives. Hitler increasingly detached from reality.
  • Total war: Goebbels' Sportpalast speech (Feb 1943) called for total mobilisation. Speer's armaments production increased despite bombing.

Essay Frameworks

  • "Hitler was a weak dictator": Polycratic state, avoided decisions, chaotic administration. Counter: Hitler made key decisions (Night of Long Knives, war), Führer cult gave him ultimate authority.
  • "The Nazi regime rested on popular support": Economic recovery, propaganda, nationalism. Counter: terror essential; opposition crushed; denunciations show fear, not consent.
  • "Hitler planned war from the start": Mein Kampf, rearmament, eastward expansion. Counter: improvisation, responses to opportunities, economic pressures.

📜 Worked Example

Question: To what extent was Germany responsible for WWI?

Essay Plan:
FOR: Blank cheque, Schlieffen Plan, naval expansion, Fischer thesis
AGAINST: Austrian ultimatum, Russian mobilisation, alliance system, Clark "sleepwalkers"
Conclusion: Significant responsibility but not sole blame
Writing narrative instead of analysis. History essays need CAUSATION — explain WHY and SIGNIFICANCE.
Always use SPECIFIC EVIDENCE — dates, names, statistics. "On 28 June 1914" not "in 1914".

⚡ Quick Recall

  • Long-term: Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism
  • Short-term: Assassination of Franz Ferdinand
  • Treaty of Versailles: War guilt, reparations, territorial losses
  • Cold War: Containment, Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan

Source Analysis Framework

  • Content: What does the source say? What information does it provide?
  • Provenance: Who wrote it? When? Why? What is their perspective?
  • Reliability: Is it trustworthy? What biases might exist?
  • Utility: How useful is it for a specific enquiry?

Essay Structure

  • Introduction: Define key terms, set out argument/line of reasoning
  • Paragraphs: Point → Evidence → Explanation → Link back to question
  • Synthesis: Compare factors, weigh importance, show interconnections
  • Conclusion: Judgment based on evidence, avoid introducing new points
Always answer the EXACT question asked. Underline key words ("To what extent", "How far", "Most important") and address them directly.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Writing narrative instead of analysis — history essays need causation and significance, not just chronology.
  • Forgetting to link back to the question in each paragraph — every point should support your argument.
  • Using modern perspectives to judge historical actions — consider the context of the time.
  • Forgetting to evaluate sources for reliability and usefulness in Paper 2 questions.
  • Using vague generalisations instead of specific evidence — always include dates, names, and statistics.

✅ Key Points to Remember

  • Be able to compare and contrast different historical interpretations of the same event.
  • Practise writing timed essays — time management is crucial in the examination.
  • Learn to evaluate sources using NOP (Nature, Origin, Purpose) and content analysis.
  • Always plan your essay before writing — a clear structure improves coherence and argumentation.
  • Understand the difference between causes (why it happened) and consequences (what resulted).

Historical Analysis: h8

History is not about memorising dates and events — it is about understanding causation, change, continuity, and significance. Examiners reward analysis over narrative.

Essay Structure
  1. Introduction: Define key terms and state your line of argument.
  2. Paragraphs: Each should make one point, supported by specific evidence.
  3. Analysis: Explain why events happened and why they matter.
  4. Conclusion: Directly answer the question with a clear judgement.
Evidence and Analysis

Poor: "The Treaty of Versailles was signed in 1919 and Germany had to pay reparations."

Better: "The Treaty of Versailles imposed reparations of £6.6 billion on Germany because the war guilt clause (Article 231) held Germany responsible for WWI. This meant that the German economy was crippled by debt, leading to hyperinflation in 1923 and resentment that fuelled Nazi support."

Specific evidence earns marks. "On 28 June 1914, Franz Ferdinand was assassinated in Sarajevo" is far better than "in 1914 something happened". Always use names, dates, and statistics.

Worked Example 1: Analysing a Source

Problem

Analyse the usefulness of a government propaganda poster from 1916 encouraging men to enlist in the army.

Solution

Content: The poster shows a smiling soldier with the caption "Your Country Needs You." It portrays military service as honourable and patriotic.

Provenance: Created by the British government in 1916, after conscription was introduced. The purpose was to encourage voluntary enlistment and maintain public support for the war.

Usefulness: Useful for showing how governments used emotional appeals to recruit soldiers. However, limited by bias — it does not show the realities of trench warfare. It should be compared with letters from soldiers or casualty figures for a balanced view.

Worked Example 2: Causation Essay Plan

Problem

To what extent was the Treaty of Versailles responsible for the outbreak of World War II?

Essay Plan

Introduction: Define the Treaty of Versailles (1919) and state that while it contributed to WWII, it was not the sole cause.

FOR (Treaty was responsible):

  • War guilt clause (Article 231) humiliated Germany
  • Reparations crippled the German economy (hyperinflation 1923)
  • Territorial losses (13% of land, colonies, Alsace-Lorraine) created resentment
  • Demilitarisation was seen as unfair and weak

AGAINST (Other factors):

  • Failure of the League of Nations to stop aggression (Manchuria 1931, Abyssinia 1935)
  • Appeasement policy of Britain and France (Munich Agreement 1938)
  • Great Depression created economic instability worldwide
  • Hitler's expansionist ambitions and rearmament

Conclusion: The Treaty created conditions for resentment but did not make war inevitable. The failures of the League, appeasement, and Hitler's actions were equally or more important.

Worked Example 3: Comparing Interpretations

Problem

Historian A argues that the Cold War was caused by Soviet aggression. Historian B argues it was caused by American policy. Evaluate these interpretations.

Solution

Historian A (Soviet aggression): Supported by Soviet expansion into Eastern Europe (1945–48), creation of puppet states, Berlin Blockade (1948), and invasion of Hungary (1956).

Historian B (American policy): Supported by Truman Doctrine (1947), Marshall Plan (1948), NATO (1949), and US support for anti-communist regimes worldwide.

Evaluation: Both interpretations have merit. Soviet actions in Eastern Europe were aggressive, but US policies like containment were also confrontational. The orthodox view (Soviet blame) dominated until the 1960s; the revisionist view (US blame) emerged with the Vietnam War. Post-revisionist historians (like Gaddis) argue both sides share responsibility.

Practice Questions

Question 1

Analyse the reliability of a diary entry from a soldier on the Western Front in 1916.

Answer: Consider proximity to events (likely reliable on details), personal perspective (may be emotional or biased), purpose (private vs published), compare with official records or other soldiers' accounts.

Question 2

Explain why the League of Nations failed to stop Japanese aggression in Manchuria (1931).

Answer: Economic sanctions not applied (USA not member, major trading partner); no military force available; Britain and France unwilling to act (focused on domestic issues); Japan simply left the League; slow decision-making process.

Question 3

"The Cuban Missile Crisis was a victory for the USA." How far do you agree?

Answer: FOR: missiles removed, Khrushchev backed down, USA appeared strong. AGAINST: USA had to remove missiles from Turkey secretly, crisis showed dangers of brinkmanship, hotline established afterwards. CONCLUSION: Short-term victory for USA but long-term recognition of need for détente.

🎯 Examination Tips

  • Structure essays with an introduction, developed paragraphs, and a clear conclusion.
  • Check the number of marks — higher marks require greater depth, analysis, and evaluation.
  • Each paragraph should make one main point, supported by evidence and explanation.
  • Leave time to review — check that you have answered the question asked, not the one you expected.
  • In essay questions, use specific evidence — "On 28 June 1914" not "in 1914".
