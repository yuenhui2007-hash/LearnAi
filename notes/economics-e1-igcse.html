IGCSE

📘 Subject Context

Understanding Basic Economic Ideas is central to IGCSE Economics. This topic links microeconomic and macroeconomic concepts and is frequently tested in data response and essay-style questions.

IGCSE Economics requires both knowledge and application. Paper 1 (Multiple Choice) tests definitions and concepts; Paper 2 (Structured Questions) tests analysis and evaluation. Always use real-world examples where possible.

Basic Economic Ideas

0455 (IGCSE)

Detailed Notes

1. Scarcity, Choice, Opportunity Cost

Scarcity = limited resources vs unlimited wants. Choice = allocation decisions. Opportunity cost = next best alternative foregone.

2. Production Possibility Curve (PPC)

Shows maximum output combinations. Points on curve = efficient. Inside = inefficient. Outside = unattainable. Shift outward = economic growth.

3. Economic Systems

  • Free market: Private ownership, price mechanism, profit motive.
  • Planned: Government ownership, central planning.
  • Mixed: Both private and public sectors.

4. Positive vs Normative

Positive = objective, fact-based ("what is"). Normative = subjective, value-based ("what ought to be").

Real-World Applications

  • Price controls: Maximum prices (rent controls) and minimum prices (minimum wage)
  • Taxes: Direct (income tax) vs indirect (VAT, excise duties)
  • Subsidies: Producer subsidies (shift supply right) vs consumer subsidies
  • Externalities: Positive (education, vaccination) vs negative (pollution, congestion)

Market Failure & Government Intervention

  • Public goods: Non-excludable and non-rival (defence, street lighting)
  • Merit goods: Under-consumed due to information failure (education, healthcare)
  • Demerit goods: Over-consumed due to information failure (cigarettes, alcohol)
  • Government responses: Legislation, taxation, subsidies, provision, regulation

5. Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

  • Formula: PED = (% change in quantity demanded) / (% change in price)
  • Elastic (PED > 1): Quantity changes more than price — luxuries, many substitutes
  • Inelastic (PED < 1): Quantity changes less than price — necessities, few substitutes
  • Unit elastic (PED = 1): % change in quantity = % change in price
  • Perfectly inelastic (PED = 0): Quantity doesn't change at all — essential medicines
  • Factors affecting PED: Number of substitutes, necessity vs luxury, proportion of income, time period

📈 Worked Example

Question: When price rises from $4 to $5, quantity demanded falls from 200 to 160 units. Calculate PED.

%ΔQ = (160−200)/200 × 100 = −20%
%ΔP = (5−4)/4 × 100 = +25%
PED = |−20/25| = 0.8 (inelastic)

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing balance of trade deficit with a government budget deficit — they are different concepts.
  • Mixing up direct and indirect taxes — direct taxes are on income/profit; indirect taxes are on spending.
  • Forgetting that economic growth does not necessarily mean development — development includes social factors.
  • Forgetting that price elasticity of demand is always negative — economists often ignore the sign.
  • Using the term "inflation" when you mean "a rise in the price of one good" — inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level.

✅ Key Points to Remember

  • Be able to evaluate government policies using criteria like effectiveness, time lags, and unintended consequences.
  • Know the factors that shift supply and demand curves — memorise these as lists.
  • Always draw supply and demand diagrams accurately — label axes, curves, equilibrium, and shifts.
  • Be able to explain the functions of money and the role of central banks.
  • Learn the advantages and disadvantages of free trade and protectionism.

🎯 Examination Tips

  • Use connective phrases like "however", "on the other hand", "therefore" to develop arguments.
  • Use real-world examples — e.g., "as seen in the UK in 2022 when inflation reached 11%".
  • Define key terms in your answer — this demonstrates knowledge and can earn definition marks.
  • Check the command word — "discuss" requires both sides; "evaluate" requires a judgement.
  • For evaluation questions, consider both sides before reaching a reasoned judgement.
