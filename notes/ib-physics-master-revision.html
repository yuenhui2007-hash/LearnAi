IBP1: Measurements

Overview

Measurements and Uncertainties (Topic 1) is the foundation of IB Physics and appears in every examination paper. It introduces the SI system, error analysis, and the mathematical tools needed throughout the course. This topic is assessed across Paper 1 (MCQ), Paper 2 (structured), and Paper 3 (data analysis), and is essential for the Internal Assessment (IA). Mastery of uncertainty propagation is particularly valued in IA criterion D (analysis).

1.1 Fundamental SI Units

All measurements in physics are expressed in terms of the seven SI base units. You must know these for Paper 1 and Paper 2.

  • Length — metre (m)
  • Mass — kilogram (kg)
  • Time — second (s)
  • Electric current — ampere (A)
  • Temperature — kelvin (K)
  • Amount of substance — mole (mol)
  • Luminous intensity — candela (cd)

Command Terms

State — give a specific name, value or other brief answer without explanation or calculation.
Outline — give a brief account or summary.

Derived Units

Derived units are combinations of base units. Common derived units you must recall:

Force (N) = kg m s⁻²
Energy (J) = kg m² s⁻²
Power (W) = kg m² s⁻³
Pressure (Pa) = kg m⁻¹ s⁻²
Frequency (Hz) = s⁻¹
Electric charge (C) = A s

Metric Prefixes

You must convert between prefixes quickly. Common prefixes tested:

nano (n) = 10⁻⁹    micro (μ) = 10⁻⁶    milli (m) = 10⁻³
centi (c) = 10⁻²    kilo (k) = 10³    mega (M) = 10⁶    giga (G) = 10⁹

Worked Example 1: Prefix Conversion

Question: Convert 250 nm to metres.

Solution:

250 nm = 250 × 10⁻⁹ m = 2.50 × 10⁻⁷ m

The answer must be given to 3 significant figures to match the precision of the original value.

1.2 Uncertainties & Errors

Understanding the difference between random errors and systematic errors is critical for the IA and all papers.

  • Random errors — scatter readings about the true value; reduced by repeating measurements and averaging. These affect precision.
  • Systematic errors — shift all readings by the same amount in one direction; caused by faulty equipment or poor technique. These affect accuracy.

Absolute and Relative Uncertainty

Absolute uncertainty (Δx) — uncertainty in the same units as the measurement
Fractional uncertainty = Δx / x
Percentage uncertainty = (Δx / x) × 100%

Worked Example 2: Uncertainty Calculation

Question: A length is measured as (25.0 ± 0.5) cm. Calculate the percentage uncertainty.

Solution:

Percentage uncertainty = (0.5 / 25.0) × 100% = 2%

Uncertainty in Readings vs. Measurements

A reading uncertainty is typically ± half the smallest division on an analogue scale, or ±1 in the last significant digit on a digital display. A measurement uncertainty includes both the reading uncertainty and any calibration uncertainty of the instrument.

1.3 Significant Figures

Significant figures reflect the precision of a measurement. IB examiners penalise answers given to the wrong number of significant figures.

  • Non-zero digits are always significant.
  • Zeros between non-zero digits are significant (e.g., 2005 has 4 sf).
  • Leading zeros are not significant (e.g., 0.0045 has 2 sf).
  • Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant (e.g., 4.500 has 4 sf).

Rule for Calculations

For multiplication/division: the result has the same number of significant figures as the least precise value.
For addition/subtraction: the result has the same number of decimal places as the value with the fewest decimal places.

1.4 Error Propagation

When combining measurements, uncertainties propagate according to strict rules. This is tested in Paper 2 and essential for IA analysis.

Addition and Subtraction

If z = x + y or z = x − y, then:
Δz = Δx + Δy (absolute uncertainties add)

Multiplication and Division

If z = xy or z = x/y, then:
Δz/z = Δx/x + Δy/y (fractional/percentage uncertainties add)

Powers

If z = xⁿ, then:
Δz/z = |n| × (Δx/x)

Worked Example 3: Propagation in Density

Question: A block has mass m = (50.0 ± 0.5) g and volume V = (20.0 ± 0.4) cm³. Calculate the density and its absolute uncertainty.

Solution:

Density ρ = m/V = 50.0/20.0 = 2.50 g cm⁻³

Percentage uncertainty in m = (0.5/50.0) × 100% = 1.0%
Percentage uncertainty in V = (0.4/20.0) × 100% = 2.0%

Total percentage uncertainty in ρ = 1.0% + 2.0% = 3.0%
Absolute uncertainty in ρ = 3.0% of 2.50 = 0.075 g cm⁻³

Final answer: ρ = (2.50 ± 0.08) g cm⁻³ (to 2 sf for uncertainty, 3 sf for value)

1.5 Graphical Analysis

IB Physics requires you to plot graphs with error bars and determine uncertainties in gradients and intercepts. This appears in Paper 3 (data analysis) and is central to the IA.

  • Draw a line of best fit that passes through as many error bars as possible.
  • Draw lines of worst fit (steepest and shallowest reasonable lines) to find gradient uncertainty.
  • Gradient uncertainty = ½ × (gradient of steepest line − gradient of shallowest line).

IA Connection

In your IA, you must include uncertainty bars on your graphs and discuss how uncertainties affect your conclusion. Criterion D (Analysis) assesses your ability to process uncertainties correctly.

Common Misconceptions

  • Random vs. systematic: Students often confuse them. Remember: random = scatter (precision), systematic = offset (accuracy).
  • Percentage uncertainty: Do not forget to multiply by 100%. A fractional uncertainty of 0.05 is 5%, not 0.05%.
  • Significant figures in intermediate steps: Never round intermediate values — only round the final answer.
  • Error bars: Error bars represent uncertainties, not ranges of data points.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: Typically 2–4 MCQs on unit conversions, prefix recognition, and significant figures.
  • Paper 2: Structured questions on uncertainty propagation (especially with density, speed, or acceleration calculations).
  • Paper 3: Data analysis question requiring graph plotting with error bars and gradient uncertainty.
  • IA: Uncertainty analysis is assessed in Criterion D.

Past Paper Trends (2019–2024)

November 2023: Propagation of uncertainties in kinematics calculations.
May 2023: Graphical analysis with error bars and gradient calculation.
November 2022: Significant figures in multi-step calculations.
May 2022: Distinguishing random and systematic errors from experimental data.

Practice Questions

  • Calculate the percentage uncertainty in g = 9.81 m s⁻² if measured as (9.81 ± 0.05) m s⁻².
  • A rectangle has sides (5.0 ± 0.1) cm and (3.0 ± 0.1) cm. Find the area and its absolute uncertainty.
  • Explain whether zero error on a micrometer screw gauge is random or systematic.
  • Convert 450 MW to W, giving your answer in standard form to 2 significant figures.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Know all 7 SI base units and common derived units by heart.
  • Random error → precision; systematic error → accuracy.
  • For multiplication/division: add percentage uncertainties.
  • For addition/subtraction: add absolute uncertainties.
  • Always give uncertainties to 1 or 2 significant figures, and match the value's precision.
  • Include error bars and gradient uncertainty analysis in IA graphs.

  • In "explain" questions, link your answer back to the physics principle (e.g., explain why percentage uncertainty decreases with larger measurements).
  • Use a ruler for graph questions — examiners check for properly drawn best-fit lines.

IBP2: Mechanics

Overview

Mechanics (Topic 2) is the largest and most heavily weighted topic in IB Physics, forming the backbone of kinematics, dynamics, and energy. It appears across all three exam papers and is fundamental to the IA. Expect a mix of calculation-based and conceptual questions testing your understanding of motion, forces, momentum, and energy conservation.

2.1 Kinematics

Kinematics describes the motion of objects without reference to the forces causing the motion.

Key Definitions

  • Displacement (s) — distance moved in a stated direction (vector, m).
  • Velocity (v) — rate of change of displacement (vector, m s⁻¹).
  • Speed — rate of change of distance (scalar, m s⁻¹).
  • Acceleration (a) — rate of change of velocity (vector, m s⁻²).

Equations of Motion (SUVAT)

For motion with constant acceleration:

v = u + at
s = ut + ½at²
v² = u² + 2as
s = ½(u + v)t

where u = initial velocity, v = final velocity, a = acceleration, t = time, s = displacement.

Worked Example 1: SUVAT

Question: A car accelerates uniformly from rest at 2.0 m s⁻² for 5.0 s. Calculate the distance travelled.

Solution:

u = 0, a = 2.0 m s⁻², t = 5.0 s
s = ut + ½at² = 0 + ½(2.0)(5.0)² = 25 m

Graphs of Motion

  • Displacement–time: gradient = velocity.
  • Velocity–time: gradient = acceleration; area under graph = displacement.
  • Acceleration–time: area under graph = change in velocity.

Common Trap

Do not confuse displacement–time and velocity–time graphs. A horizontal line on a displacement–time graph means the object is stationary; a horizontal line on a velocity–time graph means constant velocity.

Projectile Motion

For projectile motion, resolve into horizontal and vertical components:

Horizontal: aₓ = 0, so vₓ = u cos θ (constant)
Vertical: aᵧ = −g, so use SUVAT with uᵧ = u sin θ

The trajectory is parabolic (neglecting air resistance). Time of flight depends only on vertical motion.

2.2 Forces & Newton's Laws

Newton's First Law (Law of Inertia)

An object remains at rest or moves with constant velocity unless acted upon by a resultant (net) force.

Newton's Second Law

F = ma
Resultant force (N) = mass (kg) × acceleration (m s⁻²)

Newton's Third Law

If body A exerts a force on body B, then body B exerts an equal and opposite force on body A. These forces act on different bodies.

Common Misconception

Action–reaction pairs do not cancel out because they act on different objects. The weight of a book and the normal force from the table are NOT an action–reaction pair (they act on the same object).

Types of Force

  • Weight (W) = mg, where g ≈ 9.81 m s⁻² (or 9.8/10 as specified).
  • Normal reaction (N) — perpendicular contact force from a surface.
  • Tension (T) — force in a string or rope.
  • Friction — opposes relative motion; static friction ≤ μₛN, kinetic friction = μₖN.
  • Drag — resistive force in fluids; increases with speed.

2.3 Work, Energy & Power

Work

W = Fs cos θ
Work (J) = force (N) × displacement (m) × cos(angle between F and s)

Kinetic Energy

Eₖ = ½mv²

Gravitational Potential Energy

Eₚ = mgh
(change in GPE = mgΔh, measured relative to a chosen zero level)

Principle of Conservation of Energy

For an isolated system, total energy remains constant. In mechanics problems:

Eₖ(initial) + Eₚ(initial) = Eₖ(final) + Eₚ(final) + work done against friction

Power

P = W/t = Fv (when force and velocity are in the same direction)

Worked Example 2: Energy Conservation

Question: A 2.0 kg object slides from rest down a frictionless slope of height 3.0 m. Find its speed at the bottom.

Solution:

mgΔh = ½mv²
v = √(2gΔh) = √(2 × 9.81 × 3.0) = 7.67 m s⁻¹ ≈ 7.7 m s⁻¹

2.4 Momentum & Impulse

Linear Momentum

p = mv (kg m s⁻¹)

Impulse

Impulse = FΔt = Δp = mv − mu

Conservation of Momentum

For a closed system with no external forces:

Total momentum before = Total momentum after
m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

Elastic and Inelastic Collisions

  • Elastic: kinetic energy is conserved. Relative speed of approach = relative speed of separation.
  • Inelastic: kinetic energy is not conserved (some converted to heat/sound). Objects may stick together.

Worked Example 3: Collision

Question: A 3.0 kg ball moving at 4.0 m s⁻¹ collides with a stationary 2.0 kg ball. After the collision, the 3.0 kg ball moves at 1.0 m s⁻¹. Find the velocity of the 2.0 kg ball.

Solution:

(3.0)(4.0) + (2.0)(0) = (3.0)(1.0) + (2.0)(v)
12 = 3 + 2v
v = 4.5 m s⁻¹ in the original direction

2.5 Circular Motion [HL only]

HL students must also understand uniform circular motion.

Centripetal Acceleration and Force

a = v²/r = ω²r
F = mv²/r = mω²r
ω = 2πf = 2π/T

Centripetal force is not a new force — it is provided by tension, friction, gravity, or normal reaction depending on the situation.

HL Exam Focus

May 2024 HL Paper 2: Conical pendulum problem requiring resolution of tension into horizontal and vertical components to find angular velocity.

Common Misconceptions

  • Newton's Third Law: Action–reaction pairs act on different bodies. Do not use them to balance forces on a single free-body diagram.
  • Energy conservation: GPE = mgh uses change in height, not absolute height. Always define a zero level.
  • Momentum: Momentum is a vector. In 2D collisions, resolve into components before applying conservation.
  • Circular motion: Centripetal force does not do work (it is perpendicular to velocity), so speed is constant in uniform circular motion.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: 4–6 MCQs on kinematics graphs, forces, energy, and momentum.
  • Paper 2: Structured questions combining SUVAT, forces, energy conservation, and momentum in multi-step problems.
  • Paper 3: Data analysis involving motion sensors and energy calculations.
  • IA: Many IAs use mechanics experiments (e.g., projectile motion, conservation of momentum, inclined planes).

Past Paper Trends (2019–2024)

November 2023: Momentum conservation in 2D collision with vector resolution.
May 2023: Work-energy theorem with friction on an inclined plane.
November 2022: Projectile motion with horizontal launch from a height.
May 2022: Newton's Second Law applied to connected particles over a pulley.

  • A stone is thrown vertically upwards at 15 m s⁻¹. Calculate the maximum height reached (take g = 9.81 m s⁻²).
  • A 5.0 kg box is pulled along a rough horizontal surface by a 20 N force at 30° to the horizontal. The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.15. Calculate the acceleration.

Last-Minute Summary

  • SUVAT equations apply only for constant acceleration.
  • Always draw free-body diagrams — they earn method marks.

IBP3: Thermal

Overview

Thermal Physics (Topic 3) bridges macroscopic thermodynamics and microscopic kinetic theory. It is examined across all papers and connects to energy production (Topic 8). Understanding the first law of thermodynamics and the behaviour of ideal gases is essential for both SL and HL students.

3.1 Temperature & Internal Energy

Temperature is a measure of the average random kinetic energy of the particles in a substance. It is measured in kelvin (K).

T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15

Internal energy (U) is the total potential and random kinetic energy of the molecules of a substance. For an ideal gas, internal energy depends only on temperature.

Key Distinction

Temperature is a macroscopic property related to average kinetic energy. Internal energy includes both kinetic and potential energy of all particles.

3.2 Specific Heat Capacity & Latent Heat

Specific heat capacity (c) is the energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K.

Q = mcΔT
Q = energy (J), m = mass (kg), c = specific heat capacity (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹), ΔT = temperature change (K)

Specific latent heat (L) is the energy required to change the phase of 1 kg of a substance without changing its temperature.

Q = mL
L = specific latent heat (J kg⁻¹)

Worked Example 1: Heating and Phase Change

Question: Calculate the energy needed to convert 0.50 kg of ice at −10°C to water at 20°C. (c_ice = 2.1 × 10³ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, c_water = 4.2 × 10³ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, L_fusion = 3.3 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹)

Solution:

Heating ice: Q₁ = (0.50)(2.1 × 10³)(10) = 1.05 × 10⁴ J
Melting: Q₂ = (0.50)(3.3 × 10⁵) = 1.65 × 10⁵ J
Heating water: Q₃ = (0.50)(4.2 × 10³)(20) = 4.2 × 10⁴ J
Total Q = 2.18 × 10⁵ J ≈ 2.2 × 10⁵ J

3.3 Kinetic Theory of Gases

The kinetic model assumes gas molecules are point masses in random motion, with elastic collisions and negligible intermolecular forces except during collisions.

Pressure and Molecular Speed

p = ⅓ρ〈c²〉 = ⅓(Nm/V)〈c²〉
where 〈c²〉 = mean-square speed, ρ = density, N = number of molecules, m = molecular mass

Kinetic Energy and Temperature

½m〈c²〉 = (3/2)kT
where k = Boltzmann constant = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹

The average translational kinetic energy of a molecule is directly proportional to absolute temperature.

Root-Mean-Square Speed

c_rms = √(3kT/m) = √(3RT/M)
where R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, M = molar mass (kg mol⁻¹)

3.4 Laws of Thermodynamics

First Law of Thermodynamics

ΔU = Q − W
ΔU = change in internal energy, Q = heat added to system, W = work done by system

Sign convention: Q > 0 when heat enters the system; W > 0 when the system does work on surroundings.

Second Law of Thermodynamics

Heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder body to a hotter body. The total entropy of an isolated system never decreases.

ΔS ≥ Q/T (for reversible processes, ΔS = Q/T)

Processes

  • Isothermal: ΔT = 0, so ΔU = 0, therefore Q = W.
  • Adiabatic: Q = 0, so ΔU = −W.
  • Isochoric: W = 0, so ΔU = Q.
  • Isobaric: p = constant, W = pΔV.

3.5 Ideal Gas Equation

pV = nRT = NkT
p = pressure (Pa), V = volume (m³), n = number of moles, N = number of molecules
R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹

Worked Example 2: Ideal Gas

Question: 2.0 mol of an ideal gas occupies 5.0 × 10⁻² m³ at 300 K. Calculate the pressure.

Solution:

p = nRT/V = (2.0)(8.31)(300)/(5.0 × 10⁻²) = 9.97 × 10⁴ Pa ≈ 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa

Common Misconceptions

  • Heat vs. temperature: Heat is energy transfer; temperature is a measure of average kinetic energy.
  • Internal energy of ideal gas: Depends only on temperature, not volume or pressure.
  • Latent heat: Occurs at constant temperature — students often incorrectly add a temperature change.
  • First law signs: Some textbooks use ΔU = Q + W (work done on system). IB uses ΔU = Q − W (work done by system).

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: 2–3 MCQs on temperature conversion, specific heat, and gas laws.
  • Paper 2: Structured questions on calorimetry, first law applications, and ideal gas calculations.
  • Paper 3: Data analysis of gas law experiments or calorimetry.
  • IA: Calorimetry and gas law experiments are popular IA topics.

May 2023: Calorimetry calculation with heat loss to surroundings.
November 2022: Kinetic theory derivation linking pressure and molecular speed.
May 2022: Specific latent heat experiment with uncertainty analysis.

IBP4: Waves

Overview

Waves (Topic 4) covers mechanical and electromagnetic wave phenomena, including standing waves, diffraction, interference, and the Doppler effect. This topic requires strong diagram-drawing skills and appears across all three exam papers. Wave–particle duality connections are developed further in Topic 7 (Atomic Physics).

4.1 Wave Characteristics

Definitions

  • Displacement (y) — distance of a particle from its equilibrium position.
  • Amplitude (A) — maximum displacement.
  • Wavelength (λ) — distance between consecutive identical points (e.g., crest to crest).
  • Frequency (f) — number of oscillations per second (Hz).
  • Period (T) — time for one complete oscillation (s).
  • Wave speed (v) — speed at which the wave profile moves.
v = fλ = λ/T

Transverse vs. Longitudinal

  • Transverse: particle oscillation is perpendicular to wave direction (e.g., light, waves on a string).
  • Longitudinal: particle oscillation is parallel to wave direction (e.g., sound).

Longitudinal waves have compressions (high pressure/density) and rarefactions (low pressure/density).

4.2 Wave Equation & Graphs

The wave equation in its general form:

y = A sin(ωt − kx) or y = A sin(2πft − 2πx/λ)
where ω = 2πf = angular frequency, k = 2π/λ = wave number

Worked Example 1: Wave Parameters

Question: A wave has amplitude 0.05 m, frequency 2.0 Hz, and speed 4.0 m s⁻¹. Write the wave equation and find the wavelength.

Solution:

λ = v/f = 4.0/2.0 = 2.0 m
y = 0.05 sin(4πt − πx)

4.3 Standing Waves

Standing waves form from the superposition of two waves of equal amplitude and frequency travelling in opposite directions.

  • Nodes (N): points of zero amplitude (destructive interference).
  • Antinodes (A): points of maximum amplitude (constructive interference).

String Fixed at Both Ends

fₙ = nv/2L (n = 1, 2, 3, ...)
Fundamental (n=1): λ = 2L
First harmonic (n=2): λ = L

Pipe Open at One End

fₙ = nv/4L (n = 1, 3, 5, ...)
Fundamental: λ = 4L

Pipe Open at Both Ends

fₙ = nv/2L (n = 1, 2, 3, ...)

Common Trap

For a pipe closed at one end, only odd harmonics are present. Students often incorrectly include the second harmonic.

4.4 Diffraction

Diffraction is the spreading of waves as they pass through an aperture or around an obstacle.

  • Significant diffraction occurs when λ ≈ aperture/obstacle size.
  • Diffraction explains why sound bends around corners but light appears to travel in straight lines (λ_sound ≈ 1 m, λ_light ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁷ m).

4.5 Interference

Path Difference

Constructive: path difference = nλ (n = 0, 1, 2, ...)
Destructive: path difference = (n + ½)λ

Young's Double-Slit Experiment

s = λD/d
s = fringe spacing, D = slit-to-screen distance, d = slit separation

Worked Example 2: Double Slit

Question: Light of wavelength 600 nm passes through slits separated by 0.20 mm. The screen is 1.5 m away. Calculate the fringe spacing.

Solution:

s = (600 × 10⁻⁹)(1.5)/(0.20 × 10⁻³) = 4.5 × 10⁻³ m = 4.5 mm

4.6 Resolution

The Rayleigh criterion states that two point sources are just resolvable when the central maximum of one diffraction pattern coincides with the first minimum of the other.

θ ≈ 1.22λ/b (circular aperture)
θ ≈ λ/b (single slit)

where θ is the minimum angular separation (in radians), λ is wavelength, and b is aperture diameter.

4.7 Doppler Effect

The apparent change in frequency of a wave due to relative motion between source and observer.

f' = f(v ± v₀)/(v ∓ vₛ)
Top sign (numerator): observer moving towards source
Bottom sign (denominator): source moving towards observer

For light, the Doppler shift leads to redshift (receding) and blueshift (approaching):

Δλ/λ ≈ v/c (for v << c)

Common Misconceptions

  • Standing waves: Energy is not transmitted in a standing wave; it is stored in the medium.
  • Diffraction vs. refraction: Diffraction is spreading around obstacles; refraction is bending at a boundary due to speed change.
  • Doppler effect: The source frequency does not change — only the observed frequency changes.
  • Resolution: Larger apertures give better resolution (smaller θ), which is why large telescopes are built.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: 3–4 MCQs on wave speed, standing wave harmonics, and Doppler effect.
  • Paper 2: Structured questions on double-slit calculations, standing wave patterns, and resolution.
  • Paper 3: Data analysis of sound wave experiments or diffraction patterns.
  • IA: Standing waves on strings and diffraction experiments are common IA topics.

  • Pipe closed at one end: only odd harmonics, fₙ = nv/4L.
  • Young's slit: s = λD/d.
  • Doppler: observed frequency changes due to relative motion; source frequency is constant.

IBP5: Electricity

Overview

Electricity and Magnetism (Topic 5) is one of the most calculation-heavy topics in IB Physics, covering electric fields, circuits, magnetic fields, and electromagnetic induction. It appears across all papers and is frequently tested in combination with mechanics and energy topics.

5.1 Electric Fields

Coulomb's Law

F = kq₁q₂/r² = q₁q₂/(4πε₀r²)
k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻², ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C² N⁻¹ m⁻²

Electric Field Strength

E = F/q (definition)
E = kQ/r² = Q/(4πε₀r²) (point charge)
E = V/d (uniform field between parallel plates)

Electric Potential

V = kQ/r = Q/(4πε₀r)
Equipotential surfaces are perpendicular to electric field lines.

Worked Example 1: Electric Field

Question: Calculate the electric field strength 0.10 m from a point charge of +2.0 μC.

Solution:

E = (8.99 × 10⁹)(2.0 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.10)² = 1.80 × 10⁶ N C⁻¹ (radially outward)

5.2 Electric Circuits

Ohm's Law

V = IR
R = resistance (Ω), I = current (A), V = potential difference (V)

Resistance

R = ρL/A
ρ = resistivity (Ω m), L = length (m), A = cross-sectional area (m²)

Resistors in Series and Parallel

Series: R_total = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...
Parallel: 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + ...

Power and Energy in Circuits

P = VI = I²R = V²/R
Energy = Power × time = VIt

Kirchhoff's Laws

  • First Law (Junction Rule): Sum of currents entering a junction = sum leaving.
  • Second Law (Loop Rule): Sum of emfs = sum of potential differences around any closed loop.

Worked Example 2: Circuit Analysis

Question: A 12 V battery with negligible internal resistance is connected to a 4.0 Ω and 8.0 Ω resistor in series. Calculate the current and the power dissipated in the 4.0 Ω resistor.

Solution:

R_total = 4.0 + 8.0 = 12 Ω
I = V/R = 12/12 = 1.0 A
P = I²R = (1.0)²(4.0) = 4.0 W

5.3 Magnetic Fields

Magnetic Field Definitions

  • Magnetic field direction: direction of force on a north pole.
  • Magnetic field lines: go from N to S externally; form continuous closed loops.

Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

F = BIL sin θ
B = magnetic flux density (T), I = current (A), L = length (m), θ = angle between B and I

Force on a Moving Charge

F = Bqv sin θ
Direction given by Fleming's left-hand rule (conventional current).

Magnetic Field Around a Long Straight Wire

B = μ₀I/(2πr)
μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T m A⁻¹

5.4 Electromagnetic Induction

Magnetic Flux

Φ = BA cos θ (Wb)
where B = flux density (T), A = area (m²), θ = angle between B and normal to A

Faraday's Law

ε = −N dΦ/dt
Induced emf is proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux linkage.

Lenz's Law

The direction of the induced current is such that it opposes the change producing it. This is represented by the negative sign in Faraday's law.

Worked Example 3: Induction

Question: A coil of 50 turns and area 2.0 × 10⁻³ m² is perpendicular to a magnetic field that changes from 0.20 T to 0.50 T in 0.10 s. Calculate the induced emf.

Solution:

ΔΦ = ΔB × A = (0.50 − 0.20)(2.0 × 10⁻³) = 6.0 × 10⁻⁴ Wb
ε = N ΔΦ/Δt = 50 × (6.0 × 10⁻⁴)/0.10 = 0.30 V

5.5 AC Circuits [HL only]

HL students study alternating current and its behaviour in resistive, capacitive, and inductive circuits.

Peak and rms values: V_rms = V₀/√2, I_rms = I₀/√2
Power in AC circuits: P = I_rms²R = V_rms²/R
Transformer equation: Vₛ/Vₚ = Nₛ/Nₚ

HL Exam Focus

May 2024 HL Paper 2: Transformer efficiency calculation with power losses.

Common Misconceptions

  • Electric field vs. potential: Field is force per unit charge; potential is energy per unit charge. Field is a vector; potential is a scalar.
  • Current in parallel: Current splits at junctions — the sum entering equals the sum leaving. Do not assume equal current in parallel branches with different resistances.
  • Lenz's law: The induced current opposes the change in flux, not the flux itself.
  • Magnetic force: No force acts on a charge moving parallel to a magnetic field (θ = 0).

IBP6: Circular Motion

Overview

Circular Motion and Gravitation (Topic 6) combines the study of rotational kinematics with Newton's law of universal gravitation. This topic is tested across all papers and connects strongly to orbital mechanics and astrophysics. HL students should be comfortable with vector resolution in circular motion problems.

6.1 Circular Motion

Angular Quantities

ω = 2πf = 2π/T (angular velocity, rad s⁻¹)
v = ωr (linear speed)
a = v²/r = ω²r (centripetal acceleration)

Centripetal Force

F = mv²/r = mω²r

Centripetal force is not a separate force — it is provided by tension, friction, gravity, or normal reaction depending on the context.

Worked Example 1: Horizontal Circular Motion

Question: A 0.50 kg mass on a 0.80 m string moves in a horizontal circle at 3.0 m s⁻¹. Calculate the tension in the string.

Solution:

T = mv²/r = (0.50)(3.0)²/0.80 = 5.6 N

Banked Curves

For a banked curve with no friction:

tan θ = v²/(rg)

where θ is the banking angle, v is speed, r is radius of curve.

6.2 Newton's Law of Gravitation

Universal Gravitation

F = Gm₁m₂/r²
G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻²

Gravitational Field Strength

g = F/m = GM/r²
At Earth's surface: g ≈ 9.81 N kg⁻¹

Gravitational Potential

V = −GM/r
Gravitational potential energy: Eₚ = −GMm/r

Potential is negative and increases towards zero as r → ∞. Work done to move a mass from one point to another = mΔV.

6.3 Orbital Motion

Orbital Speed

For a satellite in circular orbit, gravitational force provides the centripetal force:

GMm/r² = mv²/r
v = √(GM/r)

Orbital Period

T² = (4π²/GM) r³ (Kepler's Third Law)

Total Energy of an Orbiting Satellite

E_total = Eₖ + Eₚ = ½mv² − GMm/r = −GMm/(2r)
E_total = ½Eₚ = −Eₖ

Worked Example 2: Satellite Orbit

Question: Calculate the orbital speed of a satellite 400 km above Earth's surface. (M_Earth = 6.0 × 10²⁴ kg, R_Earth = 6.4 × 10⁶ m)

Solution:

r = 6.4 × 10⁶ + 0.4 × 10⁶ = 6.8 × 10⁶ m
v = √(GM/r) = √((6.67 × 10⁻¹¹)(6.0 × 10²⁴)/(6.8 × 10⁶)) = 7.7 × 10³ m s⁻¹

6.4 Kepler's Laws

  • First Law: Planets orbit the Sun in elliptical paths with the Sun at one focus.
  • Second Law: A line connecting a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times (conservation of angular momentum).
  • Third Law: T² ∝ r³ for all planets orbiting the same central mass.

6.5 Escape Speed [HL only]

v_escape = √(2GM/R)
For Earth: v_escape ≈ 11.2 km s⁻¹

Escape speed is independent of the mass of the escaping object.

HL Exam Focus

May 2024 HL Paper 2: Calculate escape speed from a planet given its mass and radius, then compare to orbital speed at the surface.

Common Misconceptions

  • Centripetal force: It is not a new force — always identify which real force provides it.
  • Gravitational potential: It is negative because potential is defined as zero at infinity.
  • Orbital speed: Satellites in higher orbits move slower (v ∝ 1/√r), contrary to intuition.
  • Weightlessness in orbit: Astronauts are not beyond Earth's gravity — they are in free fall, so the apparent weight is zero.

  • Paper 2: Structured questions on orbital mechanics, energy graphs, and Kepler's laws.
  • Paper 3: Data analysis of pendulum or circular motion experiments.
  • IA: Circular motion experiments (conical pendulum, rotating turntable) and gravitational investigations are common.

IBP7: Atomic Physics

Overview

Atomic, Nuclear and Particle Physics (Topic 7) explores the structure of matter from atoms to quarks. This topic is conceptually demanding and highly tested in Paper 1 (MCQ) and Paper 2 (structured). HL students encounter additional material on the Standard Model and exchange particles. Strong understanding of conservation laws is essential.

7.1 Atomic Structure

Rutherford Scattering

  • Alpha particles fired at thin gold foil.
  • Most passed through → atom is mostly empty space.
  • Some deflected at large angles → concentrated positive charge (nucleus).

Bohr Model

  • Electrons orbit in discrete energy levels.
  • Energy is emitted/absorbed as photons when electrons transition between levels.
E = hf = hc/λ
where h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s

Worked Example 1: Photon Energy

Question: Calculate the wavelength of a photon emitted when an electron transitions from −1.5 eV to −3.4 eV in a hydrogen atom.

Solution:

ΔE = (−1.5) − (−3.4) = 1.9 eV = 1.9 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.04 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
λ = hc/ΔE = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴)(3.0 × 10⁸)/(3.04 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 6.5 × 10⁻⁷ m = 650 nm

7.2 Radioactive Decay

Types of Radiation

  • Alpha (α): helium nucleus (²⁴He), +2 charge, high ionisation, low penetration.
  • Beta (β): electron (β⁻) or positron (β⁺), moderate ionisation and penetration.
  • Gamma (γ): electromagnetic radiation, no charge, low ionisation, high penetration.

Decay Equations

α decay: ᴬZX → ᴬ⁻⁴Z⁻₂Y + ⁴₂He
β⁻ decay: ᴬZX → ᴬZ₊₁Y + ⁰₋₁e + ν̄ₑ
β⁺ decay: ᴬZX → ᴬZ₋₁Y + ⁰₊₁e + νₑ

Half-Life

N = N₀(½)ⁿ where n = number of half-lives
Activity A = λN, where λ = decay constant
t½ = ln(2)/λ ≈ 0.693/λ

Worked Example 2: Half-Life

Question: A sample has an initial activity of 800 Bq and a half-life of 5.0 days. Calculate the activity after 20 days.

Solution:

n = 20/5 = 4 half-lives
A = 800 × (½)⁴ = 800/16 = 50 Bq

7.3 Nuclear Reactions

Mass Defect and Binding Energy

Mass defect Δm = (sum of nucleon masses) − (nuclear mass)
Binding energy E = Δmc²
Binding energy per nucleon indicates nuclear stability — peaks at iron-56 (⁵⁶Fe).

Fission and Fusion

  • Fission: heavy nucleus splits into smaller nuclei, releasing energy. Used in nuclear power reactors.
  • Fusion: light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy. Powers stars; requires extremely high temperatures.

7.4 Fundamental Particles [HL only]

Quarks

  • Up (u): charge +⅔e
  • Down (d): charge −⅓e
  • Charm (c): charge +⅔e
  • Strange (s): charge −⅓e
  • Top (t): charge +⅔e
  • Bottom (b): charge −⅓e

Hadrons

  • Baryons: three quarks (e.g., proton = uud, neutron = udd).
  • Mesons: quark–antiquark pair (e.g., π⁺ = uđ).

Leptons

  • Electron (e⁻), muon (μ⁻), tau (τ⁻) and their neutrinos (νₑ, ν_μ, ν_τ).
  • Lepton number is conserved in all interactions.

Exchange Particles

  • Photon (γ): electromagnetic force.
  • W⁺, W⁻, Z⁰: weak nuclear force.
  • Gluon (g): strong nuclear force.
  • Graviton (hypothetical): gravitational force.

Conservation Laws

In all particle interactions, the following are conserved:

  • Energy and momentum
  • Charge
  • Baryon number
  • Lepton number (separately for each generation)

HL Exam Focus

May 2024 HL Paper 2: Determine quark composition from conservation of charge and baryon number in a particle interaction.

Common Misconceptions

  • Half-life: It is the time for half the nuclei to decay, not half the activity (though for large samples these are equivalent).
  • Binding energy: It is the energy required to separate a nucleus into its nucleons, not the energy stored in the nucleus.
  • β decay: The electron emitted is created at the moment of decay, not pre-existing in the nucleus.
  • Quark confinement: Individual quarks cannot be isolated — they are always found in hadrons.

  • IA: Half-life experiments and background radiation measurements are popular.

IBP8: Energy Production

Overview

Energy Production (Topic 8) examines how humanity generates energy, the physics of power plants, and the environmental consequences of different energy sources. This topic is heavily conceptual and often includes data analysis questions in Paper 3. It connects strongly to thermal physics (Topic 3) and the IA.

8.1 Energy Sources

Primary vs. Secondary Energy

  • Primary: energy found in nature (fossil fuels, uranium, sunlight, wind).
  • Secondary: energy converted from primary sources (electricity, hydrogen).

Energy Density

Energy density = energy released / mass of fuel (J kg⁻¹)
Specific energy = energy released / mass of fuel (same as energy density for fuels)

Higher energy density fuels require less mass for the same energy output.

8.2 Fossil Fuels

  • Coal, oil, natural gas: formed from ancient organic matter.
  • Combustion releases CO₂ and other pollutants.
  • Advantages: high energy density, established infrastructure, reliable.
  • Disadvantages: finite resource, greenhouse gas emissions, air pollution.
Efficiency = (useful energy output / total energy input) × 100%

8.3 Nuclear Power

Nuclear Fission Reactors

  • Fuel: enriched uranium-235 or plutonium-239.
  • Moderator: slows neutrons to thermal speeds (water, graphite).
  • Control rods: absorb neutrons to regulate reaction rate.
  • Coolant: transfers heat from reactor core to steam generator.
  • Shielding: concrete and steel to contain radiation.

Worked Example 1: Reactor Power

Question: A reactor produces 1.0 GW of electrical power with an efficiency of 33%. Each fission releases 200 MeV. Calculate the number of fissions per second.

Solution:

Total thermal power = 1.0 GW / 0.33 = 3.03 GW = 3.03 × 10⁹ J s⁻¹
Energy per fission = 200 × 10⁶ × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.2 × 10⁻¹¹ J
Fissions per second = (3.03 × 10⁹)/(3.2 × 10⁻¹¹) = 9.5 × 10¹⁹ s⁻¹

Nuclear Safety and Waste

  • High-level waste remains radioactive for thousands of years.
  • Storage options: deep geological repositories, reprocessing.
  • Meltdown risk: loss of coolant leads to core overheating.

8.4 Solar Power

Photovoltaic Cells

  • Convert sunlight directly into electricity using the photoelectric effect.
  • Efficiency typically 15–22% for commercial panels.
Power output = solar intensity × area × efficiency
Solar constant ≈ 1.36 kW m⁻² (at Earth's distance from Sun)

Solar Thermal

  • Concentrates sunlight to heat a working fluid, which drives a turbine.
  • Advantages: no emissions, renewable.
  • Disadvantages: intermittent, requires large land area, weather-dependent.

Worked Example 2: Solar Panel

Question: A solar panel of area 2.0 m² receives solar radiation at 800 W m⁻². If the panel efficiency is 18%, calculate the electrical power output.

Solution:

P = (800)(2.0)(0.18) = 288 W ≈ 290 W

8.5 Wind Power

P = ½ρAv³
ρ = air density (≈ 1.2 kg m⁻³), A = swept area (m²), v = wind speed (m s⁻¹)
  • Power is proportional to v³ — small increases in wind speed give large power gains.
  • Maximum theoretical efficiency (Betz limit) = 59.3%.
  • Advantages: renewable, no emissions.
  • Disadvantages: intermittent, visual/noise impact, bird migration concerns.

8.6 Hydroelectric Power

P = ρgΔH × (flow rate)
or P = mgh/t for a given mass of water
  • Pumped storage: water is pumped uphill during low demand and released during peak demand.
  • Advantages: reliable, fast response, no emissions during operation.
  • Disadvantages: disrupts ecosystems, limited suitable sites, high capital cost.

8.7 Energy Transfers & Degradation

Energy is degraded when it is transferred to the surroundings as thermal energy at low temperature, becoming less useful for doing work. This is a consequence of the second law of thermodynamics.

Sankey diagrams represent energy flows — the width of arrows is proportional to the energy.

Common Misconceptions

  • Energy "loss": Energy is conserved; it is transformed into less useful forms (usually thermal).
  • Nuclear waste: Not all nuclear waste is high-level — most is low-level and can be safely stored.
  • Wind power: The Betz limit is a theoretical maximum; real turbines achieve 35–45%.
  • Solar panels: Efficiency is limited by the band gap of the semiconductor, not just engineering.

  • Paper 2: Structured questions comparing energy sources, reactor physics, and environmental impact.
  • Paper 3: Data analysis of solar panel efficiency or wind power experiments.
  • IA: Solar panel angle experiments, insulation comparisons, and power plant efficiency models are common.
