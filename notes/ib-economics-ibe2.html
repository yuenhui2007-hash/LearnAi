Microeconomics

IB DP Economics HL/SL — Topic 2

Overview

Microeconomics (Topic 2) examines how markets function, how prices are determined, and why markets sometimes fail. It is the largest topic in IB Economics and is tested across all papers. Diagrammatic analysis is essential — examiners award marks for correctly drawn and labelled supply and demand diagrams.

2.1 Demand & Supply

Demand

  • Law of demand: as price falls, quantity demanded rises (ceteris paribus).
  • Determinants of demand: income, tastes, price of substitutes/complements, expectations, number of buyers.
Movement along the demand curve: caused by price change of the good itself.
Shift of the demand curve: caused by non-price determinants.

Supply

  • Law of supply: as price rises, quantity supplied rises (ceteris paribus).
  • Determinants of supply: input prices, technology, taxes/subsidies, number of sellers, expectations.

Market Equilibrium

Equilibrium occurs where demand equals supply. At this price, there is no surplus or shortage.

2.2 Elasticity

Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

PED = (% change in quantity demanded) / (% change in price)
PED > 1: elastic (quantity responsive to price)
PED < 1: inelastic (quantity unresponsive to price)
PED = 1: unit elastic
PED = 0: perfectly inelastic; PED = ∞: perfectly elastic

Determinants of PED: availability of substitutes, necessity vs. luxury, proportion of income, time period.

Income Elasticity of Demand (YED)

YED = (% change in quantity demanded) / (% change in income)
YED > 0: normal good; YED < 0: inferior good
YED > 1: luxury good; 0 < YED < 1: necessity

Cross Elasticity of Demand (XED)

XED = (% change in Qd of good A) / (% change in price of good B)
XED > 0: substitutes; XED < 0: complements

Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)

PES = (% change in quantity supplied) / (% change in price)
Determinants: spare capacity, mobility of factors, time period, ease of storage

2.3 Consumer & Producer Surplus

  • Consumer surplus: difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay (area below demand, above price).
  • Producer surplus: difference between what producers receive and their minimum acceptable price (area above supply, below price).
  • Total surplus: consumer surplus + producer surplus — maximised at competitive equilibrium.

2.4 Market Failure

Market failure occurs when the free market allocates resources inefficiently.

Externalities

  • Negative externality: social cost > private cost (e.g., pollution). Leads to overproduction.
  • Positive externality: social benefit > private benefit (e.g., education). Leads to underproduction.

Public Goods

  • Non-excludable: cannot prevent anyone from consuming.
  • Non-rivalrous: one person's consumption does not reduce availability for others.
  • Examples: national defence, street lighting. Free-rider problem means private markets underprovide.

Common Access Resources

  • Rivalrous but non-excludable (e.g., fish stocks, clean air).
  • Tragedy of the commons: overuse due to lack of property rights.

Asymmetric Information

  • One party has more information than another.
  • Moral hazard: hidden action (e.g., insurance encouraging risky behaviour).
  • Adverse selection: hidden characteristics (e.g., sick people buying more insurance).

2.5 Government Intervention

  • Price ceilings: maximum price (e.g., rent control). Causes shortages if set below equilibrium.
  • Price floors: minimum price (e.g., minimum wage). Causes surpluses if set above equilibrium.
  • Indirect taxes: shift supply left; reduce quantity, increase price.
  • Subsidies: shift supply right; increase quantity, decrease price.
  • Regulation: direct control of behaviour (e.g., emissions standards).
  • Tradable permits: market-based solution to negative externalities.

Common Misconceptions

  • Demand vs. quantity demanded: A change in price causes movement along the curve (Qd); other factors shift the curve (D).
  • PED sign: PED is usually stated as an absolute value (positive), even though the calculation gives a negative.
  • Externalities: The existence of an externality means market output is inefficient, not that the market fails completely.
  • Price controls: A price ceiling above equilibrium has no effect; a price floor below equilibrium has no effect.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1 (Part a): 10-mark questions on elasticity calculations, consumer/producer surplus, and market failure diagrams.
  • Paper 1 (Part b): 15-mark evaluations of government intervention policies.
  • Paper 2: Data response with elasticity calculations and policy recommendations.
  • IA: Microeconomics commentaries on market failure and government intervention are very common.

Practice Questions

  • Explain the difference between a shift in supply and a movement along the supply curve.
  • Calculate PED if price rises from $10 to $12 and quantity demanded falls from 100 to 80 units.
  • Draw a diagram showing a negative externality of production and explain why the free market overproduces.
  • Evaluate the effectiveness of indirect taxes in correcting negative externalities.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Demand: inverse price–quantity relationship; shift = non-price factors.
  • Supply: direct price–quantity relationship.
  • PED: responsiveness of Qd to price; |PED| > 1 = elastic.
  • Externalities: social cost/benefit differs from private → market failure.
  • Public goods: non-excludable, non-rivalrous → free-rider problem.
  • Government tools: taxes, subsidies, price controls, regulation, tradable permits.

Exam Tips

  • Always define key terms in your opening sentence.
  • Draw accurate, labelled diagrams — they earn significant marks.
  • Use real-world examples to support theoretical explanations.
  • In evaluation questions, consider short-run vs. long-run, stakeholders, and assumptions.
  • Show elasticity calculations with the formula and substitution.
