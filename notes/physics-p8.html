IGCSE AS Level

Current Electricity

9702 (A-Level) · 0625 (IGCSE)

Detailed Notes

1. Electric Current

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge. In metals, current is carried by free electrons; in electrolytes, by positive and negative ions; in semiconductors, by electrons and holes.

I = Q/t (rate of flow of charge)

Unit: ampere (A) = coulomb per second. Conventional current flows from positive to negative, even though electrons actually flow from negative to positive.

📖 Key Definition

Electric Current (I): The rate of flow of charge. I = ΔQ/Δt. Unit: ampere (A).

2. Charge Carriers

  • Metals: Free electrons are the charge carriers. The number density of free electrons is very high (~10²⁹ m⁻³), which is why metals are good conductors.
  • Electrolytes: Positive and negative ions carry charge. Conduction involves chemical changes at the electrodes.
  • Semiconductors: At low temperatures, few charge carriers. As temperature rises, more electron-hole pairs are created, increasing conductivity.
The drift velocity v of electrons in a conductor is given by I = nAve, where n = number density of charge carriers, A = cross-sectional area, e = electron charge. Typical drift velocities are very small (~10⁻⁴ m/s) because n is enormous.

3. Potential Difference (Voltage)

Potential difference between two points is the energy transferred per unit charge when charge moves from one point to the other.

V = W/Q (energy transferred per unit charge)

Unit: volt (V) = joule per coulomb. A potential difference of 1 V means 1 J of energy is transferred per coulomb of charge.

4. Resistance

Resistance is a measure of how much a component opposes the flow of current. It is defined as the ratio of potential difference across the component to the current through it.

R = V/I

Unit: ohm (Ω). For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, R is constant and V ∝ I.

5. Ohm's Law

For a metallic conductor at constant temperature, the current is directly proportional to the potential difference. The V-I graph is a straight line through the origin.

V = IR (for ohmic conductors)

⚠️ Common Misconception

Ohm's Law does not apply to all conductors! It only applies to metallic conductors at constant temperature. Filament lamps, diodes, and thermistors do not obey Ohm's Law because their resistance changes with temperature or current.

6. Resistivity

Resistivity is a material property that does not depend on the dimensions of the sample. It relates resistance to the geometry of the conductor:

R = ρL/A

where ρ = resistivity (unit: Ω·m), L = length, A = cross-sectional area. Good conductors (copper, silver) have low resistivity; insulators have very high resistivity.

📝 Worked Example — Resistivity

Question: A wire of length 2.0 m has a resistance of 4.5 Ω. The resistivity of the material is 1.8 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m. Calculate the diameter of the wire.

Solution:

R = ρL/A → A = ρL/R = (1.8 × 10⁻⁸ × 2.0)/4.5 = 8.0 × 10⁻⁹ m²

A = πd²/4 → d = √(4A/π) = √(4 × 8.0 × 10⁻⁹/π) = 1.01 × 10⁻⁴ m (0.10 mm)

7. Power in Electrical Circuits

Power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred. There are three equivalent expressions:

P = VI = I²R = V²/R

Unit: watt (W). The energy dissipated in a resistor is converted to internal energy (heat) — this is called Joule heating.

📝 Worked Example — Power and Cost

Question: A 2.0 kW electric heater is used for 3.5 hours. The cost of electricity is 25p per kWh. Calculate: (a) the energy used in kWh, (b) the cost.

Solution:

(a) Energy = power × time = 2.0 × 3.5 = 7.0 kWh

(b) Cost = 7.0 × 25 = 175p (£1.75)

🎯 Exam-Style Question

Question: A copper wire of diameter 0.25 mm carries a current of 2.0 A. The number density of free electrons in copper is 8.0 × 10²⁸ m⁻³. Calculate the drift velocity of the electrons. (e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) [3 marks]

Mark Scheme

• A = πd²/4 = π × (0.25 × 10⁻³)²/4 = 4.91 × 10⁻⁸ m² (1 mark)

• I = nAve → v = I/(nAe) (1 mark)

• v = 2.0/(8.0 × 10²⁸ × 4.91 × 10⁻⁸ × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 3.18 × 10⁻⁴ m/s (accept 3.2 × 10⁻⁴) (1 mark)

🎯 Exam-Style Question

Question: (a) State Ohm's law. (b) A resistor has a potential difference of 12 V across it and a current of 0.25 A. Calculate its resistance. (c) The resistor is a wire of length 1.5 m and cross-sectional area 0.20 mm². Calculate the resistivity of the material. [4 marks]

Mark Scheme

• (a) For a metallic conductor at constant temperature, current is directly proportional to potential difference / V = IR (1 mark)

• (b) R = V/I = 12/0.25 = 48 Ω (1 mark)

• (c) ρ = RA/L = (48 × 0.20 × 10⁻⁶)/1.5 (1 mark)

• ρ = 6.4 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m (1 mark)

Last-Minute Summary

  • I = Q/t (A) — rate of flow of charge
  • V = W/Q (V) — energy per unit charge
  • R = V/I (Ω) — opposition to current
  • Ohm's Law: V ∝ I at constant temperature (metallic conductors only)
  • Resistivity: R = ρL/A
  • Power: P = VI = I²R = V²/R (W)
  • Drift velocity: I = nAve

Exam Tips

  • Always check whether a component is ohmic before applying V = IR.
  • Convert all lengths to metres and areas to m² before calculating resistivity.
  • Drift velocity is typically very small — if your answer is m/s, check your calculation!
  • For power calculations, choose the formula that uses the quantities you know.
  • Remember: conventional current flows + to −; electron flow is − to +.
  • In resistivity experiments, measure diameter at several points and average.
