H1: Origins of WWI

📘 IGCSE Content

The Alliance System

  • Triple Alliance (1882): Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy — Bismarck's diplomatic creation to isolate France
  • Triple Entente (1907): Britain, France, Russia — formed in response to German expansion
  • Significance: Turned regional disputes into continental conflicts; guaranteed escalation

Militarism

  • Arms race: Germany's naval expansion challenged British supremacy; Dreadnought crisis 1909
  • Schlieffen Plan: German strategy for two-front war — attack France through Belgium first
  • Conscription: All major powers had large standing armies by 1914

Imperialism

  • Moroccan Crises (1905, 1911): Germany challenged French influence; heightened tensions
  • Balkan Wars (1912–13): Weakened Ottoman Empire; Serbia emerged stronger

The July Crisis 1914

  • 28 June: Archduke Franz Ferdinand assassinated in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip (Black Hand)
  • 23 July: Austria-Hungary issued impossible ultimatum to Serbia
  • 28 July: Austria declared war on Serbia
  • 1 August: Germany declared war on Russia
  • 3 August: Germany declared war on France; invaded Belgium
  • 4 August: Britain declared war on Germany (guarantee to Belgium, 1839)

📗 AS Level Content

Historiographical Debates

  • Fischer Thesis (1961): Germany deliberately planned war; "War Council" December 1912 as evidence of intent
  • Counter-arguments: Clark (Sleepwalkers) — all powers share blame; crisis mismanagement not deliberate planning
  • Structural vs intentionalist: Were long-term forces or short-term decisions more important?

Source Evaluation

  • Grey's speech to Parliament (3 Aug 1914): Justification for British entry
  • Bethmann-Hollweg's "blank cheque": Evidence of German encouragement to Austria

📙 A2 Level Content

Thematic Synthesis

  • German responsibility: Weltpolitik, naval expansion, support for Austria — but other powers made choices too
  • Role of nationalism: Pan-Slavism vs Pan-Germanism; Serbian nationalism as trigger
  • Systemic failure: Alliance system + mobilisation timetables made war difficult to stop once started

Essay Frameworks

  • "Germany was solely responsible": Evaluate Fischer evidence vs structural factors
  • "Short-term causes were more important": July Crisis analysis vs long-term militarism

H2: The Holocaust

📘 IGCSE Content

Definition

The systematic, state-sponsored persecution and murder of approximately 6 million Jews by Nazi Germany and collaborators between 1941 and 1945.

Stages of Persecution

  • 1933–35: Boycotts, Nuremberg Laws (stripped citizenship, banned intermarriage)
  • 1938: Kristallnacht — state-organised pogrom; 30,000 sent to concentration camps
  • 1939–41: Ghettoisation — Warsaw, Lodz, Krakow; forced labour
  • 1941–45: Final Solution — Einsatzgruppen (mass shootings), extermination camps (Auschwitz, Treblinka, Sobibor)

Key Events

  • Wannsee Conference (20 Jan 1942): Coordinated implementation of Final Solution
  • Auschwitz-Birkenau: Largest death camp; 1.1 million killed, mostly Jews

📗 AS Level Content

Ideological Roots

  • Anti-Semitism: Historical prejudice + pseudo-scientific racial theories
  • Volksgemeinschaft: Racial community excluding Jews
  • Lebensraum: Need for living space in East — Jews as obstacle

Role of Collaboration

  • Vichy France, Hungary, Romania — local administrations assisted deportations
  • Some resistance: Denmark saved most Jews; Bulgaria refused deportations

📙 A2 Level Content

Historiography

  • Intentionalists: Hitler planned genocide from early (Mein Kampf 1925)
  • Functionalists: Holocaust emerged from wartime radicalisation; cumulative radicalisation
  • Browning (Ordinary Men): Ordinary Germans participated — not just fanatics

Comparison & Significance

  • Unique or part of broader genocide? Comparison with other 20th century atrocities
  • Memory and memorialisation: Yad Vashem, Holocaust Memorial Day

H3: Cold War in Europe

📘 IGCSE Content

Origins (1941–47)

  • Grand Alliance tensions: Different ideologies, delayed Second Front
  • Yalta (Feb 1945): Agreed on post-war Germany; disagreed on Poland
  • Potsdam (July 1945): Truman (atomic bomb knowledge), Stalin — increasing suspicion
  • Iron Curtain speech (March 1946): Churchill — symbolic division of Europe

Containment & Expansion

  • Truman Doctrine (1947): US commitment to stop communism globally
  • Marshall Plan (1948): $13bn economic aid to rebuild Western Europe
  • Berlin Blockade (1948–49): Soviet test; defeated by Western airlift
  • NATO (1949): Military alliance of Western powers

Key Crises

  • Hungarian Uprising (1956): Crushed by Soviet tanks; West did not intervene
  • Berlin Wall (1961): Symbol of division; stopped refugee flow
  • Prague Spring (1968): Dubcek's reforms crushed by Warsaw Pact invasion

📗 AS Level Content

Ideological Conflict

  • Capitalism vs Communism: Free markets vs state planning; democracy vs one-party rule
  • Security dilemma: Both sides saw defensive measures as threats

Détente (1969–79)

  • SALT I (1972): Limited nuclear arms
  • Helsinki Accords (1975): Recognised post-war borders; human rights provisions
  • Reasons: Nuclear parity, economic costs, Sino-Soviet split

📙 A2 Level Content

End of Cold War (1985–91)

  • Gorbachev's reforms: Glasnost (openness) + Perestroika (restructuring)
  • Fall of Berlin Wall (9 Nov 1989): Symbolic end of division
  • German reunification (1990): NATO membership issue resolved
  • Soviet collapse (Dec 1991): Gorbachev resigned; USSR dissolved

Historiography

  • Traditionalists: Soviet expansion caused Cold War
  • Revisionists: US economic imperialism provoked Soviet response
  • Post-revisionists: Mutual misunderstanding and security dilemma

H4: Cold War in Asia

📘 IGCSE Content

Chinese Civil War & Communist Victory

  • 1945–49: Civil war resumes after Japanese defeat
  • CCP victory (1949): Mao Zedong proclaims People's Republic; Chiang Kai-shek retreats to Taiwan
  • Reasons for CCP victory: Peasant support, effective guerrilla warfare, KMT corruption

Korean War (1950–53)

  • June 1950: North Korea (Kim Il-sung) invades South
  • UN intervention: Led by US General MacArthur
  • Chinese intervention (Oct 1950): 300,000 "volunteers" push UN back to 38th parallel
  • Armistice (1953): Korea remains divided at 38th parallel

Vietnam War

  • 1954: Geneva Conference divides Vietnam at 17th parallel
  • US escalation: Gulf of Tonkin incident (1964) → full ground war
  • Tet Offensive (1968): Military defeat but psychological victory for Viet Cong
  • US withdrawal (1973): Paris Peace Accords
  • Fall of Saigon (1975): Communist victory; reunification

📗 AS Level Content

Containment in Asia

  • Domino Theory: If one country falls to communism, others will follow
  • SEATO (1954): Southeast Asia Treaty Organisation
  • Cost of containment: 58,000 US deaths in Vietnam; $150bn spent

Sino-Soviet Split

  • Ideological differences: Mao's revolutionary fervour vs Khrushchev's peaceful coexistence
  • 1960: Soviet advisors withdrawn from China
  • 1969: Border clashes — potential for Sino-US rapprochement

📙 A2 Level Content

US-China Rapprochement

  • 1971: Kissinger secret visit; Ping-Pong diplomacy
  • 1972: Nixon visits China; Shanghai Communiqué
  • Strategic significance: Triangular diplomacy — US plays China against USSR

Why Communism Succeeded in Asia

  • Anti-colonialism: Communism linked to national liberation
  • Peasant support: Land reform promises
  • US failures: Supported unpopular regimes (Diem, Chiang)

H5: Civil Rights USA

📘 IGCSE Content

Reconstruction (1865–77)

  • 13th Amendment (1865): Abolished slavery
  • 14th Amendment (1868): Citizenship and equal protection
  • 15th Amendment (1870): Voting rights regardless of race
  • Failure: Jim Crow laws, sharecropping, KKK violence

Jim Crow Era (1877–1954)

  • Plessy v Ferguson (1896): "Separate but equal" justified segregation
  • Disenfranchisement: Poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses
  • Economic discrimination: Limited jobs, lower wages, segregated facilities

Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955–56)

  • Rosa Parks: Refused to give up seat (1 Dec 1955)
  • Martin Luther King Jr: Emerged as leader; 381-day boycott
  • Result: Supreme Court banned bus segregation (Browder v Gayle)

Key Legislation

  • Civil Rights Act (1964): Banned discrimination in public places and employment
  • Voting Rights Act (1965): Eliminated literacy tests; federal oversight

📗 AS Level Content

Campaign Strategies

  • NAACP: Legal strategy — challenged segregation in courts (Brown v Board 1954)
  • SCLC: Non-violent direct action; moral persuasion
  • SNCC: Student activism; Freedom Rides (1961), Freedom Summer (1964)
  • Nation of Islam: Black separatism; Malcolm X until 1965

Opposition & Violence

  • White Citizens' Councils: Economic boycott of activists
  • KKK: Bombings, murders, intimidation
  • Police brutality: Birmingham (1963) — attacked with dogs and fire hoses

📙 A2 Level Content

Evaluation of Success

  • Political: Voting rights secured; Black elected officials increased
  • Economic: Limited progress — wealth gap persisted
  • Social: De jure segregation ended; de facto segregation remained
  • Black Power: Stokely Carmichael, Black Panthers — shift from integration to empowerment

Comparative Analysis

  • Comparison with other civil rights movements globally
  • Role of federal government vs grassroots activism

H6: International History

📘 IGCSE Content

The UN and the New World Order

  • UN founded (24 Oct 1945): Security Council (USA, USSR, UK, France, China as permanent members with veto), General Assembly, Secretariat, International Court of Justice.
  • Goals: maintain peace, self-determination, human rights, economic cooperation.
  • Limitations: veto power paralysed Security Council during Cold War; superpowers used UN when useful, ignored it when not (e.g., Korea 1950 — USSR boycott allowed action).

Decolonisation

  • India and Pakistan (1947): Mountbatten Plan; partition; 1 million dead in communal violence.
  • Middle East: Israel declared independence (1948); Arab-Israeli War; 700,000 Palestinian refugees. Suez Crisis (1956): Britain, France, Israel invaded Egypt after Nasser nationalised canal; USA and USSR forced withdrawal — end of British imperial role.
  • Africa: Ghana first sub-Saharan colony to gain independence (1957, Nkrumah). 1960 — "Year of Africa" (17 states independent). Algeria (1962) after brutal war with France.
  • Congo (1960): Patrice Lumumba assassinated (1961); UN intervention; civil war and secession of Katanga.
  • Non-Aligned Movement (1961): Bandung Conference (1955); founding members included Tito, Nasser, Nehru, Sukarno, Nkrumah.

The Arab-Israeli Conflict

  • 1948 War: Israel defeated Arab states; expanded territory beyond UN partition plan.
  • 1956 Suez Crisis: Nasser nationalised canal; British/French/Israeli invasion; superpower pressure forced retreat.
  • Six-Day War (1967): Israel launched pre-emptive strike; destroyed Egyptian air force; captured Sinai, Gaza, West Bank, East Jerusalem, Golan Heights. UN Resolution 242 called for withdrawal and peace.
  • Yom Kippur War (1973): Egypt and Syria attacked on Jewish Day of Atonement. Initial Arab gains reversed by Israeli counter-offensive. Oil crisis — OPEC embargo.
  • Camp David Accords (1978): Sadat (Egypt) and Begin (Israel) peace treaty; Sinai returned to Egypt. Sadat assassinated 1981.
  • Palestinian issue: PLO founded 1964 (Arafat); Intifadas (1987–93, 2000–05); Oslo Accords (1993).

The Cuban Revolution and Missile Crisis

  • 1959: Fidel Castro overthrew Batista. US-owned property nationalised; alliance with USSR.
  • Bay of Pigs (April 1961): CIA-backed invasion failed; embarrassed Kennedy.
  • Cuban Missile Crisis (Oct 1962): USSR placed nuclear missiles in Cuba. US naval blockade ("quarantine"). 13-day standoff. Khrushchev agreed to remove missiles in exchange for US pledge not to invade Cuba and secret removal of Jupiter missiles from Turkey.
  • Consequences: Hotline established (1963); Limited Test Ban Treaty (1963); nuclear diplomacy became more cautious.

The Middle East and Oil

  • Iranian Revolution (1979): Shah overthrown; Ayatollah Khomeini established Islamic Republic. US Embassy hostage crisis (Nov 1979–Jan 1981).
  • Iran-Iraq War (1980–88): Saddam Hussein invaded Iran; stalemate; 1 million dead.
  • Oil shocks (1973, 1979): transformed global economy; shifted power to producer states.

📗 AS Level Content

The Role of the UN: Success and Failure

  • Korean War (1950–53): UN authorised intervention only because USSR boycotted Security Council. Demonstrated UN potential when superpowers agreed; impotence when they disagreed.
  • Congo (1960–64): UN peacekeeping (ONUC) controversial — Lumumba's death, Katanga secession. Led to tighter peacekeeping rules.
  • Suez (1956): UN Emergency Force (first armed peacekeeping force) supervised withdrawal. Success for Hammarskjöld's preventive diplomacy.
  • Cold War paralysis: vetoes by both superpowers. General Assembly resolutions non-binding (e.g., condemning Soviet invasion of Afghanistan).

Superpower Involvement in the Middle East

  • USA: supported Israel (economic/military aid), allied with conservative monarchies (Saudi Arabia, Iran pre-1979), sought to contain Soviet influence.
  • USSR: supported Arab nationalist regimes (Egypt, Syria, Iraq), provided arms and advisors. Lost influence after Sadat's expulsion of Soviet advisors (1972) and Camp David.
  • Oil as leverage: 1973 embargo showed Arab states could influence Western policy. But long-term effect was Western energy diversification.

Decolonisation: Causes and Patterns

  • Immediate causes: WWII weakened European powers; Japanese victories shattered myth of white supremacy; USA and USSR supported self-determination.
  • Varied paths: peaceful transfer (India, Ghana); armed struggle (Algeria, Vietnam, Kenya — Mau Mau); negotiated settlement (most of British Africa).
  • Challenges of independence: artificial borders, ethnic divisions, lack of trained administrators, economic dependence (neocolonialism). Cold War competition often exacerbated instability.

The Cuban Missile Crisis: Decision-Making

  • ExComm deliberations: options ranged from air strike to blockade to diplomatic solution. Kennedy chose blockade — demonstrated resolve while allowing time.
  • Khrushchev's motives: close missile gap (USSR had few ICBMs), defend Cuba from invasion, bargain for concessions on Berlin/Turkey.
  • Resolution: public Soviet withdrawal for public US non-invasion pledge + secret Turkish missile deal. Both sides claimed victory; both made concessions.
  • Legacy: "eyeball to eyeball" mythology (later debunked — more negotiation than brinkmanship). Hotline and arms control followed.

📙 A2 Level Content

The Cold War as a Global System

  • Bipolarity shaped international relations but did not determine everything. Non-aligned states carved out autonomy (Tito, Nasser, India).
  • Dependency theory (André Gunder Frank): decolonisation did not end economic exploitation; former colonies remained in periphery, exporting raw materials to core capitalist states.
  • Modernisation theory (Walt Rostow): all societies progress through stages to industrial modernity; US aid could accelerate development. Critiqued as ethnocentric and ignoring structural inequalities.

The Middle East: Local and International Dimensions

  • Arab-Israeli conflict was not purely Cold War proxy war. Local factors (religion, nationalism, refugees, water) were at least as important as superpower rivalry.
  • Camp David showed USA could broker peace without USSR. But Palestinian issue remained unresolved, leading to continued conflict.
  • Iranian Revolution: anti-Western, anti-communist, Islamist. Confounded both superpowers. Demonstrated limits of Cold War framework.

Arms Control and Détente

  • Limited Test Ban Treaty (1963); NPT (1968); SALT I (1972) — froze ICBM and SLBM numbers; ABM Treaty limited missile defences.
  • SALT II (1979) signed but not ratified due to Soviet invasion of Afghanistan.
  • Détente's limits: regional conflicts continued; human rights issues (Helsinki) exploited by dissidents but ignored by governments.
  • Second Cold War (1979–85): Afghanistan, Euromissiles, Reagan's rhetoric ("evil empire"). Détente declared dead.

Essay Frameworks

  • "The UN failed to keep the peace during the Cold War": Vetoes, superpower bypassing, limited enforcement. Counter: Korean War, Suez, peacekeeping successes, post-Cold War expansion of role.
  • "Decolonisation was driven by the Cold War": Superpowers pressured empires; independence movements gained support. Counter: weakened Europe after WWII was primary cause; nationalism predated Cold War.
  • "The Cuban Missile Crisis was a victory for the USA": Missiles removed, Khrushchev backed down, hotline established. Counter: secret Turkey deal, USA pledged not to invade Cuba, crisis showed dangers of nuclear brinkmanship.

H7: French Revolution

📘 IGCSE Content

France in 1774: Ancien Régime

  • Absolute monarchy under Louis XVI (r. 1774–92). Divine right; no constitution.
  • Three Estates: First (clergy, 0.5%), Second (nobility, 1.5%), Third (commoners, 98%). Third Estate included bourgeoisie, urban workers, and peasants.
  • Privilege system: First and Second Estates exempt from most taxation (taille).
  • Financial crisis: wars (Seven Years' War, American Revolution) left massive debt. Calonne's reforms failed (1787).
  • Bad harvests 1787–88; bread prices soared; population pressure.

From Estates-General to National Assembly (1789)

  • Estates-General called (May 1789) — first since 1614. Dispute over voting: by estate (favours First/Second) or by head (favours Third).
  • Third Estate declared itself National Assembly (17 June 1789); Tennis Court Oath (20 June) — pledged not to disband until constitution written.
  • Storming of the Bastille (14 July 1789): popular uprising in Paris; symbol of royal tyranny destroyed.
  • Great Fear (July–Aug 1789): peasant uprisings across France; châteaux attacked.
  • Abolition of feudalism (4 Aug 1789): National Assembly ended feudal dues and privileges.

The Constitutional Monarchy (1789–92)

  • Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (26 Aug 1789): liberty, property, security, resistance to oppression; equality before law; freedom of speech and religion.
  • Constitution of 1791: constitutional monarchy; separation of powers; active vs. passive citizenship (property qualification).
  • Financial reforms: confiscation and sale of Church lands (assignats as currency); Civil Constitution of the Clergy (July 1790) — bishops elected by citizens, priests salaried by state. Pope condemned it; divided France.
  • Flight to Varennes (June 1791): Louis XVI attempted to flee; captured; discredited monarchy.
  • Champ de Mars massacre (July 1791): National Guard fired on republican petitioners.

War and Radicalisation (1792–93)

  • April 1792: France declared war on Austria (and soon Prussia). Fear of counter-revolution and émigré armies.
  • Brunswick Manifesto (July 1792): Allied commander threatened destruction of Paris if royal family harmed. Inflamed radicals.
  • 10 Aug 1792: Insurrection of sans-culottes; stormed Tuileries Palace; royal family imprisoned.
  • September Massacres (Sept 1792): prisoners killed by Paris mobs.
  • National Convention (Sept 1792): abolished monarchy; declared republic.
  • Battle of Valmy (20 Sept 1792): French victory; saved revolution.
  • Trial and execution of Louis XVI (Jan 1793): guillotined 21 Jan 1793.

The Terror (1793–94)

  • Crisis: war (Britain, Spain, Netherlands joined coalition), Vendée revolt (royalist/Catholic uprising), federalist revolts, economic chaos (assignats depreciated).
  • Committee of Public Safety (established April 1793): dominated by Robespierre, Saint-Just, Couthon.
  • Reign of Terror (Sept 1793–July 1794): Revolutionary Tribunal; Law of Suspects; 17,000–40,000 executed.
  • Dechristianisation: churches closed, Gregorian calendar replaced, Cult of Reason/Cult of the Supreme Being.
  • Thermidorian Reaction (27 July 1794): Robespierre arrested and executed (9 Thermidor Year II). End of Terror.

📗 AS Level Content

Causes of the Revolution: Long and Short Term

  • Political: Absolute monarchy increasingly dysfunctional; parlements (especially Paris) resisted royal edicts. Louis XVI indecisive; Marie Antoinette unpopular ("Madame Déficit").
  • Social: Rise of bourgeoisie — wealthy, educated, but excluded from political power and noble status. Noble reaction: some nobles sought to claw back privileges, creating tension.
  • Economic: Debt from wars; inefficient tax system; physiocratic ideas; subsistence crises.
  • Intellectual: Enlightenment — Voltaire (toleration), Rousseau (popular sovereignty, social contract), Montesquieu (separation of powers). Salons and pamphlets spread ideas.
  • Immediate triggers: Bankruptcy, calling of Estates-General, bad harvests.

The Role of Key Groups

  • Bourgeoisie: Led early revolution (Sieyès, What is the Third Estate? 1789). Dominated National Assembly and Convention. Sought legal equality and constitutional government.
  • Sans-culottes: Urban workers and artisans. Demanded price controls, direct democracy, and violent action against counter-revolution. Influenced radical phases (1792–94).
  • Peasants: Drove abolition of feudalism (Great Fear). Supported revolution when it brought land; turned against it when Church attacked and conscription imposed.
  • Women: October Days (1789) — march to Versailles; Olympe de Gouges (Declaration of the Rights of Woman, 1791); excluded from political rights in 1791 Constitution; Republic of Virtue gave them some legal equality but no vote.

From Reform to Terror: Why Did Radicalisation Occur?

  • External war: revolutionaries felt surrounded by enemies; created siege mentality.
  • Internal opposition: Vendée, federalist revolts, royalist plots. Convinced revolutionaries that only terror could save the republic.
  • Economic crisis: assignat inflation, food shortages. Sans-culottes demanded Maximum (price controls, Sept 1793).
  • Ideological: Rousseau's concept of general will justified coercion in name of virtue. Robespierre believed terror was necessary to establish virtue.
  • Political conflict: factional struggle — Girondins vs. Montagnards; purge of Girondins (June 1793); Hébertists and Indulgents also purged.

📙 A2 Level Content

Historiography of the Revolution

  • Marxist (Georges Lefebvre, Albert Soboul): Revolution was bourgeois revolution — transition from feudalism to capitalism. Third Estate led by bourgeoisie overthrew feudal aristocracy. Class struggle central.
  • Revisionist (Alfred Cobban, François Furet): Revolution was not bourgeois in modern sense. Many leaders were nobles or office-holders (robins). Bourgeoisie already economically dominant before 1789. Revolution was political, not social, in origin.
  • Cultural/linguistic turn (Furet, Mona Ozouf): Revolution created modern political culture — nationalism, citizenship, ideology. Language and symbolism were as important as material interests.
  • Tocquevillian (Alexis de Tocqueville): Revolution did not break with past but accelerated centralising trends begun under absolute monarchy. Old regime already modernising.

The Terror: Necessity or Ideological Excess?

  • Defence: Republic was in mortal danger; terror saved revolution from internal and external enemies. War justified emergency measures.
  • Critique: Terror was counter-productive — alienated peasantry (dechristianisation), provoked more resistance, consumed revolutionaries themselves. Robespierre's virtue became tyranny.
  • Comparative: Similar patterns in 20th-century revolutions (Russian, Chinese) — radicalisation under siege, purges of original revolutionaries.

Women and the Revolution

  • Olympe de Gouges challenged male-centric rights. Revolutionary clubs for women (Society of Revolutionary Republican Women, 1793) — banned Oct 1793.
  • Paradox: revolution proclaimed universal rights but excluded women from political participation. Rousseau's domestic ideology influenced republican model.
  • Legacy: Napoleon Code (1804) reinforced patriarchal family structure. Women's political rights not won until 1944 in France.

Essay Frameworks

  • "The French Revolution was caused by the bourgeoisie": Use Marxist interpretation, rise of capitalist economy, Third Estate demands. Counter with Cobban/Furet — bourgeoisie not a class, nobles led revolution.
  • "War caused the Terror": External threats, internal revolt, siege mentality. Counter: terror also driven by ideology (Rousseau, virtue) and internal factionalism.
  • "The Revolution achieved its aims by 1791": Constitutional monarchy, Declaration of Rights, end of feudalism. Counter: monarchy fell, Terror followed, instability until Napoleon.

H8: Hitler's Germany

📘 IGCSE Content

The Rise of the Nazis (1929–33)

  • Wall Street Crash (Oct 1929) triggered global depression. German economy collapsed: unemployment reached 6 million (1932), GDP fell 40%.
  • Weimar Republic fragile: proportional representation created unstable coalitions; Article 48 allowed presidential rule by decree.
  • Nazi appeal: promised economic recovery, national revival, anti-communism, anti-Semitism. Hitler's oratory and propaganda (Goebbels).
  • SA (Sturmabteilung): paramilitary force; street battles with Communists. SS (Schutzstaffel): originally Hitler's bodyguard, later elite force under Himmler.
  • 1930 election: Nazis won 107 seats (18.3%) — up from 12 (2.6%) in 1928.
  • 1932: Nazis peaked — 230 seats (37.3%) in July election. Hitler ran for president against Hindenburg (lost).
  • 30 Jan 1933: Hitler appointed Chancellor by Hindenburg. Only two Nazis in cabinet (Hitler and Frick). Conservatives (Papen, Schleicher) believed they could control him.

Consolidation of Power (1933–34)

  • Reichstag Fire (27 Feb 1933): Dutch communist van der Lubbe arrested. Decree for the Protection of People and State (28 Feb): suspended civil liberties, allowed arrest without trial.
  • March 1933 election: Nazis won 288 seats (43.9%) with coalition partners (DNVP) giving majority. Enabling Act (23 March): gave Hitler power to rule by decree for 4 years. Passed with Centre Party support.
  • Gleichschaltung (coordination): All institutions brought under Nazi control. Trade unions abolished (2 May 1933); replaced by DAF (German Labour Front). Political parties banned (July 1933).
  • Night of the Long Knives (30 June 1934): Hitler purged SA leadership (Röhm and others) — 85–200 killed. SS gained power. Army approved.
  • Hindenburg's death (2 Aug 1934): Hitler merged offices of president and chancellor — became Führer. Army swore personal oath of loyalty.

Nazi Germany 1933–39: Society and Economy

  • Economy: Public works (Autobahns), rearmament (conscription 1935), Mefo bills (off-budget financing). Unemployment fell from 6 million (1932) to under 1 million (1936). However, wages stagnated; working hours increased.
  • Propaganda: Goebbels as Minister of Propaganda. Control of press, radio, cinema, rallies (Nuremberg rallies), Olympics (1936).
  • Education and Youth: Curriculum Nazified; teachers purged. Hitler Youth (compulsory 1936): indoctrination in racial ideology and militarism. League of German Girls.
  • Women: Encouraged to be mothers and homemakers. Honour Cross of the German Mother (bronze for 4 children, silver for 6, gold for 8). Professional women and university students reduced.
  • Religion: Reich Church (pro-Nazi) vs Confessing Church (opposition, e.g., Pastor Niemöller). Concordat with Vatican (1933) violated.
  • Racial policy: Nuremberg Laws (1935); persecution of Jews intensified (see Holocaust notes). Also persecution of Roma, disabled, homosexuals, Jehovah's Witnesses.

Foreign Policy and Road to War

  • Withdrawal from League of Nations (1933). Rearmament announced (1935).
  • Rhineland remilitarised (March 1936): troops entered demilitarised zone. France and Britain did not act.
  • Anschluss with Austria (March 1938): union forbidden by Versailles. Achieved without war.
  • Munich Agreement (Sept 1938): Hitler demanded Sudetenland (German-speaking part of Czechoslovakia). Britain (Chamberlain) and France agreed. "Peace for our time."
  • March 1939: Germany occupied rest of Czechoslovakia — broke Munich promises. Britain guaranteed Poland.
  • Nazi-Soviet Pact (Aug 1939): non-aggression treaty with secret protocol dividing Eastern Europe.
  • 1 Sept 1939: Germany invaded Poland. Britain and France declared war (3 Sept).

War and Defeat (1939–45)

  • Blitzkrieg victories: Poland (Sept 1939), Denmark and Norway (April 1940), Netherlands, Belgium, France (May–June 1940). Dunkirk evacuation (May–June 1940).
  • Battle of Britain (July–Oct 1940): Luftwaffe failed to gain air superiority; invasion of Britain cancelled.
  • Operation Barbarossa (June 1941): invasion of USSR. Initial gains; stalled at Moscow (Dec 1941). Declaration of war on USA (Dec 1941) after Pearl Harbor.
  • Turning points: Stalingrad (Feb 1943), El Alamein (Nov 1942), Kursk (July 1943).
  • D-Day (6 June 1944): Allied invasion of Normandy. Soviet advance from East.
  • Hitler's suicide (30 April 1945); Germany surrendered (7–8 May 1945).

Occupation and Nuremberg (1945–47)

  • Germany divided into four zones (USA, UK, France, USSR). Berlin also divided.
  • Denazification: trials of war criminals. Nuremberg Trials (Nov 1945–Oct 1946): 22 major Nazis tried; 12 sentenced to death (including Göring, Ribbentrop, Kaltenbrunner). Hess life imprisonment.
  • Morgenthau Plan (initial) vs. policy of reconstruction. By 1947, Cold War tensions led to creation of West German state.

📗 AS Level Content

Why Did Hitler Come to Power?

  • Great Depression: economic catastrophe discredited Weimar democracy. Unemployed turned to extremes (Nazis and Communists).
  • Weimar weaknesses: proportional representation, presidential cabinets (Brüning, Papen, Schleicher), lack of democratic culture in some sectors.
  • Conservative elite miscalculation: Papen and Hindenburg believed they could use Hitler's mass support while controlling him. "We've hired him" (Papen).
  • Nazi strengths: effective propaganda, Hitler's charisma, organisation (Gauleiters), appeal across classes (lower middle class, farmers, some workers).
  • Not inevitable: Nazis lost 2 million votes between July and Nov 1932. Hitler appointed by elite manoeuvring, not popular majority.

The Nazi State: Totalitarian or Polycratic?

  • Totalitarian model: Hitler had absolute power; ideology permeated all aspects of life; terror (Gestapo, SS, camps) enforced conformity.
  • Polycratic model (historians like Martin Broszat, Ian Kershaw): Nazi state was chaotic, with competing agencies and personal rivalries. Hitler was a "weak dictator" who often avoided decisions, allowing subordinates to compete.
  • Source evaluation: use of decrees, Hitler's daily routine (late mornings, aversion to paperwork), power of Himmler, Goebbels, Göring.

Consensus and Coercion

  • How much did Germans support the regime? Some historians emphasise popular consent (economic recovery, national pride, effective propaganda). Others emphasise fear and surveillance.
  • Gestapo relatively small (approx. 32,000 for 66 million people). Relied on denunciation by citizens.
  • Opposition: isolated and fragmented. Communist and Socialist undergrounds. White Rose (Munich students, 1942–43). July 20 Plot (1944): Stauffenberg's bomb failed to kill Hitler. Army officers, some clergy (Niemöller, Bonhoeffer).

📙 A2 Level Content

The Intentionalist vs Structuralist Debate on Nazi Foreign Policy

  • Intentionalist (e.g., Klaus Hildebrand, Andreas Hillgruber): Hitler had a clear programme from Mein Kampf (1925): destroy Versailles, expand eastward (Lebensraum), destroy Jews. War was inevitable because Hitler planned it.
  • Structuralist/Functionalist (e.g., Hans Mommsen, Timothy Mason): Nazi foreign policy was more improvised. Economic pressures (need for resources) and internal dynamics drove expansion. Hitler was an opportunist who exploited situations.
  • Mason's "domestic crisis" thesis: expansion was driven by need to solve economic problems and prevent social unrest. War in 1939 was premature because Germany was not ready.
  • A.J.P. Taylor, The Origins of the Second World War (1961): Hitler was a traditional German statesman pursuing traditional aims; other powers mishandled him. Highly controversial.

Hitler as War Leader

  • Initial successes due to boldness and Allied unpreparedness. But strategic errors: invasion of USSR, declaration of war on USA, no retreat orders (Stalingrad).
  • "No surrender" policy wasted German lives. Hitler increasingly detached from reality.
  • Total war: Goebbels' Sportpalast speech (Feb 1943) called for total mobilisation. Speer's armaments production increased despite bombing.

Essay Frameworks

  • "Hitler was a weak dictator": Polycratic state, avoided decisions, chaotic administration. Counter: Hitler made key decisions (Night of Long Knives, war), Führer cult gave him ultimate authority.
  • "The Nazi regime rested on popular support": Economic recovery, propaganda, nationalism. Counter: terror essential; opposition crushed; denunciations show fear, not consent.
  • "Hitler planned war from the start": Mein Kampf, rearmament, eastward expansion. Counter: improvisation, responses to opportunities, economic pressures.

H9: Stalin's Russia

📘 IGCSE Content

The Power Struggle (1924–29)

  • Lenin died (21 Jan 1924). No clear successor. Testament (Dec 1922–Jan 1923) criticised all leading Bolsheviks; recommended removing Stalin as General Secretary. Not published fully.
  • Contenders: Trotsky (brilliant orator, Red Army founder, but arrogant, Jewish, late Bolshevik), Zinoviev and Kamenev (Leningrad/Moscow), Bukharin (right, pro-NEP), Stalin (General Secretary, built patronage).
  • Stalin's strategy: formed alliances to eliminate rivals. With Zinoviev and Kamenev against Trotsky (1924–25); then with Bukharin against Zinoviev and Kamenev (1926–27); then destroyed Bukharin (1928–29).
  • Trotsky expelled from party (1927); exiled (1929); assassinated in Mexico (1940).
  • By 1929 Stalin was undisputed leader.

Collectivisation (1929–34)

  • Goal: consolidate peasant land into collective farms (kolkhozy); state-controlled grain collection; finance industrialisation.
  • Kulaks (wealthier peasants) targeted as "class enemies." Dekulakisation: 1.8 million sent to labour camps or executed; property confiscated.
  • Forced collectivisation met violent resistance. Peasants slaughtered livestock rather than surrender. Grain production fell.
  • Famine (1932–33): Ukraine (Holodomor), Kazakhstan, North Caucasus. 5–7 million dead. Grain exported during famine.
  • Results: by 1937, 93% of peasant households collectivised. Agriculture never fully recovered; inefficiency persisted.

The Five-Year Plans (1928–41)

  • First Five-Year Plan (1928–32): priority to heavy industry (steel, coal, electricity). Magnitogorsk, Dnieprostroi dam. Targets not met but impressive growth.
  • Second Five-Year Plan (1933–37): consumer goods slightly more emphasis. Stakhanovite movement (Alexei Stakhanov, coal miner, 1935).
  • Third Five-Year Plan (1938–41): rearmament priority due to threat of war.
  • Results: USSR became second industrial power. But quality poor; consumer goods neglected; urban housing crisis; rationing.

The Great Terror (1936–38)

  • Show trials: Old Bolsheviks confessed to absurd crimes. Zinoviev and Kamenev (1936); Radek and Pyatakov (1937); Bukharin and Rykov (1938).
  • Army purges: Marshal Tukhachevsky and 8 senior commanders executed (1937). 35,000 officers arrested. Weakened Red Army before WWII.
  • Yezhovshchina (named after NKVD head Yezhov): 700,000–1.2 million executed 1937–38; millions sent to Gulag.
  • Yezhov himself arrested and shot (1940); replaced by Beria.

World War II and Aftermath

  • Nazi-Soviet Pact (Aug 1939): bought time; gained Baltic states and eastern Poland.
  • Operation Barbarossa (22 June 1941): Germany invaded. Initial disasters; 3 million Soviet POWs by end of 1941.
  • Scorched earth; factories moved east of Urals. Leningrad siege (1941–44): 1 million dead. Stalingrad (Aug 1942–Feb 1943): turning point. Kursk (July 1943): largest tank battle.
  • Soviet victory: Red Army reached Berlin (April 1945). 27 million Soviet dead (approx.).
  • Post-war: Stalin reimposed control. Purges in Eastern Europe (show trials: Rajk 1949, Slánský 1952). Leningrad Affair (1949–50). Doctors' Plot (1952–53) — anti-Semitic campaign cut short by Stalin's death.
  • Stalin died (5 March 1953).

📗 AS Level Content

Why Did Stalin Win the Power Struggle?

  • Institutional power: General Secretary controlled appointments (patronage), party organisation, and records. Built loyal network.
  • Ideological flexibility: shifted from left (anti-NEP) to right (pro-NEP) and back to left, outmanoeuvring opponents.
  • Trotsky's weaknesses: underestimated Stalin; missed Lenin's funeral (possibly due to illness — Stalin gave wrong date); arrogant; "permanent revolution" unpopular.
  • Stalin's image: "man of the people," modest, hardworking. Cultivated image of Lenin's loyal disciple.

Collectivisation: Why and With What Results?

  • Motives: ideological (socialist agriculture); political (destroy kulaks as class); economic (extract grain for export and industrialisation); strategic (feed cities and army).
  • Resistance: passive (slaughtering livestock) and active (rebellions in Ukraine and North Caucasus). Government responded with terror and deportation.
  • Human cost: famine of 1932–33 was man-made — grain requisitioned while peasants starved. Some historians debate whether it was genocide (Ukrainian and some Western historians) or catastrophic policy failure (some Russian historians).
  • Long-term: agriculture remained inefficient; kolkhozniki had no incentive; USSR dependent on food imports by 1970s.

The Purges: Causes and Consequences

  • Traditional explanation: Stalin's paranoia and desire for total power. Destroyed all potential rivals.
  • Alternative explanations: social engineering (Sheila Fitzpatrick) — purges as a tool to create a new Soviet elite; local initiative (Getty, Naumov) — regional bosses used purges to settle scores; economic pressures — scapegoating for plan failures.
  • Consequences: Red Army weakened (affected early WWII performance); created climate of fear; but also opened careers for ambitious young workers and party members.

📙 A2 Level Content

Totalitarian Model vs. Revisionism

  • Totalitarian (Carl Friedrich, Zbigniew Brzezinski): Stalin controlled everything through ideology, terror, and monopoly of communications. Society was atomised and passive.
  • Revisionist (Sheila Fitzpatrick, Ronald Suny): Soviet society was not passive. There was social mobility, genuine enthusiasm for modernisation, and negotiation between state and society. Purges were also driven from below (denunciations, local conflicts).
  • Post-revisionist (Stephen Kotkin): Stalinism was a civilisation — people internalised values, worked within system, made careers. Magnitogorsk as case study.

Stalin as Revolutionary or Conservative?

  • Revolutionary: transformed USSR from agrarian to industrial; destroyed old elites; created new socialist society; spread communism.
  • Conservative: reinforced authoritarian tradition of Russian state; used nationalism ("Great Patriotic War"); restored family values (1936 anti-abortion law); hierarchical and bureaucratic.
  • Continuity debate: was Stalinism a continuation of Leninism (one-party state, Cheka, War Communism) or a betrayal?

Comparing Stalin and Hitler

  • Similarities: one-party states, cult of personality, terror camps (Gulag vs. concentration camps), control of culture and education, aggressive foreign policy, mass murder.
  • Differences: Stalin rose within party bureaucracy; Hitler came to power through election/alliance. Nazi ideology was racial; Soviet was class-based. Stalin's terror was often directed at party members; Hitler's initially targeted outsiders (Jews, Communists).
  • Historiographical caution: comparison risks equating two distinct systems; but useful for understanding totalitarianism as a concept.

Essay Frameworks

  • "Stalin's industrialisation was a success": USSR second industrial power by 1937; won WWII; modernised society. Counter: appalling human cost; inefficiency; consumer neglect; agriculture damaged.
  • "The purges were caused by Stalin's paranoia": Eliminated rivals, destroyed Old Bolsheviks, controlled all power. Counter: local dynamics, social engineering, economic scapegoating.
  • "Stalinism was a continuation of Leninism": One-party state, secret police, censorship, terror. Counter: Lenin allowed some debate (until 1921); NEP was market-based; Stalin's personal dictatorship exceeded Lenin's.

  • Historiography: Totalitarian vs Revisionist vs Post-revisionist. Continuity (Lenin→Stalin) vs discontinuity.
  • Exam Tip: Know the progression: power struggle → collectivisation/industrialisation → terror → war → post-war repression. Compare Stalin and Hitler where relevant.

H10: Britain 1918-1951

📘 IGCSE Content

Post-War Britain and the Lloyd George Coalition (1918–22)

  • "Khaki election" (Dec 1918): Lloyd George's coalition won landslide on promise to "make Germany pay."
  • Treaty of Versailles: Britain gained mandates (Palestine, Iraq, Tanganyika), German colonies, and reparations.
  • Domestic reforms: Fisher Education Act (1918) raised school leaving age to 14; Addison Act (1919) began council housing.
  • Economic problems: return to gold standard (1925) at pre-war rate (£1 = $4.86) hurt exports. Unemployment remained high in staple industries (coal, textiles, shipbuilding).
  • Anglo-Irish Treaty (Dec 1921): Irish Free State created (26 counties); Northern Ireland (6 counties) remained in UK. Civil War in Ireland (1922–23).
  • Lloyd George fell (Oct 1922): Conservative backbenchers (Carlton Club meeting) withdrew support.

The Interwar Period: Conservative Dominance (1922–29)

  • Bonar Law (1922–23); Baldwin (1923, 1924–29, 1935–37); MacDonald (Labour, 1924, 1929–31, 1931–35 in National Government).
  • General Strike (1926): miners' dispute over wages and hours. TUC called general strike (4–12 May). Government prepared with volunteers and propaganda (BBC). Strike collapsed; Trade Disputes Act (1927) restricted sympathy strikes.
  • Coal miners locked out until Nov 1926; many accepted worse terms.
  • "Flapper election" (1929): Labour became largest party for first time. Baldwin's "Safety First" campaign failed.

The Great Depression and the National Government (1931–39)

  • Wall Street Crash (1929) hit Britain hard. Gold standard abandoned (Sept 1931). MacDonald's Labour government split over cuts to unemployment benefits.
  • National Government (Aug 1931): MacDonald led coalition with Conservatives and Liberals. "Doctor's Mandate" election (Oct 1931): massive National Government victory (554 seats).
  • Economy: south and Midlands recovered (new industries: cars, chemicals, electronics); north and Wales suffered ("Special Areas"). Jarrow March (1936): 200 unemployed men marched to London.
  • Abdication crisis (1936): Edward VIII wanted to marry Wallis Simpson (divorced American). Baldwin and government opposed; Edward abdicated (Dec 1936); George VI succeeded.
  • Foreign policy: appeasement. Rearmament began slowly (1935) but accelerated after Munich (1938). Conscription introduced (April 1939).

World War II (1939–45)

  • Phoney War (Sept 1939–May 1940); Churchill became PM (10 May 1940) after Norway disaster.
  • Dunkirk (May–June 1940); Battle of Britain (July–Oct 1940); Blitz (Sept 1940–May 1941).
  • North Africa: El Alamein (Nov 1942) — Montgomery defeated Rommel. Turning point in British war.
  • D-Day (June 1944); VE Day (8 May 1945).
  • Home front: rationing (from Jan 1940), evacuation, conscription of women, Bevin Boys (mines), Land Army.
  • Beveridge Report (Dec 1942): proposed welfare state — attack on "five giants" (want, disease, ignorance, squalor, idleness).

The Attlee Government (1945–51)

  • Labour landslide (July 1945): 393 seats. Mandate for radical change.
  • Nationalisation: Bank of England (1946), coal (1947), railways and canals (1948), steel (1951 — reversed by Conservatives).
  • NHS (1948): Aneurin Bevan. Free at point of use. Doctors initially opposed (BMA).
  • Welfare state: National Insurance Act (1946); National Assistance Act (1948); Education Act (1944, implemented by Labour) — Butler Act, tripartite system (grammar, technical, secondary modern), free secondary education, school leaving age raised to 15.
  • India and Pakistan independent (1947); Burma and Ceylon (1948); end of British Mandate in Palestine (1948).
  • Marshall Aid (1948): Britain received largest share. Austerity continued; bread rationing (1946–48).
  • Korea (1950–53): Britain contributed troops.
  • 1951 election: Conservatives won. Churchill returned to office (Oct 1951).

📗 AS Level Content

The Decline of the Liberal Party and Rise of Labour

  • Liberal split (1916): Lloyd George vs. Asquith. Never reunited. Lloyd George's "coupon election" (1918) associated Liberals with Conservatives.
  • Labour growth: trade union expansion (from 4 million 1914 to 8 million 1920); 1918 constitution (Clause IV committed to common ownership); individual membership.
  • 1924: first Labour government (minority). MacDonald as PM. Not radical — focused on housing and unemployment. Zinoviev Letter (Oct 1924) probably forged; contributed to Labour defeat.
  • By 1931 Labour had replaced Liberals as main opposition.

Depression and the National Government

  • Was the National Government a betrayal of Labour? MacDonald and Snowden accepted cuts; most Labour MPs opposed. Expelled from party.
  • Economic policy: orthodox (balanced budgets, deflation). Abandoned gold standard; protective tariffs (Ottawa Conference, 1932); cheap money (low interest rates).
  • Recovery was patchy: new industries in Midlands/south; old staples in north declined. "Two Britains." Government's "Special Areas" Act (1934) was limited.
  • Political consensus: no party proposed radical alternatives. Labour moved left (Socialism and Peace, 1934) but remained out of power.

Appeasement: Why and With What Results?

  • Motives: guilt over Versailles; fear of another war; belief Hitler had legitimate grievances; military unpreparedness; economic constraints; focus on empire; pacifist public opinion.
  • Munich (Sept 1938): Chamberlain returned with "peace for our time." Critics (Churchill, Duff Cooper, Eden) argued it was dishonourable and encouraged Hitler.
  • Rearmament: actually began in 1935 (Defence White Paper) and accelerated 1938–39. By 1939 Britain was spending heavily on air force and radar.
  • Guarantee to Poland (March 1939): marked end of appeasement. Conscription (April 1939).

War and Social Change

  • "People's War" thesis: war broke down class barriers; created expectations of welfare state; Labour's 1945 victory reflected desire for change.
  • Evidence: evacuation exposed middle classes to poverty; bombing was egalitarian; common sacrifice; full employment.
  • Counter: class differences persisted; middle-class evacuees often returned; rationing was accepted but not enjoyed; Churchill still popular in 1945 but voters wanted domestic reform.

📙 A2 Level Content

Consensus Politics: Was There a Post-War Settlement?

  • Butler-Adler thesis (Paul Addison): wartime coalition created cross-party consensus on welfare state, full employment, and mixed economy. Both parties accepted Keynesianism and Beveridge.
  • Critics: consensus exaggerated. Labour was more radical than Conservatives. Conservatives accepted welfare state under pressure (1945 defeat). Differences remained over nationalisation, trade unions, and taxation.
  • Thatcherite critique (1970s–80s): consensus was a mistake — stifled enterprise, created dependency, led to decline.

The British Empire and Decline

  • 1918–39: Empire at greatest territorial extent. But Dominion status (Statute of Westminster, 1931) weakened control. India Act (1935) granted limited self-government.
  • Post-1945: rapid decolonisation. India (1947) most important. Reasons: Labour's anti-imperialism, economic weakness, Indian nationalism (Congress, Muslim League), WWII discredited empire.
  • Debate: was decolonisation planned retreat or forced surrender? Some argue Britain managed orderly withdrawal; others that it was driven by nationalist pressure and economic incapacity.

Comparing Interwar Britain with Other Democracies

  • Britain avoided fascism and communism. Why? Stable parliamentary tradition; gradual reform; Labour as constitutional party; no military defeat or economic catastrophe on German scale.
  • But: Mosley's BUF (1932) attracted some support; Cable Street (1936) demonstrated anti-fascist opposition. BBC and press were largely anti-fascist.
  • Comparison with France (Popular Front, instability) and USA (New Deal): Britain's response was more conservative, less radical.

Essay Frameworks

  • "The General Strike was a defeat for the trade unions": TUC called off strike; miners lost; Trade Disputes Act. Counter: demonstrated solidarity; government overestimated trade union threat; unions survived.
  • "Appeasement was the only realistic policy": Military unpreparedness, economic constraints, public opinion. Counter: encouraged Hitler, sacrificed Czechoslovakia, moral failure.
  • "The Attlee government transformed Britain": Welfare state, NHS, nationalisation, decolonisation. Counter: many wartime plans already made; Conservatives would have implemented similar reforms; economic constraints limited achievement.

H11: US Civil War

📘 IGCSE Content

Slavery and the Antebellum South

  • By 1860: 4 million enslaved people in 15 slave states. Cotton kingdom — "King Cotton" dominated exports and global textile industry.
  • Slave system: plantation agriculture (cotton, tobacco, rice, sugar). Slave codes restricted movement, education, marriage. Punishments: whipping, sale of family members.
  • Domestic slave trade: 1 million enslaved people forcibly moved from Upper South to Deep South (1810–60) — "Second Middle Passage."
  • Free Black population: 500,000 by 1860; faced discrimination and risk of kidnapping.

North and South: Diverging Economies and Societies

  • North: industrialising, urbanising, wage labour, immigration (Irish, German), diversified economy, railroads, canals.
  • South: agrarian, dependent on slave labour, limited industrialisation, wealth concentrated in planter class (top 10% owned 50% of slaves).
  • Political balance: 11 free states and 11 slave states by 1819. Missouri Compromise (1820) maintained balance: Missouri slave, Maine free; slavery prohibited north of 36°30′ (except Missouri).

Expansion and the Slavery Question

  • Texas (1836–45): Independent after rebellion against Mexico; annexed 1845 — provoked Mexican-American War (1846–48).
  • Mexican Cession (1848): USA gained California, New Mexico, Arizona, etc. Wilmot Proviso (1846) sought to ban slavery in new territories — defeated but inflamed debate.
  • Compromise of 1850: California free state; Utah and New Mexico territories decide by popular sovereignty; Fugitive Slave Act strengthened; slave trade banned in DC (but slavery retained).
  • Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854): Stephen Douglas's bill organised territories on basis of popular sovereignty — overturned Missouri Compromise. Led to "Bleeding Kansas" — pro- and anti-slavery settlers fought (1854–56).

The Rise of Abolitionism and Southern Reaction

  • Abolitionism: William Lloyd Garrison (The Liberator, 1831); Theodore Weld; Frederick Douglass (escaped slave, orator, writer); Harriet Tubman (Underground Railroad).
  • Uncle Tom's Cabin (Harriet Beecher Stowe, 1852): bestselling novel; shaped Northern opinion against slavery.
  • Southern defence of slavery: "positive good" argument (John C. Calhoun); biblical justifications; paternalism. Gag rules in Congress suppressed anti-slavery petitions.

Political Crisis: 1850s

  • Fugitive Slave Act (1850): Required citizens to assist in capturing escaped slaves. Outraged North; Personal Liberty Laws passed by Northern states.
  • Dred Scott decision (1857): Supreme Court ruled African Americans could not be citizens; Congress could not ban slavery in territories. Undermined Republican platform.
  • Lincoln-Douglas debates (1858): Senate race in Illinois. Douglas defended popular sovereignty; Lincoln opposed expansion of slavery but did not call for abolition where it existed. Douglas won Senate seat but debates made Lincoln nationally known.
  • John Brown's raid (Oct 1859): Abolitionist seized federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry; hoped to spark slave rebellion. Executed. Seen as martyr in North; terrorist in South.

The Election of 1860 and Secession

  • 1860 election: Democratic Party split (Northern Douglas vs. Southern Breckinridge). Republicans nominated Lincoln on platform of opposing slavery's expansion (not abolition).
  • Lincoln won without a single Southern electoral vote. 39.8% popular vote, 180 electoral votes.
  • South Carolina seceded (20 Dec 1860). By Feb 1861, 7 states had seceded. Confederate States of America formed (Feb 1861); Jefferson Davis president.
  • Lincoln's inaugural (4 March 1861): pledged not to interfere with slavery where it existed but vowed to hold federal property.
  • Fort Sumter (12 April 1861): Confederate forces fired on federal fort in Charleston harbour. Lincoln called for volunteers to suppress rebellion. 4 more states seceded (Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, North Carolina).

📗 AS Level Content

Was the Civil War Inevitable?

  • Earlier compromises (1820, 1850) had preserved Union. But each compromise was more difficult and left both sides dissatisfied.
  • Structural factors: slavery was incompatible with free labour ideology; expansion made compromise impossible because each new state tilted balance.
  • Contingent factors: if Douglas had won 1860, or if different candidate had run, or if Fort Sumter had been evacuated — war might have been delayed or avoided. But fundamental conflict likely to erupt eventually.

The Role of Political Parties

  • Second Party System (Whigs vs Democrats) collapsed over slavery. Whigs divided (Conscience vs Cotton Whigs); collapsed by 1856.
  • Republican Party founded (1854): anti-slavery expansion, free soil, free labour, protective tariffs. Drew from former Whigs, Free-Soilers, anti-slavery Democrats.
  • Democrats: only national party remaining but split along sectional lines by 1860.
  • Know-Nothings (American Party): nativist, anti-immigrant, anti-Catholic; briefly strong but irrelevant to slavery issue.

Lincoln's Position on Slavery

  • Lincoln was anti-slavery but not an abolitionist in 1860. Opposed expansion but promised not to interfere in slave states. This was enough to provoke secession.
  • Why did South secede despite Lincoln's moderation? Because slave economy required expansion to maintain political power and economic viability. "Slave Power" feared encirclement by free states.
  • Lincoln's evolution: Emancipation Proclamation (1863) transformed war aims. But see Civil Rights notes for Reconstruction era.

Economic Interpretations

  • Charles Beard (1920s): war was conflict between industrial Northeast and agrarian South over economic interests (tariffs, banks, internal improvements). Slavery was pretext.
  • Critics: Beard underestimated moral and political dimensions. Most Northerners fought to preserve Union, not for economic gain. Slavery was central to Southern economy and identity.

📙 A2 Level Content

Historiography: Causes of the Civil War

  • "Blundering generation" (James Randall, Avery Craven): War caused by failures of political leadership and extremists on both sides. Moderates lost control; emotion overran reason. Slavery could have died out peacefully.
  • Revisionist (1930s–40s): War was avoidable; abolitionists and fire-eaters (Southern extremists) pushed moderates into conflict.
  • Neo-revisionist / Consensus: War was fundamentally about slavery, but not necessarily inevitable. Compromises failed because of political miscalculations.
  • Modern consensus (since 1960s): Slavery was the central cause. Without slavery, no secession; without secession, no war. Economic, political, and cultural differences all derived from slavery system.

Slavery as a Constitutional and Moral Issue

  • Constitutional ambiguity: Constitution never mentioned "slavery" directly ("persons held to service"). 3/5 clause gave South disproportionate power. Fugitive Slave clause.
  • States' rights vs federal authority: South used states' rights to protect slavery; but supported federal Fugitive Slave Act. Inconsistency revealed slavery as real issue, not abstract states' rights.
  • Moral dimensions: abolitionism was a moral crusade; Southern defence of slavery also moralised ("positive good," biblical, paternalistic).

The International Dimension

  • Britain and France depended on Southern cotton but had anti-slavery sentiment. British workers supported North despite Lancashire cotton famine.
  • Trent Affair (1861): Union intercepted British ship carrying Confederate diplomats; nearly caused Anglo-American war. Lincoln released diplomats.
  • Emancipation Proclamation (1863) made it impossible for Britain to support South.

Essay Frameworks

  • "Slavery was the only cause of the Civil War": Secession documents cite slavery; economy dependent on it; Dred Scott, Kansas-Nebraska, all about slavery. Counter: states' rights, tariff disputes, cultural differences also factors — but these derived from slavery.
  • "The Civil War was caused by political failure": Compromises of 1820, 1850 failed; party system collapsed; leaders polarised. Counter: political failure was symptom of irreconcilable differences, not cause.
  • "The South seceded to protect slavery, not states' rights": Confederate constitution protected slavery; states' rights rhetoric inconsistent (supported Fugitive Slave Act). Counter: Southerners genuinely believed in limited federal government.

H12: International Relations

📘 IGCSE Content

The New European Order, 1871–1914

  • German unification (1871) shifted balance of power. Bismarck's alliance system (see h1): Dual Alliance 1879, Triple Alliance 1882, Reinsurance Treaty 1887.
  • Kaiser Wilhelm II (1888) abandoned Bismarck's caution. Weltpolitik; naval expansion; Morocco crises.
  • Triple Entente (1907): Britain, France, Russia. Europe divided into two armed camps.
  • Balkan crises (1908–09, 1912–13) destabilised region. Assassination at Sarajevo (28 June 1914) triggered July Crisis and WWI.

The First World War (1914–18)

  • Western Front: Schlieffen Plan failed at Marne (Sept 1914); trench warfare 1914–18; Verdun (1916), Somme (1916), Passchendaele (1917). Stalemate until 1918.
  • Eastern Front: initial Russian advances; German victories (Tannenberg 1914); Russia exited after Bolshevik Revolution (Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, March 1918).
  • War expanded: Ottoman Empire joined Central Powers (Nov 1914); Gallipoli (1915–16); Middle East campaigns (Lawrence of Arabia). Italy joined Entente (1915); Romania (1916); USA (April 1917).
  • Why did USA enter? Unrestricted submarine warfare (Lusitania 1915, resumption 1917); Zimmerman Telegram (Jan 1917) — German proposal for Mexican alliance against USA.
  • 1918: German Spring Offensive failed; Allied Hundred Days Offensive; armistice (11 Nov 1918).

The Paris Peace Settlement (1919–20)

  • Big Three: Wilson (USA), Lloyd George (Britain), Clemenceau (France). Orlando (Italy) less influential.
  • Wilson's Fourteen Points (Jan 1918): self-determination, League of Nations, freedom of seas, open diplomacy. Idealistic but vague.
  • Treaty of Versailles (June 1919): War Guilt Clause (Article 231); reparations (£6.6 billion); demilitarisation (Rhineland occupied, army limited to 100,000); loss of colonies; Alsace-Lorraine to France; Polish Corridor.
  • Other treaties: St Germain (Austria), Trianon (Hungary), Neuilly (Bulgaria), Sèvres (Ottoman, revised by Lausanne 1923).
  • League of Nations: Covenant included collective security, arbitration, disarmament. USA never joined. 42 founding members.

The League of Nations and the 1920s

  • Successes: Aaland Islands (1920), Upper Silesia (1921), Mosul (1926), Greece-Bulgaria (1925).
  • Failures: Vilna (1920) — Poland seized city; League unable to act. Corfu (1923) — Mussolini bombarded Greek island; League backed down.
  • Disarmament: Washington Naval Conference (1921–22) limited US, Britain, Japan navies. Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928) outlawed war — no enforcement mechanism.
  • Locarno Era (1925): Germany, France, Belgium guaranteed borders; Britain and Italy as guarantors. Germany joined League (1926). Dawes Plan (1924) and Young Plan (1929) rescheduled reparations.

The Road to War, 1929–39

  • Great Depression: economic nationalism; extremism rose in Germany and Japan.
  • Japan invaded Manchuria (1931): League investigated (Lytton Report); Japan left League (1933). No effective action — demonstrated League's weakness against major powers.
  • Italy invaded Abyssinia (1935): League imposed sanctions but excluded oil; Hoare-Laval Pact (secret deal to give Italy most of Abyssinia) leaked and abandoned. Mussolini conquered Abyssinia (1936).
  • German rearmament (1935); Rhineland remilitarised (1936); Anschluss (1938); Munich (1938); Czechoslovakia occupied (1939); Poland invaded (1939).
  • Appeasement: Britain and France sought to avoid war, believed Hitler had limited aims, feared communism more than fascism.
  • Nazi-Soviet Pact (Aug 1939): stunned world; enabled Hitler's invasion of Poland.
  • 1 Sept 1939: Germany invaded Poland. Britain and France declared war (3 Sept).

World War II (1939–45)

  • Blitzkrieg in Europe (1939–41): Poland, Scandinavia, Low Countries, France. Battle of Britain (1940) — first German defeat.
  • Barbarossa (June 1941): Germany invaded USSR. Pearl Harbor (Dec 1941): Japan attacked USA; Germany declared war on USA.
  • Turning points: Midway (June 1942) — Pacific; El Alamein (Nov 1942) — North Africa; Stalingrad (Feb 1943) — Eastern Front.
  • Grand Alliance: Churchill, Roosevelt, Stalin. Tehran (1943), Yalta (1945), Potsdam (1945).
  • D-Day (June 1944); liberation of France; Soviet advance from East.
  • Atomic bombs (Hiroshima 6 Aug, Nagasaki 9 Aug 1945): Japan surrendered (15 Aug). WWII dead: approx. 70–85 million.

📗 AS Level Content

The Versailles Settlement: Fair or Carthaginian?

  • "Carthaginian peace" (Keynes, Economic Consequences of the Peace, 1919): Versailles was too harsh; destabilised Germany economically and politically; led to WWII.
  • Revisionist (e.g., Étienne Mantoux, A.J.P. Taylor): Versailles was not especially harsh compared to Brest-Litovsk or German treatment of Russia. Germany could have paid reparations; problem was political will, not economic capacity. Weimar politicians used "stab-in-the-back" myth to avoid responsibility.
  • Fair assessment: Versailles was harsh in parts (guilt clause, reparations) but left Germany as largest European state with industrial potential. Failure of enforcement (Locarno spirit, appeasement) was more important than treaty terms.

Why Did the League Fail?

  • Structural: no armed force; unanimity required for action; great powers dominated but were not committed (USA absent; USSR expelled 1939; Germany/Japan/Italy left).
  • Great power rivalry: Britain and France were primary enforcers but had conflicting interests (France wanted security; Britain wanted balance).
  • Economic depression: reduced willingness to act; prioritised domestic recovery over collective security.
  • Manchuria and Abyssinia: both showed that without great power commitment, League was powerless. Sanctions were half-hearted.

Appeasement: Causes and Assessment

  • Motives (see h8): guilt over Versailles, fear of war, military unpreparedness, belief in Hitler's limited aims, anti-communism, empire concerns.
  • Alternative policies: could Britain and France have stopped Hitler earlier? Rhineland (1936) was probably last chance for easy resistance. By 1938, Germany too strong.
  • Churchill's critique: Munich was dishonourable and encouraged Hitler. Counter: Chamberlain bought time for rearmament (though he hoped for peace, not merely time).

📙 A2 Level Content

The Thirty Years' War Thesis

  • Some historians (e.g., Ernst Nolte, controversially; more broadly, Arno Mayer) see 1914–45 as a continuous crisis of European civilisation. WWI and WWII were part of same conflict with intermission.
  • Evidence: unresolved issues from WWI (German grievances, border disputes, economic instability); same powers fought; ideological extremes (fascism, communism) emerged from WWI.
  • Critics: WWII was fundamentally different — racial genocide, total war, global scope. Treating as one war risks minimising distinct causes.

Comparing WWI and WWII

  • Causes: WWI — alliance system, militarism, nationalism, miscalculation. WWII — aggressive dictatorships, failure of Versailles, appeasement, economic crisis.
  • Nature of war: WWI — trench warfare, stalemate, millions dead for limited gains. WWII — mobile warfare (Blitzkrieg), air power, civilian bombing, genocide.
  • Peace settlements: Versailles (1919) blamed Germany; created resentment. 1945 settlements (Potsdam) divided Germany, acknowledged shared responsibility, but Cold War prevented lasting peace.

Historiography of Appeasement

  • 1930s–40s: appeasement condemned as cowardly and foolish (Churchill, Guilty Men, 1940).
  • 1960s–70s: revisionist historians (e.g., A.J.P. Taylor) argued appeasement was realistic given military and economic constraints. Taylor saw Hitler as opportunist, not planner.
  • 1980s–90s: post-revisionist (e.g., R.A.C. Parker): Chamberlain had some room for manoeuvre but chose appeasement due to misjudgment, not necessity.
  • Recent: emphasis on intelligence failures, overestimation of German strength, and ideological anti-communism as factors.

Essay Frameworks

  • "The Treaty of Versailles caused WWII": War guilt, reparations, territorial losses, created resentment. Counter: Germany could have recovered; enforcement failed; Hitler's aggression was primary cause.
  • "The League of Nations was doomed from the start": No USA, no armed force, unanimity rule, great power rivalry. Counter: succeeded in minor disputes; failed because powers did not support it, not because concept was flawed.
  • "Appeasement was the only realistic policy in the 1930s": Military weakness, public opinion, empire commitments, economic crisis. Counter: early resistance possible (Rhineland 1936); encouraged Hitler; moral failure.

Source Analysis Framework

  • Content: What does the source say? What information does it provide?
  • Provenance: Who wrote it? When? Why? What is their perspective?
  • Reliability: Is it trustworthy? What biases might exist?
  • Utility: How useful is it for a specific enquiry?

Essay Structure

  • Introduction: Define key terms, set out argument/line of reasoning
  • Paragraphs: Point → Evidence → Explanation → Link back to question
  • Synthesis: Compare factors, weigh importance, show interconnections
  • Conclusion: Judgment based on evidence, avoid introducing new points
Always answer the EXACT question asked. Underline key words ("To what extent", "How far", "Most important") and address them directly.
