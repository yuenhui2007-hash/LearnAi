IBH1: Causes of War

Overview

Rights and Protest (Prescribed Subject 1) examines two case studies: the US Civil Rights Movement (1954–1965) and Apartheid South Africa (1948–1964). Students must understand the causes, key events, role of leaders, methods of protest, and the role of international pressure in bringing about change.

Case Study A: US Civil Rights Movement (1954–1965)

Causes

  • Legacy of slavery and Jim Crow segregation laws.
  • WWII experiences — black soldiers fought for freedom abroad but faced discrimination at home.
  • Urbanisation and growth of black middle class.
  • NAACP legal strategy challenging segregation.

Key Events

  • Brown v. Board of Education (1954): Supreme Court ruled school segregation unconstitutional.
  • Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955–1956): Rosa Parks' arrest sparked year-long boycott; Supreme Court ruled bus segregation illegal.
  • Little Rock Nine (1957): Federal troops escorted black students into previously white high school.
  • Sit-ins (1960): Non-violent protests at segregated lunch counters.
  • Freedom Rides (1961): Integrated bus journeys through the South to challenge interstate segregation.
  • March on Washington (1963): 250,000 people; MLK delivered "I Have a Dream" speech.
  • Civil Rights Act (1964): Outlawed discrimination based on race, colour, religion, sex, or national origin.
  • Voting Rights Act (1965): Removed barriers to black voter registration.

Role of Leaders

  • Martin Luther King Jr.: Non-violent resistance, Christian ethos, mass mobilisation.
  • Malcolm X: Black Power, self-defence, black nationalism, later moderation before assassination.
  • Stokely Carmichael: Coined "Black Power," SNCC leader.

Methods of Protest

  • Non-violent direct action (boycotts, sit-ins, marches).
  • Legal challenges (NAACP).
  • Mass mobilisation and media exposure.

Case Study B: Apartheid South Africa (1948–1964)

Causes

  • Long history of racial discrimination and colonialism.
  • 1948 National Party victory — formalised apartheid policy.
  • Land Acts, Pass Laws, and Group Areas Act entrenched segregation.

Key Events

  • Defiance Campaign (1952): Mass civil disobedience against unjust laws.
  • Sharpeville Massacre (1960): Police killed 69 protesters; ANC and PAC banned.
  • Rivonia Trial (1963–1964): Mandela and others sentenced to life imprisonment.

Role of Leaders

  • Nelson Mandela: ANC leader, co-founder of MK (armed wing), imprisoned 1964–1990.
  • Albert Luthuli: ANC president, advocated non-violence, Nobel Peace Prize 1960.
  • Steve Biko: Black Consciousness Movement (post-1964, but influential).

Methods of Protest

  • Non-violent civil disobedience (pre-1960).
  • Armed struggle (MK post-1961).
  • International sanctions and boycotts.

Common Misconceptions

  • MLK vs. Malcolm X: They were not simply opposites — both sought racial equality, though their methods and ideologies differed.
  • Apartheid end date: This case study ends in 1964, but apartheid continued until 1994.
  • Non-violence effectiveness: Non-violence worked partly because it garnered media attention and white liberal support — context matters.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: Four source-based questions (comprehension, analysis, evaluation, synthesis) on either case study.
  • Students must use sources AND own knowledge.

November 2022: Role of leadership in the Montgomery Bus Boycott.

Practice Questions

  • Evaluate the significance of the Brown v. Board of Education decision in advancing civil rights.
  • Compare the methods of protest used in the US Civil Rights Movement and anti-apartheid South Africa.
  • To what extent was international pressure responsible for changes in South Africa by 1964?
  • Analyse the role of leadership in achieving civil rights in either the US or South Africa.

Last-Minute Summary

  • US Civil Rights: Brown (1954), Montgomery (1955), Little Rock (1957), Sit-ins (1960), Freedom Rides (1961), March on Washington (1963), Civil Rights Act (1964).
  • Leaders: MLK (non-violence), Malcolm X (Black Power/black nationalism).
  • South Africa: Defiance Campaign (1952), Sharpeville (1960), Rivonia Trial (1963–64).
  • Leaders: Mandela (ANC/MK), Luthuli (non-violence).
  • Paper 1: use sources + own knowledge; evaluate origin, purpose, value, limitation (OPVL).

Exam Tips

  • Read all sources carefully — annotate as you read.
  • For Q1 (comprehension), paraphrase — do not copy directly from sources.
  • For Q2 (analysis), identify explicit and implicit messages.
  • For Q3 (evaluation), use OPVL (Origin, Purpose, Value, Limitation).
  • For Q4 (synthesis), integrate sources with your own knowledge to build an argument.

IBH2: Authoritarian States

Overview

Authoritarian States (Prescribed Subject 2 / World History Topic 10) examines how authoritarian rulers rose to power, consolidated their regimes, and maintained control. Common case studies include Hitler's Germany, Stalin's USSR, Mao's China, and Castro's Cuba. This topic is examined in Paper 2 (essays).

1. Rise to Power

Conditions Favouring Authoritarian Rule

  • Political instability (weak democratic institutions, coalition governments).
  • Economic crisis (hyperinflation, unemployment, depression).
  • Social divisions (class conflict, ethnic tensions).
  • Post-war disillusionment or national humiliation (e.g., Treaty of Versailles).
  • Existing elite support or military backing.

Methods of Rise

  • Legal/constitutional: Hitler appointed Chancellor legally (1933).
  • Revolution: Castro's guerrilla war and overthrow of Batista (1959).
  • Coup d'état: military takeover.
  • Party consolidation: Stalin outmanoeuvred rivals after Lenin's death.

2. Consolidation & Maintenance of Power

Ideology

  • Fascism (Hitler, Mussolini): nationalism, racism, militarism, corporatism, rejection of democracy.
  • Communism (Stalin, Mao, Castro): Marxism-Leninism, class struggle, state ownership, one-party rule.

Propaganda

  • Control of media, education, arts, and culture.
  • Cult of personality (Stalin, Hitler, Mao).
  • Use of symbolism, rallies, and mass mobilisation.

Use of Force

  • Secret police (Gestapo, NKVD, Stasi).
  • Concentration camps, gulags, forced labour.
  • Show trials and purges (Moscow Trials 1936–38, Cultural Revolution).

Domestic Policies

  • Economic: Five-Year Plans (USSR), Great Leap Forward (China), autarky (Germany).
  • Social: control of women, youth organisations (Hitler Youth, Komsomol), education reform.
  • Racial/ideological: Holocaust, Ukrainian famine (Holodomor), Cultural Revolution.

Common Misconceptions

  • Totalitarian vs. authoritarian: Totalitarian regimes seek total control over all aspects of life; authoritarian regimes may allow some limited pluralism.
  • Hitler's rise: Hitler did not seize power by force — he was appointed and then exploited emergency powers.
  • Stalin's rise: Stalin was not Lenin's chosen successor — he outmanoeuvred Trotsky through bureaucratic skill.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 2: Essay questions on rise to power, consolidation, or domestic policies of authoritarian states.
  • Students must use specific evidence from at least two states.

Past Paper Trends (2019–2024)

November 2023: "Compare and contrast the role of ideology in the rise to power of two authoritarian rulers."
May 2023: "Evaluate the methods used by two authoritarian rulers to maintain power."
November 2022: "To what extent was economic crisis responsible for the rise of authoritarian states?"
May 2022: "Compare the domestic policies of two authoritarian states with respect to women and minorities."

Practice Questions

  • Compare and contrast the conditions that enabled Hitler and Stalin to rise to power.
  • Evaluate the importance of propaganda in maintaining authoritarian control.
  • To what extent did economic policies contribute to the stability of authoritarian regimes?
  • Analyse the treatment of minorities in two authoritarian states.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Rise to power: exploit weakness, use legal and illegal methods, gain elite/military support.
  • Consolidation: ideology, propaganda, force, control of economy and society.
  • Domestic policies: economic planning, social control, education, treatment of minorities.
  • Paper 2: compare two states; use specific evidence; address all parts of the question.

Exam Tips

  • Plan your essay for 5–10 minutes before writing.
  • Use specific dates, names, and events — vague generalisations lose marks.
  • For comparison essays, integrate both states within each paragraph.
  • Evaluate — do not just describe. Ask "To what extent?" and "How significant?"
  • Conclusion should directly answer the question with justification.

IBH4: Rights & Protest

Overview

Causes and Effects of 20th Century Wars (World History Topic 11) examines the origins, conduct, and consequences of major conflicts. Case studies typically include WWI, WWII, and the Spanish Civil War. Students analyse long-term and short-term causes, the nature of total war, and the social, economic, and political effects.

1. World War I (1914–1918)

Long-Term Causes

  • Militarism: arms races, especially Anglo-German naval rivalry.
  • Alliances: Triple Alliance vs. Triple Entente created rigid bloc system.
  • Imperialism: colonial competition increased tensions.
  • Nationalism: Slavic nationalism in Balkans; pan-Germanism; French revanchism.

Short-Term Causes

  • Assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand (28 June 1914).
  • Austro-Hungarian ultimatum to Serbia.
  • Blank cheque (Germany's support for Austria).
  • Schlieffen Plan and violation of Belgian neutrality.

Nature of War

  • Trench warfare on Western Front.
  • Technological developments: machine guns, artillery, poison gas, tanks, aircraft.
  • War of attrition — victory through wearing down enemy resources.
  • Total war: mobilisation of entire economy and society.

Effects

  • 10 million military deaths; 7 million civilian deaths.
  • Collapse of four empires: German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, Ottoman.
  • Treaty of Versailles: war guilt clause, reparations, territorial losses, military restrictions.
  • Economic disruption and rise of US as creditor nation.

2. Spanish Civil War (1936–1939)

Causes

  • Political instability: monarchy collapsed (1931), Second Republic faced opposition from left and right.
  • Social divisions: landless peasants, industrial workers, Catholic Church, army officers.
  • Regional nationalism (Catalonia, Basque Country).

Course

  • Military uprising led by Nationalists (Franco) against Republican government.
  • Foreign intervention: Germany and Italy supported Nationalists; USSR supported Republicans; International Brigades joined Republicans.
  • Guernica bombing (1937): symbol of fascist brutality.

Effects

  • Franco's authoritarian regime until 1975.
  • 350,000 deaths; repression of political opponents.
  • "Dress rehearsal" for WWII — testing of weapons and tactics.

3. World War II (1939–1945)

Causes

  • Failure of Treaty of Versailles and League of Nations.
  • Rise of fascism in Italy and Nazism in Germany.
  • Appeasement (Munich Agreement 1938).
  • German expansion: remilitarisation of Rhineland, Anschluss, invasion of Poland.

Nature of War

  • Blitzkrieg: rapid combined-arms warfare.
  • Total war: bombing of civilians (Dresden, Hiroshima, Nagasaki).
  • Holocaust: systematic genocide of 6 million Jews and others.
  • War in Pacific: island-hopping, atomic bombs (August 1945).

Effects

  • 50–85 million deaths.
  • Creation of United Nations (1945).
  • Bipolar world order: USA vs. USSR.
  • Decolonisation acceleration.
  • Nuremberg Trials: established international criminal justice.
  • Economic: Marshall Plan, European integration.

Common Misconceptions

  • WWI cause: Germany was not solely responsible — the alliance system and Austro-Hungarian aggression were equally significant.
  • Spanish Civil War: It was not simply fascism vs. democracy — the Republican side included anarchists, communists, and regional separatists with conflicting goals.
  • WWII start date: In Europe, 1939; in Asia, 1937 (Marco Polo Bridge Incident) or even 1931 (Manchuria).

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 2: Essay questions on causes, practices, and effects of 20th century wars.
  • Must compare at least two wars.

Past Paper Trends (2019–2024)

November 2023: "Compare and contrast the economic effects of two 20th century wars."
May 2023: "To what extent was the Treaty of Versailles responsible for the outbreak of WWII?"
November 2022: "Evaluate the impact of foreign intervention on the outcome of the Spanish Civil War."
May 2022: "Analyse the reasons for the development of total war in the 20th century."

Practice Questions

  • Evaluate the impact of total war on civilian populations in two wars.

Last-Minute Summary

  • WWI causes: militarism, alliances, imperialism, nationalism + assassination.
  • WWI nature: trench warfare, total war, technological slaughter.
  • Spanish Civil War: social/political divisions + foreign intervention = Franco victory.
  • WWII causes: Versailles failure, appeasement, fascist expansion.
  • WWII effects: UN, Cold War, decolonisation, Holocaust, Nuremberg.

Exam Tips

  • Plan your essay with clear causes/practices/effects structure.
  • Compare within paragraphs — do not write about one war then the other.
  • Use specific battles, treaties, and statistics.
  • Evaluate: "To what extent?" requires a weighted judgment.
  • Conclusion should directly answer the question.
