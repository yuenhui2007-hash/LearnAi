Coordination & Response

9700 (A-Level) · 0610 (IGCSE)

IGCSE Content

1

Nervous System

  • Central nervous system (CNS): Brain and spinal cord. Processes information and coordinates responses.
  • Peripheral nervous system (PNS): Nerves connecting CNS to body. Sensory (afferent) neurones carry impulses to CNS. Motor (efferent) neurones carry impulses from CNS to effectors.
  • Neurone structure: Cell body (contains nucleus and organelles), dendrites (receive impulses), axon (transmits impulses), myelin sheath (insulation, speeds conduction), Schwann cells (produce myelin), nodes of Ranvier (gaps between Schwann cells).
Sensory neurones have cell bodies in dorsal root ganglia. Motor neurones have cell bodies in CNS. Relay neurones (interneurones) are entirely within CNS.
2

Reflex Arc

Reflex arc: A rapid, automatic response to a stimulus without conscious thought. It provides rapid protection from harmful stimuli.
Pathway of a Reflex Arc
  1. Receptor detects stimulus (e.g., pain, heat).
  2. Sensory neurone transmits impulse to CNS.
  3. Relay neurone (interneurone) in CNS processes and passes impulse to motor neurone.
  4. Motor neurone transmits impulse to effector.
  5. Effector (muscle or gland) responds (e.g., muscle contracts).
Synapses in the reflex arc ensure one-way transmission and allow integration. The reflex arc bypasses the brain for speed, though the brain is informed simultaneously.
3

Synapse

Synapse: A junction between two neurones (or between a neurone and an effector) where impulses are transmitted chemically across a gap (synaptic cleft).
Events at a Cholinergic Synapse
  1. Electrical impulse (action potential) arrives at presynaptic membrane.
  2. Depolarisation opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels; Ca²⁺ enters presynaptic knob.
  3. Ca²⁺ causes synaptic vesicles containing acetylcholine (ACh) to fuse with presynaptic membrane.
  4. ACh is released by exocytosis into synaptic cleft.
  5. ACh diffuses across cleft and binds to receptors on postsynaptic membrane.
  6. Binding opens Na⁺ channels; Na⁺ enters, causing depolarisation → new action potential.
  7. ACh is broken down by acetylcholinesterase into acetate and choline (recycled into presynaptic knob).
Synaptic transmission is one-way because vesicles and neurotransmitter are only on the presynaptic side, and receptors are only on the postsynaptic side.
4

Hormones

  • Chemical messengers transported in blood from endocrine glands to target organs.
  • Slower but longer-lasting than nerve impulses.
  • Insulin: Secreted by pancreatic β cells. Lowers blood glucose (increases uptake by cells, increases glycogen storage in liver).
  • Glucagon: Secreted by pancreatic α cells. Raises blood glucose (breaks down glycogen to glucose in liver).
  • ADH: Secreted by posterior pituitary. Increases water reabsorption in kidney collecting ducts.
5

Plant Responses

  • Phototropism: Growth towards light. Auxin accumulates on shaded side → cell elongation → bending towards light.
  • Geotropism (gravitropism): Roots grow downwards (positive), shoots upwards (negative).

AS Level Content

6

Resting Potential & Action Potential

  • Resting potential: -70 mV. Maintained by Na⁺/K⁺ pump (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in) and K⁺ leak channels (K⁺ diffuses out, making inside negative).
  • Action potential:
    • Depolarisation: Stimulus opens voltage-gated Na⁺ channels; Na⁺ rushes in; potential rises to +40 mV.
    • Repolarisation: Na⁺ channels close; K⁺ channels open; K⁺ leaves; potential falls.
    • Hyperpolarisation: K⁺ channels slow to close; potential overshoots resting level.
    • Return to resting: Na⁺/K⁺ pump restores ion distribution.
  • All-or-nothing principle: Action potential either fires fully or not at all. Threshold stimulus required.
  • Refractory period: Brief period after action potential when neurone cannot fire again, ensuring one-way transmission and limiting frequency.
Worked Example: Explaining Resting Potential

Question: Explain how the resting potential of -70 mV is maintained in a neurone. [3 marks]

Answer:

  • The sodium-potassium pump actively transports 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in, creating an electrochemical gradient (1).
  • Potassium leak channels allow K⁺ to diffuse out of the axon down its concentration gradient (1).
  • More positive ions leave than enter, making the inside of the axon negative relative to the outside (-70 mV) (1).
7

Saltatory Conduction

  • Myelin sheath is an electrical insulator (made of Schwann cell membranes).
  • Action potential "jumps" between nodes of Ranvier (where voltage-gated channels are concentrated).
  • Faster and more energy-efficient than continuous conduction.
  • In demyelinating diseases (e.g., multiple sclerosis), saltatory conduction is impaired, slowing nerve impulses.
8

Neurotransmitters & Synapses

  • Excitatory synapses: Neurotransmitter (e.g., acetylcholine, glutamate) opens Na⁺ channels → depolarisation (EPSP).
  • Inhibitory synapses: Neurotransmitter (e.g., GABA, glycine) opens Cl⁻ channels or K⁺ channels → hyperpolarisation (IPSP), making action potential less likely.
  • Summation: Spatial (multiple inputs simultaneously) and temporal (rapid repeated inputs from one source) summation can trigger action potential.
9

The Eye & Photoreception

  • Retina: Contains rods (dim light, black/white, high sensitivity, low acuity) and cones (bright light, colour, low sensitivity, high acuity).
  • Fovea: Only cones; highest visual acuity.
  • Blind spot: Optic disc; no photoreceptors.
  • Pupil reflex: Bright light → circular muscles contract, radial relax → pupil constricts. Dim light → radial contract, circular relax → pupil dilates.
  • Accommodation: Ciliary muscles contract → suspensory ligaments slacken → lens thickens (near vision). Ciliary muscles relax → ligaments taut → lens flattens (distant vision).

A2 Level Content

10

Muscle Contraction

Sliding Filament Theory
  1. Action potential arrives at T-tubule → sarcoplasmic reticulum releases Ca²⁺.
  2. Ca²⁺ binds troponin → tropomyosin moves → reveals myosin-binding sites on actin.
  3. Myosin heads bind actin (cross-bridges), swivel using ATP, pull actin filaments toward M line.
  4. ATP detaches myosin; re-energised by ATP hydrolysis; cycle repeats if Ca²⁺ present.
  5. Sarcomere shortens; I band and H zone narrow; A band stays same width.
Muscle fatigue: accumulation of lactate, depletion of glycogen/ATP. During contraction, creatine phosphate provides rapid regeneration of ATP.
11

The Brain

  • Cerebral hemispheres: Conscious thought, language, memory, personality. Motor and sensory areas (contralateral control).
  • Cerebellum: Coordination, balance, posture.
  • Medulla oblongata: Cardiovascular and respiratory centres; reflexes (sneezing, coughing, vomiting).
  • Hypothalamus: Thermoregulation, osmoregulation, endocrine control (posterior pituitary), biological rhythms.
  • Pituitary gland: Master endocrine gland; anterior produces hormones (e.g., FSH, LH, growth hormone); posterior stores/releases ADH and oxytocin.
12

Plant Hormones in Detail

  • Auxins (IAA): Synthesised in shoot tip. Cell elongation by loosening cell walls (acid growth hypothesis: H⁺-ATPases acidify cell wall, activating expansins). Transported by polar transport (PIN proteins).
  • Gibberellins: Stem elongation, seed germination (breaks dormancy by stimulating amylase production in barley), fruit development.
  • Abscisic acid (ABA): Stress hormone. Stomatal closure, seed dormancy, inhibits growth.
  • Ethene (ethylene): Fruit ripening, leaf abscission.
  • Cytokinins: Cell division, delay leaf senescence, antagonise auxin in apical dominance.
🔬 Real-World Application: Neuropathies and Synaptic Drugs

Many drugs target synapses. Botulinum toxin (Botox) blocks ACh release, preventing muscle contraction. Organophosphates inhibit acetylcholinesterase, causing prolonged muscle contraction (used as insecticides but toxic to humans). Understanding synaptic transmission is essential for treating myasthenia gravis, Parkinson's disease, and depression.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Nervous system: CNS (brain, spinal cord) + PNS. Sensory → relay → motor neurones.
  • Reflex arc: receptor → sensory → relay → motor → effector. Synapses ensure one-way transmission.
  • Resting potential: -70 mV (Na⁺/K⁺ pump + K⁺ leak). Action potential: depolarisation (Na⁺ in) → repolarisation (K⁺ out) → hyperpolarisation → recovery.
  • Saltatory conduction: action potentials jump between nodes of Ranvier on myelinated axons.
  • Synapse: Ca²⁺ influx → vesicle fusion → neurotransmitter release → receptor binding → postsynaptic potential. Excitatory (Na⁺ in) or inhibitory (Cl⁻/K⁺ out).
  • Muscle: sliding filament theory; Ca²⁺ binds troponin → myosin-actin cross-bridges → ATP-powered contraction.
  • Hormones: insulin/glucagon (blood glucose), ADH (water reabsorption), plant auxins (phototropism/geotropism).
  • Brain areas: cerebrum (conscious), cerebellum (coordination), medulla (vital centres), hypothalamus (homeostasis), pituitary (master gland).

💡 Exam Tips

  • Draw action potential graph with correct phases and refractory period.
  • For muscle contraction, always mention Ca²⁺ release from sarcoplasmic reticulum and the role of troponin/tropomyosin.
  • In synapse questions, explain why transmission is one-way (vesicles only on presynaptic side, receptors only on postsynaptic).
  • Acetylcholine is the neurotransmitter at neuromuscular junctions.
  • Saltatory conduction is faster and more energy-efficient.
  • For accommodation: ciliary muscles contract for near vision (lens thicker); relax for distant vision (lens flatter).
