IGCSE A2 Level

Deformation of Solids

9702 (A-Level) · 0625 (IGCSE)

Detailed Notes

1. Stress and Strain

When a force is applied to a solid material, it causes deformation. To compare the behaviour of different materials fairly, we use stress and strain rather than simply force and extension. Stress normalises for cross-sectional area, and strain normalises for original length.

Stress: σ = F/A (unit: Pa or N/m²)
Strain: ε = ΔL/L (no units)
Young Modulus: E = σ/ε = (F/A)/(ΔL/L) = FL/(AΔL)

📖 Key Definitions

Stress (σ): Force per unit cross-sectional area. Unit: pascal (Pa).

Strain (ε): Extension per unit original length. No units (dimensionless).

Young Modulus (E): The ratio of stress to strain in the elastic region. Unit: pascal (Pa). It is a measure of the stiffness of a material.

The Young modulus is a material property — it does not depend on the dimensions of the sample. Steel has a high Young modulus (~200 GPa), meaning it is very stiff and requires a large stress to produce a small strain. Rubber has a low Young modulus, making it easy to stretch.

Strain has no units because it is a ratio of two lengths (ΔL/L). Stress has units of Pa (N/m²). The Young modulus has the same units as stress because strain is dimensionless.

2. Force-Extension and Stress-Strain Graphs

A typical stress-strain graph for a ductile metal (like copper or mild steel) shows several important regions:

  • Linear (elastic) region: Stress is proportional to strain. The material returns to its original length when the force is removed. Hooke's Law applies.
  • Limit of proportionality: The point beyond which stress is no longer proportional to strain. The graph stops being a straight line.
  • Elastic limit: The maximum stress beyond which the material will not return to its original length when the force is removed. Permanent deformation occurs.
  • Yield point: The stress at which plastic deformation begins rapidly. The material "gives" and extends with little increase in force.
  • Plastic region: The material undergoes permanent deformation. If the force is removed, the material does not return to its original length.
  • Ultimate tensile stress (UTS): The maximum stress the material can withstand before necking begins.
  • Breaking stress: The stress at which the material fractures.
For brittle materials (like glass or cast iron), there is little or no plastic deformation — the material fractures soon after the elastic limit is reached. For polymeric materials (like rubber), the stress-strain graph is highly non-linear and shows large elastic extensions.

3. Hooke's Law

Hooke's Law states that the extension of a spring or wire is directly proportional to the applied force, provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded.

F = kx

where k is the spring constant (unit: N/m) and x is the extension from the natural length. The spring constant depends on the material and dimensions of the spring — a thicker or shorter spring has a larger k.

📝 Worked Example — Spring Constant

Question: A spring extends by 4.0 cm when a load of 2.0 N is hung from it. Calculate: (a) the spring constant, (b) the extension when a 5.0 N load is applied.

Solution:

(a) k = F/x = 2.0 / 0.040 = 50 N/m

(b) x = F/k = 5.0 / 50 = 0.10 m = 10 cm

4. Elastic and Plastic Deformation

  • Elastic deformation: The material returns to its original shape and size when the deforming force is removed. Energy stored is fully recoverable. Atomic bonds are stretched but not broken.
  • Plastic deformation: The material does not return to its original shape; permanent deformation occurs. Layers of atoms slide over each other (dislocation movement in metals). Energy is dissipated as heat.
The area under a force-extension graph represents the work done in stretching the material. In the elastic region, this work is stored as elastic potential energy and is fully recoverable. In the plastic region, some of this energy is dissipated as internal energy (heat).

5. Strain Energy

The energy stored in a deformed material is equal to the area under the force-extension graph. For a material obeying Hooke's Law:

Strain energy = ½Fx = ½kx² = area under force-extension graph

For a wire stretched within the elastic limit, the strain energy per unit volume is:

Energy per unit volume = ½ × stress × strain = ½σε = ½Eε² = σ²/(2E)

📝 Worked Example — Strain Energy

Question: A steel wire of length 2.0 m and cross-sectional area 0.50 mm² is stretched by a force of 100 N. The Young modulus of steel is 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa. Calculate: (a) the extension, (b) the strain energy stored.

Solution:

(a) E = FL/(AΔL) → ΔL = FL/(AE)

ΔL = (100 × 2.0) / (0.50 × 10⁻⁶ × 2.0 × 10¹¹) = 200 / (1.0 × 10⁵) = 2.0 × 10⁻³ m (2.0 mm)

(b) Strain energy = ½FΔL = ½ × 100 × 2.0 × 10⁻³ = 0.10 J

6. Young Modulus Experiment

The Young modulus of a wire can be determined experimentally by measuring the extension produced by known loads:

  • Measure the original length L of the wire using a metre rule.
  • Measure the diameter d using a micrometer screw gauge (at several points, average).
  • Calculate cross-sectional area A = πd²/4.
  • Add known masses and measure the extension ΔL using a travelling microscope or Vernier scale.
  • Plot a graph of force F against extension ΔL.
  • The gradient = F/ΔL = EA/L → E = gradient × L/A
A control wire (identical but unloaded) is often used to compensate for thermal expansion and sag of the support. Measurements should be taken while loading and unloading to check for elastic behaviour.

⚠️ Common Misconception

Students often confuse the limit of proportionality with the elastic limit. The limit of proportionality is where the graph stops being a straight line (Hooke's Law no longer applies). The elastic limit is where permanent deformation begins. For many materials, these two points are very close together, but they are conceptually different.

📝 Worked Example — Young Modulus from Graph

Question: A stress-strain graph for a metal wire has a linear region with gradient 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa. The wire has length 1.5 m and diameter 0.40 mm. Calculate the force needed to produce an extension of 0.30 mm.

Solution:

Gradient = Young modulus E = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa

Strain ε = ΔL/L = 0.30 × 10⁻³ / 1.5 = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴

Stress σ = Eε = 2.0 × 10¹¹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 4.0 × 10⁷ Pa

Area A = πd²/4 = π × (0.40 × 10⁻³)² / 4 = 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ m²

F = σA = 4.0 × 10⁷ × 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ = 5.0 N

📝 Worked Example — Composite Wire

Question: A composite wire consists of a steel wire (length 1.0 m, diameter 0.50 mm, E = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa) and a copper wire (length 1.0 m, diameter 0.50 mm, E = 1.1 × 10¹¹ Pa) joined end-to-end. A tensile force of 80 N is applied. Calculate: (a) the total extension, (b) the strain energy stored.

Solution:

Area A = π × (0.50 × 10⁻³)² / 4 = 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ m²

For steel: ΔL_s = FL/(AE) = (80 × 1.0)/(1.96 × 10⁻⁷ × 2.0 × 10¹¹) = 2.04 × 10⁻³ m

For copper: ΔL_c = (80 × 1.0)/(1.96 × 10⁻⁷ × 1.1 × 10¹¹) = 3.71 × 10⁻³ m

(a) Total extension = 2.04 + 3.71 = 5.75 × 10⁻³ m (5.75 mm)

(b) Total strain energy = ½F(ΔL_s + ΔL_c) = ½ × 80 × 5.75 × 10⁻³ = 0.23 J

🎯 Exam-Style Question

Question: (a) Define Young modulus. (b) A wire of original length 2.5 m and cross-sectional area 0.80 mm² extends by 3.2 mm when a tensile force of 50 N is applied. Calculate the Young modulus of the material. (c) The wire is loaded beyond its elastic limit. Explain what is meant by the elastic limit. [5 marks]

Mark Scheme

• (a) Ratio of stress to strain / E = σ/ε / force per unit area divided by extension per unit length (1 mark)

• (b) σ = F/A = 50/(0.80 × 10⁻⁶) = 6.25 × 10⁷ Pa (1 mark)

• ε = ΔL/L = 3.2 × 10⁻³/2.5 = 1.28 × 10⁻³ (1 mark)

• E = σ/ε = 6.25 × 10⁷/1.28 × 10⁻³ = 4.9 × 10¹⁰ Pa (accept 4.88 × 10¹⁰ Pa) (1 mark)

• (c) The maximum stress beyond which the material does not return to its original length / permanent deformation occurs (1 mark)

🎯 Exam-Style Question

Question: A metal wire of length 1.8 m is stretched elastically. The force-extension graph is a straight line passing through the origin. When the force is 40 N, the extension is 2.0 mm. Calculate: (a) the spring constant of the wire, (b) the strain energy stored at this extension, (c) the energy stored if the wire is stretched to twice this extension (assuming Hooke's Law still applies). [4 marks]

Mark Scheme

• (a) k = F/x = 40/(2.0 × 10⁻³) = 2.0 × 10⁴ N/m (1 mark)

• (b) E = ½Fx = ½ × 40 × 2.0 × 10⁻³ = 0.040 J (1 mark)

• (c) At 2x extension, E = ½k(2x)² = 4 × (½kx²) = 4 × 0.040 = 0.16 J (2 marks — 1 for method, 1 for answer)

Accept: strain energy is proportional to x², so doubling x quadruples energy.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Stress σ = F/A (Pa); Strain ε = ΔL/L (no units); Young Modulus E = σ/ε (Pa)
  • Hooke's Law: F = kx (up to limit of proportionality)
  • Limit of proportionality → Hooke's law stops (graph becomes non-linear)
  • Elastic limit → permanent deformation begins
  • Elastic: returns to shape; Plastic: permanent deformation
  • Strain energy = ½Fx = ½kx² = area under force-extension graph
  • Energy per unit volume = ½σε = σ²/(2E)
  • Young modulus experiment: measure L, d, F, ΔL; plot F vs ΔL; E = gradient × L/A

Exam Tips

  • Always convert mm² to m² when calculating area: 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m².
  • When using a micrometer, remember to check for zero error and apply correction.
  • Strain is dimensionless — never give it units.
  • On a stress-strain graph, the gradient in the linear region equals the Young modulus.
  • Distinguish carefully between limit of proportionality and elastic limit.
  • For strain energy calculations, use extension (not total length).
  • When a wire is stretched beyond the elastic limit, some energy is dissipated as heat — the unloading curve lies below the loading curve.
