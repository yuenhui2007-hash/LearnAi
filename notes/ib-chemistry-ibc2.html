Atomic Structure

IB DP Chemistry HL/SL — Topic 2

Overview

Atomic Structure (Topic 2) explores the composition of atoms, isotopes, and the arrangement of electrons. It provides the foundation for understanding periodicity, bonding, and chemical reactivity. This topic is heavily tested in Paper 1 and Paper 2, and connects directly to the IA through spectroscopy experiments.

2.1 Subatomic Particles

  • Proton: charge +1, mass ≈ 1 u, found in nucleus.
  • Neutron: charge 0, mass ≈ 1 u, found in nucleus.
  • Electron: charge −1, mass ≈ 1/1836 u, found in orbitals.
Atomic number (Z) = number of protons
Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons
Number of electrons = number of protons (in a neutral atom)
Isotopes: same Z, different A (different neutron number)

2.2 Mass Spectrometry

Mass spectrometry determines relative atomic mass and isotopic abundance.

  • Ionisation: sample is bombarded with high-energy electrons.
  • Acceleration: ions are accelerated by an electric field.
  • Deflection: ions are deflected by a magnetic field — lighter ions deflect more.
  • Detection: ions strike a detector and produce a mass spectrum.
Relative atomic mass (A_r) = Σ(isotope mass × fractional abundance)

Worked Example 1: Relative Atomic Mass

Question: Chlorine has two isotopes: ³⁵Cl (75.8%) and ³⁷Cl (24.2%). Calculate A_r.

Solution:

A_r = (35 × 0.758) + (37 × 0.242) = 26.53 + 8.95 = 35.48

2.3 Electron Configuration

Electrons occupy energy levels (shells), sublevels (s, p, d, f), and orbitals.

s: 1 orbital, 2 electrons
p: 3 orbitals, 6 electrons
d: 5 orbitals, 10 electrons
f: 7 orbitals, 14 electrons

Maximum electrons per shell n: 2n²

Aufbau Principle

Electrons fill orbitals from lowest to highest energy: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p...

Special Cases

  • Chromium (Cr): [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ (not 3d⁴ 4s²) — half-filled d subshell is more stable.
  • Copper (Cu): [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ (not 3d⁹ 4s²) — full d subshell is more stable.

HL only

HL students write electron configurations using the full s, p, d, f notation and explain anomalies using stability arguments.

2.4 Ionisation Energy

First ionisation energy (IE₁): energy required to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms.

X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻

Factors Affecting Ionisation Energy

  • Nuclear charge: higher Z → stronger attraction → higher IE.
  • Atomic radius: larger radius → weaker attraction → lower IE.
  • Electron shielding: more inner electrons → more shielding → lower IE.

Trends in Successive Ionisation Energies

Large jumps in successive IE values indicate a change in electron shell. For example, Na shows a large jump after the 1st IE (removing a core electron).

2.5 Emission Spectra

  • Electrons emit photons when falling from higher to lower energy levels.
  • Each element has a unique emission spectrum (fingerprint).
ΔE = hf = hc/λ
where h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹

Common Misconceptions

  • Mass number: It is not the exact mass of an atom — it is the total number of nucleons.
  • Isotopes: Isotopes have the same chemical properties (same electron configuration) but different physical properties (different mass).
  • Electron shells: Electrons do not orbit like planets — they exist in probability clouds (orbitals).
  • Ionisation energy: Always refers to gaseous atoms. Removing an electron from a solid or liquid requires different energy.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: 3–4 MCQs on subatomic particles, electron configuration, and ionisation energy trends.
  • Paper 2: Structured questions on mass spectra, electron configuration of ions, and ionisation energy data.
  • Paper 3: Spectroscopy experiments and data analysis.
  • IA: Flame tests, spectroscopy, and atomic emission experiments are common.

Practice Questions

  • Calculate the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in ⁵⁶Fe³⁺.
  • Boron has isotopes of mass 10 and 11. If A_r = 10.8, calculate the percentage abundance of each isotope.
  • Write the electron configuration of V²⁺.
  • Explain why the first ionisation energy of aluminium is lower than that of magnesium.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Protons determine the element; neutrons determine the isotope.
  • A_r = Σ(isotope mass × abundance).
  • Electron configuration follows Aufbau principle: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d...
  • Cr and Cu have anomalous configurations due to stability of half-filled/full d subshells.
  • IE increases across a period, decreases down a group.
  • Large jumps in successive IE indicate change in shell.

Exam Tips

  • Read each question carefully and identify the command term before answering.
  • Show all working with units — method marks are generous in IB Chemistry.
  • Give final answers to 2–3 significant figures unless specified otherwise.
  • In "explain" questions, always mention nuclear charge, atomic radius, and shielding.
  • When writing electron configurations, use superscripts correctly (e.g., 3d⁵ not 3d5).
