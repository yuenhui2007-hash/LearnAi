Types of Bonding — Comprehensive Comparison

Chemical bonding holds atoms together in compounds. The type of bonding depends on the electronegativity difference between the elements involved and determines the physical and chemical properties of the substance. Understanding bonding is essential for predicting melting points, solubility, conductivity, and reactivity.

TypeBetweenMechanismMelting PointElectrical ConductivitySolubility in Water
IonicMetal + Non-metalElectrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ionsHighOnly molten or aqueousUsually soluble
Covalent (simple molecular)Non-metal + Non-metalShared pair of electronsLowNon-conductingUsually insoluble
Covalent (giant covalent)Non-metal + Non-metalShared electrons in networkVery highUsually non-conducting (except graphite)Insoluble
MetallicMetal + MetalElectrostatic attraction between positive ions and delocalised electronsHighConductingInsoluble (react instead)
📘 Key Definitions

Ionic bond: Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions formed by electron transfer.

Covalent bond: A shared pair of electrons between two atoms.

Coordinate (dative) bond: A covalent bond where both electrons come from the same atom.

Metallic bond: Electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and a sea of delocalised electrons.

Electronegativity: The ability of an atom to attract the bonding electrons in a covalent bond. Values range from 0.7 (Fr) to 4.0 (F).

Ionic Bonding & Lattice Energy

Ionic compounds form giant ionic lattices. Each ion is surrounded by oppositely charged ions in a regular three-dimensional arrangement. The strong electrostatic forces act in all directions, giving ionic compounds high melting points and hardness.

Formation of Ionic Compounds — Born-Haber Cycle Context
  1. Atomisation of metal: Na(s) → Na(g) (endothermic)
  2. Atomisation of non-metal: ½Cl₂(g) → Cl(g) (endothermic)
  3. Ionisation of metal: Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻ (endothermic, IE)
  4. Electron affinity of non-metal: Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g) (exothermic)
  5. Lattice formation: Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g) → NaCl(s) (highly exothermic)
Lattice energy (ΔH°latt) is the enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic compound is formed from its gaseous ions.

Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g) → NaCl(s)     ΔH°latt = −787 kJ mol⁻¹
Lattice energy depends on: • Ionic charge: higher charge → stronger attraction → more exothermic lattice energy • Ionic radius: smaller ions → ions closer together → stronger attraction → more exothermic lattice energy Example: MgO (2+, 2−) has a much more exothermic lattice energy than NaCl (1+, 1−).
Worked Example: Predicting Lattice Energy Trends

Arrange the following in order of increasingly negative lattice energy: NaCl, MgO, NaBr, MgCl₂.

Solution:

• Charge effect: MgO (2+,2−) > MgCl₂ (2+,1−) > NaCl/NaBr (1+,1−)

• Size effect: NaCl > NaBr (Cl⁻ smaller than Br⁻)

Order: NaBr < NaCl < MgCl₂ < MgO

Worked Example: Drawing Dot-and-Cross Diagrams

MgCl₂:

Mg (2.8.2) loses 2 electrons → Mg²⁺ (2.8)

Each Cl (2.8.7) gains 1 electron → Cl⁻ (2.8.8)

Formula: MgCl₂ (one Mg²⁺ balances two Cl⁻)

Ionic compounds conduct electricity ONLY when molten or dissolved in water because the ions are free to move. In the solid state, ions are fixed in the lattice and cannot move, so solid ionic compounds are electrical insulators.
Worked Example: Explaining Properties

Explain why sodium chloride has a high melting point.

Solution: NaCl has a giant ionic lattice structure with strong electrostatic attractions between Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. These forces act in all directions throughout the lattice. A large amount of energy is required to overcome these strong forces and separate the ions, resulting in a high melting point (801°C).

Covalent Bonding & Shapes

Covalent bonds form when atoms share electron pairs to achieve noble gas configurations. The shapes of molecules can be predicted using VSEPR (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion) theory, which states that electron pairs around a central atom arrange themselves to minimise repulsion.

📘 Key Definitions

Bond pair: A shared pair of electrons between two atoms.

Lone pair: A non-bonding pair of electrons on one atom.

VSEPR: Electron pairs around a central atom arrange themselves to minimise repulsion. Order of repulsion: lone pair-lone pair > lone pair-bond pair > bond pair-bond pair.

Bond angle: The angle between two adjacent bonds in a molecule.

Worked Example: Predicting Shape — NH₃

Predict the shape and bond angle of NH₃.

Solution:

1. N has 5 valence electrons.

2. Three bond pairs (N-H) and one lone pair = 4 electron pairs total.

3. Electron pair geometry = tetrahedral; molecular shape = trigonal pyramidal.

4. Bond angle ≈ 107° (less than 109.5° because lone pair repels more than bond pairs).

Worked Example: CO₂ vs SO₂

CO₂: C has 4 valence electrons, forms 2 double bonds = 2 regions of electron density → linear, 180°

SO₂: S has 6 valence electrons, forms 2 double bonds + 1 lone pair = 3 regions of electron density → bent, ~119°

Sigma (σ) and Pi (π) Bonds

A single covalent bond consists of one sigma (σ) bond formed by head-on overlap of orbitals. A double bond consists of one σ bond and one pi (π) bond formed by sideways overlap of p orbitals. A triple bond consists of one σ and two π bonds.

Bonding in Ethene (C₂H₄):

Each C is sp² hybridised with trigonal planar geometry (120°).
• C-H bonds: σ bonds from sp² hybrid orbitals overlapping with H 1s orbitals.
• C=C bond: one σ bond from sp²-sp² overlap + one π bond from sideways p orbital overlap.
• The π bond prevents rotation about the C=C axis, leading to cis-trans isomerism.
Worked Example: Counting σ and π Bonds

How many σ and π bonds are in propene (C₃H₆)?

Solution:

Structure: CH₂=CH-CH₃

σ bonds: 8 (6 C-H + 2 C-C)

π bonds: 1 (in C=C double bond)

⚠️ Common Misconceptions

• Double bonds count as ONE region of electron density for VSEPR, not two.

• Always count ALL electron pairs (bonding + lone), not just bonds.

• Lone pairs repel more than bond pairs, so they compress bond angles.

• A molecule can have polar bonds but be non-polar overall if symmetrical (e.g., CCl₄, CO₂).

• σ bonds are stronger than π bonds individually, but a double bond (σ+π) is stronger than a single bond.

Polarity

A bond is polar if there is an electronegativity difference (ΔEN) between the atoms. The more electronegative atom attracts electrons, becoming δ−; the other becomes δ+.

Molecules with polar bonds AND an asymmetrical shape are polar overall (e.g., H₂O, NH₃). Symmetrical molecules with polar bonds are non-polar overall (e.g., CO₂, CCl₄, BF₃).

Metallic Bonding & Properties

Metals consist of a lattice of positive ions surrounded by a 'sea' of delocalised valence electrons. This model explains the characteristic properties of metals.

Metallic Bonding Model
  1. Metal atoms lose their valence electrons to form positive ions (cations).
  2. These electrons become delocalised and move freely throughout the structure.
  3. Strong electrostatic attraction exists between the positive ions and the negative sea of electrons.

Properties Explained by Metallic Bonding

PropertyExplanation
High electrical conductivityDelocalised electrons are mobile and can carry charge
Malleability and ductilityLayers of ions can slide without breaking bonds because the electron sea maintains attraction
Lustre (shiny appearance)Free electrons reflect light
High melting pointsStrong metallic bonds require much energy to break
High thermal conductivityDelocalised electrons transfer kinetic energy quickly
Worked Example: Explaining Properties

Explain why sodium is malleable but sodium chloride is brittle.

Solution: In sodium, layers of positive ions can slide over each other because the delocalised electrons maintain the metallic bond in new positions. In NaCl, sliding brings like charges (Na⁺-Na⁺ or Cl⁻-Cl⁻) together, causing strong repulsion and fracture of the crystal.

Alloys (e.g., brass = Cu + Zn) are often harder than pure metals because different-sized atoms disrupt the regular lattice, making it harder for layers to slide past each other.

Intermolecular Forces in Depth

Intermolecular forces (IMFs) are attractions between molecules. They are much weaker than covalent, ionic, or metallic bonds but determine physical properties like boiling point, melting point, viscosity, and solubility.

ForceRelative StrengthBetweenExample
London dispersion (van der Waals)Weakest (~0.05–40 kJ mol⁻¹)All moleculesCH₄, noble gases, all molecules
Dipole-dipoleModerate (~5–25 kJ mol⁻¹)Polar moleculesHCl, propanone, aldehydes
Hydrogen bondingStrongest IMF (~10–40 kJ mol⁻¹)H bonded to N, O, or FH₂O, NH₃, HF, alcohols, carboxylic acids
📘 Key Definitions

Van der Waals forces: Temporary dipoles induced by fluctuating electron clouds. Strength increases with number of electrons / molecular size / surface area.

Dipole-dipole forces: Permanent attractions between δ+ and δ− ends of polar molecules.

Hydrogen bond: Strong dipole-dipole attraction between H (δ+) bonded to N, O, or F and a lone pair on another N, O, or F atom.

Hydrogen Bonding — Special Cases

Water: Each water molecule can form up to 4 hydrogen bonds (2 through H atoms, 2 through lone pairs on O). This explains:

  • High boiling point (100°C) compared to H₂S (-60°C)
  • Ice is less dense than water — hydrogen bonds hold molecules in an open hexagonal lattice with empty space
  • High surface tension and specific heat capacity
  • Universal solvent properties

DNA: Hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs (A-T: 2 H-bonds; G-C: 3 H-bonds) hold the double helix together. The specificity of pairing ensures accurate replication.

Worked Example: Boiling Points

Explain why ethanol (C₂H₅OH, bp 78°C) has a higher boiling point than ethane (C₂H₆, bp -89°C) and methoxymethane (CH₃OCH₃, bp -24°C).

Solution:

• Ethanol has hydrogen bonding between the -OH groups (H bonded to O), which is much stronger than the van der Waals forces in ethane.

• Methoxymethane has the same molecular formula as ethanol (C₂H₆O) but no H bonded directly to O, so it cannot hydrogen bond — only dipole-dipole and van der Waals.

• Ethanol > methoxymethane > ethane in boiling point.

When comparing boiling points, follow this hierarchy: 1. Check for hydrogen bonding first (strongest IMF) 2. If both H-bond, compare number of H-bonds per molecule 3. If no H-bond, compare molecular size / number of electrons (van der Waals) 4. Consider polarity for dipole-dipole interactions 5. Branching reduces surface area → weaker van der Waals → lower bp
⚠️ Common Misconceptions

• Hydrogen bonds are NOT covalent bonds — they are intermolecular forces, typically 10–40 kJ mol⁻¹ compared to ~400 kJ mol⁻¹ for O-H covalent bonds.

• Only H bonded directly to N, O, or F can hydrogen bond. CH₃OH can; CH₃OCH₃ cannot (no H on O).

• Intramolecular hydrogen bonds (within a molecule) exist in some cases like proteins and DNA, but most H-bonds in introductory chemistry are intermolecular.

• London dispersion forces exist in ALL molecules, including polar and hydrogen-bonded ones. They are the ONLY force in non-polar molecules.

Exam-Style Questions

📝 Exam Question 1

(a) State and explain the shape of a beryllium chloride (BeCl₂) molecule. [2]

(b) State and explain the shape of a water (H₂O) molecule. [3]

(c) Explain why SiO₂ has a very high melting point while CO₂ is a gas at room temperature. [3]

Mark Scheme:
(a) Linear (1). Two bonding pairs / two regions of electron density (1) which arrange to minimise repulsion / 180° apart (1).
(b) Bent / V-shaped / non-linear (1). Two bonding pairs and two lone pairs / four regions of electron density (1). Lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs, reducing angle from 109.5° to 104.5° (1).
(c) SiO₂ is giant covalent / macromolecular (1) with many strong covalent bonds (1). CO₂ is simple molecular (1) with weak intermolecular forces / van der Waals forces between molecules (1). Max 3.
📝 Exam Question 2

Explain why ice is less dense than liquid water. [3]

Mark Scheme:
In ice, water molecules are held in a rigid lattice / open structure by hydrogen bonds (1). Each water molecule forms four hydrogen bonds (1). When ice melts, some hydrogen bonds break and the molecules can pack closer together (1), so liquid water has a higher density than ice.
📝 Exam Question 3

Arrange the following in order of increasing lattice energy (least negative to most negative): NaCl, MgO, KBr, CaO. Explain your reasoning. [4]

Mark Scheme:
Order: KBr < NaCl < CaO < MgO (1)
Lattice energy depends on ionic charge and ionic radius (1).
Higher charge → more exothermic lattice energy: MgO/CaO (2+,2−) > NaCl/KBr (1+,1−) (1).
Smaller ions → more exothermic: Mg²⁺ < Ca²⁺ and O²⁻ < Cl⁻ < Br⁻ (1).

📝 Quick Summary

  • Ionic: electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions; giant lattice; high mp; conduct when molten/aqueous
  • Covalent: shared electrons; simple molecular = low mp; giant covalent = very high mp (e.g., diamond, SiO₂)
  • Metallic: cations + delocalised electrons; conducting; malleable; high mp
  • VSEPR: electron pairs minimise repulsion; lone pairs repel more than bond pairs
  • 2 pairs = linear (180°); 3 = trigonal planar (120°); 4 = tetrahedral (109.5°); 3 bonds + 1 lone = trigonal pyramidal (~107°); 2 bonds + 2 lone = bent (~104.5°)
  • σ bonds: head-on orbital overlap; π bonds: sideways p-orbital overlap
  • IMFs: van der Waals < dipole-dipole < hydrogen bonding
  • H-bonding only when H is bonded directly to N, O, or F
  • Lattice energy ∝ (charge⁺ × charge⁻) / (r⁺ + r⁻)

💡 Exam Tips

  • Always count ALL electron pairs for VSEPR (bonding + lone)
  • When comparing melting points: giant structures > H-bonded > polar > non-polar
  • Graphite conducts (delocalised electrons between layers); diamond doesn't conduct
  • Dipole moment direction: arrow points toward the more electronegative (δ−) end
  • Symmetrical molecules with polar bonds are non-polar overall — always check shape!
  • In lattice energy calculations, remember charge has a bigger effect than size
