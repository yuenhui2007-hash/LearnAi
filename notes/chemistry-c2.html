Types of Bonding

Chemical bonding holds atoms together in compounds. The type of bonding depends on the elements involved and determines the physical and chemical properties of the substance.

TypeBetweenMechanismMelting PointElectrical Conductivity
IonicMetal + Non-metalElectrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ionsHighOnly molten or aqueous
Covalent (simple molecular)Non-metal + Non-metalShared pair of electronsLowNon-conducting
Covalent (giant covalent)Non-metal + Non-metalShared electrons in networkVery highUsually non-conducting (except graphite)
MetallicMetal + MetalElectrostatic attraction between positive ions and delocalised electronsHighConducting
📘 Key Definitions

Ionic bond: Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions formed by electron transfer.

Covalent bond: A shared pair of electrons between two atoms.

Coordinate (dative) bond: A covalent bond where both electrons come from the same atom.

Metallic bond: Electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and a sea of delocalised electrons.

Ionic Bonding in Detail

Ionic compounds form giant ionic lattices. Each ion is surrounded by oppositely charged ions. The strong electrostatic forces act in all directions, giving ionic compounds high melting points.

Worked Example: Drawing Dot-and-Cross Diagrams

MgCl₂:

Mg (2.8.2) loses 2 electrons → Mg²⁺ (2.8)

Each Cl (2.8.7) gains 1 electron → Cl⁻ (2.8.8)

Formula: MgCl₂ (one Mg²⁺ balances two Cl⁻)

Ionic compounds conduct electricity ONLY when molten or dissolved in water because the ions are free to move. In the solid state, ions are fixed in the lattice and cannot move.

Metallic Bonding in Detail

Metals consist of a lattice of positive ions surrounded by a 'sea' of delocalised valence electrons. This explains:

  • High electrical conductivity — electrons are mobile
  • Malleability and ductility — layers of ions can slide without breaking bonds
  • Lustre — free electrons reflect light
  • High melting points — strong metallic bonds
Worked Example: Explaining Properties

Explain why sodium is malleable but sodium chloride is brittle.

Solution: In sodium, layers of positive ions can slide over each other because the delocalised electrons maintain the metallic bond in new positions. In NaCl, sliding brings like charges together, causing repulsion and fracture.

Covalent Bonding & Shapes

Covalent bonds form when atoms share electron pairs to achieve noble gas configurations. The shapes of molecules can be predicted using VSEPR (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion) theory.

📘 Key Definitions

Bond pair: A shared pair of electrons between two atoms.

Lone pair: A non-bonding pair of electrons on one atom.

VSEPR: Electron pairs around a central atom arrange themselves to minimise repulsion. Order of repulsion: lone pair-lone pair > lone pair-bond pair > bond pair-bond pair.

Electron Pairs (bonding + lone)ShapeBond AngleExample
2Linear180°BeCl₂, CO₂
3 (0 lone)Trigonal planar120°BF₃, AlCl₃
4 (0 lone)Tetrahedral109.5°CH₄, CCl₄
4 (1 lone)Trigonal pyramidal107°NH₃
4 (2 lone)Bent / V-shaped104.5°H₂O
5 (0 lone)Trigonal bipyramidal90°, 120°PCl₅
6 (0 lone)Octahedral90°SF₆
Worked Example: Predicting Shape

Predict the shape and bond angle of NH₃.

Solution: N has 5 valence electrons. Three bond pairs (N-H) and one lone pair = 4 electron pairs total. Shape is trigonal pyramidal. Bond angle is approximately 107° (less than 109.5° because lone pair repels more than bond pairs).

Worked Example: CO₂ vs SO₂

CO₂: C has 4 valence electrons, forms 2 double bonds = 2 electron pairs → linear, 180°

SO₂: S has 6 valence electrons, forms 2 double bonds + 1 lone pair = 3 electron pairs → bent, ~120°

⚠️ Common Misconceptions

• Double bonds count as ONE region of electron density for VSEPR, not two.

• Always count ALL electron pairs (bonding + lone), not just bonds.

• Lone pairs repel more than bond pairs, so they compress bond angles.

• A molecule can have polar bonds but be non-polar overall if symmetrical (e.g., CCl₄).

Polarity

A bond is polar if there is an electronegativity difference between the atoms. The more electronegative atom attracts electrons, becoming δ−; the other becomes δ+.

Molecules with polar bonds AND an asymmetrical shape are polar overall (e.g., H₂O, NH₃). Symmetrical molecules with polar bonds are non-polar overall (e.g., CO₂, CCl₄).

Intermolecular Forces

Intermolecular forces (IMFs) are attractions between molecules. They are much weaker than covalent, ionic, or metallic bonds but determine physical properties like boiling point and solubility.

ForceRelative StrengthBetweenExample
London dispersion (van der Waals)WeakestAll moleculesCH₄, noble gases
Dipole-dipoleModeratePolar moleculesHCl, propanone
Hydrogen bondingStrongest IMFH bonded to N, O, or FH₂O, NH₃, HF, alcohols
📘 Key Definitions

Van der Waals forces: Temporary dipoles induced by fluctuating electron clouds. Strength increases with number of electrons / molecular size.

Dipole-dipole forces: Permanent attractions between δ+ and δ− ends of polar molecules.

Hydrogen bond: Strong dipole-dipole attraction between H (δ+) bonded to N, O, or F and a lone pair on another N, O, or F.

Hydrogen Bonding — Special Cases

Water: Each water molecule can form up to 4 hydrogen bonds. This explains:

  • High boiling point (100°C) compared to H₂S (-60°C)
  • Ice is less dense than water — hydrogen bonds hold molecules in an open hexagonal lattice
  • High surface tension and specific heat capacity

DNA: Hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs (A-T: 2 H-bonds; G-C: 3 H-bonds) hold the double helix together.

Worked Example: Boiling Points

Explain why ethanol (C₂H₅OH, bp 78°C) has a higher boiling point than ethane (C₂H₆, bp -89°C).

Solution: Both have similar numbers of electrons so similar van der Waals forces. However, ethanol has hydrogen bonding between the -OH groups (H bonded to O), which is much stronger than the van der Waals forces in ethane. More energy is needed to overcome hydrogen bonds.

When comparing boiling points: (1) Check for hydrogen bonding first; (2) If both H-bond, compare number of H-bonds; (3) If no H-bond, compare molecular size / number of electrons (van der Waals); (4) Consider polarity for dipole-dipole.
⚠️ Common Misconceptions

• Hydrogen bonds are NOT covalent bonds — they are intermolecular forces.

• Only H bonded directly to N, O, or F can hydrogen bond. CH₃OH can; CH₃OCH₃ cannot (no H on O).

• Intramolecular hydrogen bonds (within a molecule) exist in some cases like proteins, but most H-bonds in exams are intermolecular.

Exam-Style Questions

📝 Exam Question 1

(a) State and explain the shape of a beryllium chloride (BeCl₂) molecule. [2]

(b) State and explain the shape of a water (H₂O) molecule. [3]

Mark Scheme:
(a) Linear (1). Two bonding pairs / two regions of electron density (1) which arrange to minimise repulsion.
(b) Bent / V-shaped / non-linear (1). Two bonding pairs and two lone pairs / four regions of electron density (1). Lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs, reducing angle from 109.5° to 104.5° (1).
📝 Exam Question 2

Explain why ice is less dense than liquid water. [3]

Mark Scheme:
In ice, water molecules are held in a rigid lattice / open structure by hydrogen bonds (1). Each water molecule forms four hydrogen bonds (1). When ice melts, some hydrogen bonds break and the molecules can pack closer together (1), so liquid water has a higher density.

📝 Quick Summary

  • Ionic: electrostatic attraction; high mp; conduct when molten/aqueous
  • Covalent: shared electrons; simple molecular = low mp; giant covalent = very high mp
  • Metallic: cations + delocalised electrons; conducting; malleable
  • VSEPR: electron pairs minimise repulsion; lone pairs repel more than bond pairs
  • 2 pairs = linear (180°); 3 = trigonal planar (120°); 4 = tetrahedral (109.5°)
  • 3 bonds + 1 lone = trigonal pyramidal (~107°); 2 bonds + 2 lone = bent (~104.5°)
  • IMFs: van der Waals < dipole-dipole < hydrogen bonding
  • H-bonding only when H is bonded to N, O, or F

💡 Exam Tips

  • Always count ALL electron pairs for VSEPR (bonding + lone)
  • When comparing melting points: giant structures > H-bonded > polar > non-polar
  • Graphite conducts (delocalised electrons between layers); diamond doesn't
  • Dipole moment direction: arrow points toward negative (δ−) end
  • Symmetrical molecules with polar bonds are non-polar overall
