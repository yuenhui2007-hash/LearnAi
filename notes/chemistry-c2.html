C2: Chemical Bonding

9701 (A-Level) · 0620 (IGCSE)

IGCSE Content

Ionic Bonding

Ionic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. It typically occurs between metals and non-metals. Metals lose electrons to form cations (+); non-metals gain electrons to form anions (-).

Dot-and-cross diagrams: Show outer shell electrons only. Electrons from one atom are shown as dots, from the other as crosses.

Examples: NaCl, MgCl₂, CaO, Na₂O

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻
Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl (ionic lattice)

Covalent Bonding

Covalent bonding involves the sharing of electron pairs between atoms. It typically occurs between non-metal atoms. A single covalent bond = 1 shared pair; double bond = 2 shared pairs; triple bond = 3 shared pairs.

Examples: H₂ (single), O₂ (double), N₂ (triple), CO₂ (double), H₂O, CH₄, NH₃

Coordinate (dative) covalent bond: Both electrons in the shared pair come from the same atom. Examples: NH₄⁺, H₃O⁺, Al₂Cl₆

Metallic Bonding

Metallic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and a "sea" of delocalised electrons. This explains why metals:

  • Conduct electricity (delocalised electrons are mobile)
  • Are malleable and ductile (ions can slide past each other)
  • Have high melting points (strong electrostatic forces)

Allotropes of Carbon

Diamond: Each carbon bonded to 4 others in tetrahedral arrangement. Very hard, high mp, non-conducting.

Graphite: Layers of hexagonal rings. Each carbon bonded to 3 others. Layered structure, conducts electricity (delocalised electrons between layers), soft/slippery.

Graphene: Single layer of graphite. Exceptional strength and conductivity.

AS Level Content

Bond Polarity and Electronegativity

Electronegativity is the ability of an atom to attract the bonding electrons in a covalent bond. Values range from 0.7 (Fr) to 4.0 (F) on the Pauling scale.

Bond polarity: If two atoms have different electronegativities, the bond is polar. The more electronegative atom carries a partial negative charge (δ-), the other δ+.

Bond polarity = |χ₁ - χ₂|
0.0 - 0.4: non-polar covalent
0.4 - 1.7: polar covalent
> 1.7: ionic

Polar Molecules vs Polar Bonds

A molecule can have polar bonds but be non-polar overall if the bond dipoles cancel due to symmetry. Examples:

  • CO₂: polar bonds but linear → non-polar molecule
  • H₂O: polar bonds, bent shape → polar molecule
  • CCl₄: polar bonds, tetrahedral → non-polar molecule
  • NH₃: polar bonds, trigonal pyramidal → polar molecule

Intermolecular Forces

Van der Waals forces (London dispersion forces): Temporary induced dipoles. Present in all molecules. Strength increases with number of electrons (molecular size).

Permanent dipole-dipole forces: Between polar molecules. Additional to VdW.

Hydrogen bonding: Special strong dipole-dipole interaction. Occurs when H is bonded to N, O, or F and interacts with lone pairs on N, O, or F of another molecule.

Order of strength: Hydrogen bond > Permanent dipole-dipole > Van der Waals

Effect of Hydrogen Bonding on Physical Properties

  • Higher boiling points than expected (H₂O, NH₃, HF anomalously high)
  • Ice is less dense than water (hydrogen bonds hold molecules in open lattice)
  • Water has high surface tension and specific heat capacity

Shapes of Molecules (VSEPR Theory)

Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion theory states that electron pairs around a central atom arrange themselves to minimise repulsion.

  • 2 bond pairs: linear (180°) e.g., CO₂, BeCl₂
  • 3 bond pairs: trigonal planar (120°) e.g., BF₃, AlCl₃
  • 4 bond pairs: tetrahedral (109.5°) e.g., CH₄, CCl₄
  • 3 bond pairs + 1 lone pair: trigonal pyramidal (~107°) e.g., NH₃
  • 2 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs: bent (~104.5°) e.g., H₂O
  • 5 bond pairs: trigonal bipyramidal (90°, 120°) e.g., PCl₅
  • 6 bond pairs: octahedral (90°) e.g., SF₆

Lone pair-bond pair repulsion > bond pair-bond pair repulsion, so angles decrease when lone pairs present.

Bond Energy and Bond Length

Bond energy is the energy required to break one mole of a particular bond in gaseous molecules. Stronger bonds have higher bond energies and shorter bond lengths.

Trends: triple > double > single bond in strength. Shorter bonds are stronger.

A2 Level Content

Hybridisation

Hybridisation explains molecular geometry by mixing atomic orbitals:

  • sp: linear (e.g., C₂H₂, BeCl₂) — 1 s + 1 p orbital
  • sp²: trigonal planar (e.g., C₂H₄, BF₃) — 1 s + 2 p orbitals
  • sp³: tetrahedral (e.g., CH₄, NH₃, H₂O) — 1 s + 3 p orbitals

In ethene, the C=C double bond consists of one σ (sigma) bond from sp² overlap and one π (pi) bond from unhybridised p orbital overlap.

Sigma and Pi Bonds

  • Sigma (σ) bonds: formed by head-on overlap of orbitals. All single bonds are σ. Present in all multiple bonds too.
  • Pi (π) bonds: formed by sideways overlap of p orbitals. One π in a double bond, two π in a triple bond.

σ bonds are stronger than π bonds. π bonds prevent rotation about double bonds, leading to cis-trans isomerism.

Delocalisation and Resonance

In molecules like benzene (C₆H₆), electrons are delocalised over all six carbon atoms in a ring. This gives:

  • Greater stability (delocalisation energy)
  • Equal C-C bond lengths (intermediate between single and double)
  • Resistance to addition reactions (unlike alkenes)

Resonance structures are different Lewis representations of the same delocalised system.

🧪 Worked Example

Question: Calculate the mass of NaOH needed to make 500 cm³ of 0.2 mol/dm³ solution. (Mᵣ NaOH = 40)

Solution:
n = c × V = 0.2 × (500/1000) = 0.1 mol
mass = n × Mᵣ = 0.1 × 40 = 4.0 g
Answer: 4.0 g
Forgetting to convert cm³ to dm³ (÷1000) when using n = c × V. Always check your units!
In a balanced equation, mole ratios are the stoichiometric coefficients. Use these to convert between substances.

⚡ Quick Recall

  • n = mass / Mᵣ
  • n = concentration × volume (dm³)
  • 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³
  • % yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100

Last-Minute Summary

🎯 Key Points to Remember

  • Ionic: metal + non-metal, electrostatic attraction, giant lattice
  • Covalent: non-metal + non-metal, electron sharing, can be simple molecular or giant covalent
  • Metallic: metal cations + delocalised electrons
  • Electronegativity increases across period, decreases down group
  • H-bonding requires H bonded to N, O, or F interacting with N, O, or F
  • VSEPR: lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs, reducing bond angles
  • sp = linear, sp² = trigonal planar, sp³ = tetrahedral
  • Every bond has 1 σ; double bond = 1σ + 1π; triple bond = 1σ + 2π
  • Delocalisation in benzene gives stability and equal bond lengths

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Confusing intermolecular forces with covalent bonds
  • Forgetting that giant covalent structures don't have intermolecular forces
  • Saying NH₃ or H₂O is tetrahedral (they're trigonal pyramidal / bent)
  • Not mentioning both electronegativity difference AND molecular symmetry for polarity
  • Confusing bond energy with atomisation energy
