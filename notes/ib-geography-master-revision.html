IBG1: Population

Overview

Changing Population explores global population trends, demographic transition, fertility and mortality patterns, migration, and the challenges posed by ageing populations. This topic is examined in Paper 1 (short answers and essays) and Paper 2 (geographic perspectives). Understanding population pyramids and the Demographic Transition Model (DTM) is essential.

1.1 Global Population Trends

  • World population reached 8 billion in 2022.
  • Growth is uneven: rapid in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia; slowing or declining in Europe and East Asia.
  • Population momentum: even if fertility falls, population continues growing due to youthful age structure.
Natural increase rate (NIR) = crude birth rate (CBR) − crude death rate (CDR)
Doubling time ≈ 70 / growth rate (%)

1.2 Demographic Transition Model (DTM)

StageCBRCDRNIRExample
1 (High stationary)HighHighLow/zeroNo countries today
2 (Early expanding)HighFallingHighNiger, Angola
3 (Late expanding)FallingLowModerateIndia, Brazil
4 (Low stationary)LowLowLow/zeroUSA, Australia
5 (Declining)Very lowLowNegativeJapan, Germany

Limitations of DTM

  • Originally based on European experience — may not apply to all countries.
  • Does not account for migration.
  • Some countries (e.g., China) moved through stages rapidly due to policy intervention.

1.3 Fertility & Mortality

Fertility

  • Total Fertility Rate (TFR): average number of children per woman.
  • Replacement level: approximately 2.1 (accounting for infant mortality).
  • Factors affecting fertility: education (especially female), employment, urbanisation, contraception access, cultural/religious norms, government policy.

Mortality

  • Infant Mortality Rate (IMR): deaths under 1 year per 1000 live births.
  • Life expectancy: affected by healthcare, sanitation, nutrition, education, and conflict.

1.4 Migration

  • Voluntary: economic migration, education, family reunification.
  • Forced: refugees, asylum seekers, internally displaced persons (IDPs).
  • Lee's Push-Pull Model: push factors (unemployment, conflict) and pull factors (jobs, safety, education).

Impacts of Migration

  • Origin country: remittances, brain drain, reduced labour supply, demographic imbalance.
  • Destination country: labour supply, cultural diversity, strain on services, social tensions.

1.5 Ageing Populations

  • Caused by low fertility and increased life expectancy.
  • Challenges: increased dependency ratio, pension costs, healthcare demand, labour shortages.
  • Responses: pro-natalist policies, raising retirement age, immigration, automation.
Dependency ratio = (dependents / working-age population) × 100
Old-age dependency ratio = (population 65+ / population 15–64) × 100

Common Misconceptions

  • DTM universality: Not all countries follow the DTM exactly — policy and culture can accelerate or delay transitions.
  • Migration and refugees: Refugees have specific legal status under the 1951 Convention; not all migrants are refugees.
  • Population pyramids: A broad base does not always mean rapid growth — check the scale and actual numbers.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: Short-answer questions on population pyramids, DTM stages, and migration data.
  • Paper 2: 10-mark and 15-mark essays on population policies, migration impacts, or ageing populations.
  • IA: Population change fieldwork or secondary data analysis is possible.

Last-Minute Summary

  • TFR replacement level ≈ 2.1; affected by education, policy, culture.

  • In evaluation, consider short-term vs. long-term and different stakeholder perspectives.

IBG2: Climate Change

Overview

Climate Change examines the causes, impacts, and responses to global climate change. It is one of the most topical and frequently examined areas in IB Geography, appearing in Paper 1, Paper 2, and the IA. Understanding the enhanced greenhouse effect, climate vulnerability, and mitigation/adaptation strategies is essential.

2.1 Causes of Climate Change

Natural Causes

  • Orbital variations (Milankovitch cycles).
  • Solar output changes.
  • Volcanic eruptions (aerosols reflect sunlight).

Anthropogenic (Human) Causes

  • Burning fossil fuels: releases CO₂.
  • Deforestation: reduces carbon sinks.
  • Agriculture: livestock produce methane (CH₄); rice paddies emit methane.
  • Industry: cement production releases CO₂; refrigerants release CFCs/HFCs.

The Enhanced Greenhouse Effect

Human activities increase concentrations of greenhouse gases (GHGs), trapping more infrared radiation and raising global temperatures.

Major GHGs: CO₂ (fossil fuels), CH₄ (agriculture, landfill), N₂O (fertilisers), CFCs/HFCs (industry)
CO₂ is the most significant by volume; CH₄ has much higher global warming potential (GWP) per molecule.

2.2 Impacts of Climate Change

  • Rising temperatures: heatwaves, changed growing seasons.
  • Melting ice: sea level rise, loss of albedo (positive feedback).
  • Extreme weather: more intense hurricanes, droughts, floods.
  • Ocean acidification: CO₂ dissolves in seawater, harming marine ecosystems.
  • Ecosystem disruption: species migration, coral bleaching, biodiversity loss.
  • Human systems: food security threats, water scarcity, health impacts (disease vectors), climate migration.

2.3 Mitigation

Efforts to reduce or prevent emission of greenhouse gases.

  • Renewable energy: solar, wind, hydroelectric, geothermal.
  • Energy efficiency: improved building standards, public transport.
  • Carbon capture and storage (CCS): capturing CO₂ at source.
  • Reforestation/afforestation: increasing carbon sinks.
  • International agreements: Kyoto Protocol (1997), Paris Agreement (2015).

2.4 Adaptation

Adjusting to actual or expected climate change effects.

  • Hard engineering: sea walls, flood barriers, coastal realignment.
  • Soft engineering: beach nourishment, wetland restoration, managed retreat.
  • Agricultural: drought-resistant crops, changing planting dates.
  • Social: early warning systems, climate-resilient infrastructure.

2.5 Vulnerability & Resilience

  • Vulnerability: susceptibility to harm from climate change. Determined by exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity.
  • Resilience: ability to recover from climate shocks.
  • Low-income countries are often more vulnerable due to limited adaptive capacity, despite lower emissions.

Common Misconceptions

  • Weather vs. climate: Weather is short-term; climate is long-term average. A cold day does not disprove climate change.
  • Ozone depletion vs. greenhouse effect: Different issues. CFCs deplete ozone but also contribute to warming.
  • Climate change vs. global warming: Global warming refers to temperature increase; climate change includes all impacts (extreme weather, sea level, etc.).

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: Short-answer questions on greenhouse gases, climate data, and mitigation strategies.
  • Paper 2: Essays on vulnerability, mitigation vs. adaptation, or international cooperation.
  • IA: Climate-related fieldwork (coastal erosion, urban heat island, carbon footprint) is common.

  • Explain the enhanced greenhouse effect and distinguish it from the natural greenhouse effect.
  • Discuss the impacts of climate change on food security in sub-Saharan Africa.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Causes: fossil fuels, deforestation, agriculture, industry → enhanced greenhouse effect.
  • Impacts: temperature rise, sea level rise, extreme weather, ocean acidification, ecosystem loss.
  • Mitigation: reduce emissions (renewables, efficiency, CCS, reforestation).
  • Adaptation: adjust to impacts (hard/soft engineering, agricultural change, early warning).
  • Vulnerability = exposure + sensitivity − adaptive capacity.
  • Low-income countries often most vulnerable despite lowest emissions.

Exam Tips

  • Use specific data (e.g., "global temperatures have risen by ~1.1°C since pre-industrial times").
  • Always distinguish between mitigation and adaptation.
  • Include named case studies (e.g., Bangladesh adaptation, Germany's Energiewende).
  • For evaluation, consider effectiveness, cost, equity, and timescale.
  • Diagrams of the greenhouse effect should show incoming shortwave and outgoing longwave radiation.

IBG3: Globalisation

Overview

Global Resource Consumption examines patterns of resource use, unequal access, geopolitical tensions, and pathways to sustainability. It covers water, food, and energy resources and is examined across all papers. Case studies of resource conflicts and sustainable management are particularly important.

3.1 Patterns of Resource Consumption

  • Ecological footprint: measure of human demand on nature. High-income countries have much larger per capita footprints.
  • Biocapacity: Earth's ability to regenerate resources and absorb waste.
  • Humanity currently uses resources equivalent to ~1.7 Earths — unsustainable.

3.2 Water Resources

  • Physical scarcity: insufficient water to meet demand (e.g., Middle East, North Africa).
  • Economic scarcity: water exists but infrastructure is lacking (e.g., sub-Saharan Africa).
  • Water stress: annual supply below 1700 m³ per person.

Water Management

  • Large dams (Three Gorges, Aswan) — benefits (hydropower, irrigation) and costs (displacement, ecosystem damage).
  • Desalination — energy-intensive, expensive, brine disposal issues.
  • Water transfers — e.g., China's South-North Water Transfer Project.

3.3 Food Resources

  • Food security: physical, social, and economic access to sufficient, safe, nutritious food.
  • Threats: climate change, conflict, land degradation, water scarcity, population growth.
  • Green Revolution: increased yields through HYV seeds, irrigation, fertilisers — but environmental costs.

Food Production Systems

  • Intensive vs. extensive farming.
  • Organic vs. conventional.
  • Local vs. global food systems.
  • Food miles and carbon footprint.

3.4 Energy Resources

  • Fossil fuels: coal, oil, natural gas — high energy density but carbon emissions.
  • Renewables: solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, biomass.
  • Nuclear: low carbon but waste and safety concerns.

Energy Security

  • Uninterrupted availability of energy at affordable prices.
  • Geopolitical implications: oil shocks (1973, 1979), Russia-Ukraine gas disputes.

3.5 Geopolitics of Resources

  • Resource nationalism: states control access to resources (e.g., OPEC).
  • Resource curse: countries rich in natural resources often have poor development outcomes.
  • Conflicts: water disputes (Nile, Indus), oil conflicts (Nigeria, Middle East).

3.6 Sustainability

  • Sustainable development: meeting present needs without compromising future generations (Brundtland Report, 1987).
  • Circular economy: reduce, reuse, recycle.
  • Local and international initiatives: SDGs, fair trade, organic certification.

Common Misconceptions

  • Food security vs. food self-sufficiency: A country can be food secure through imports; self-sufficiency is not required.
  • Renewable = sustainable: Renewables have environmental impacts too (e.g., hydroelectric dams displace communities).
  • Resource curse: Not inevitable — Norway managed oil wealth effectively through sovereign wealth funds.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: Short-answer questions on resource data, ecological footprints, and water stress.
  • Paper 2: Essays on resource conflicts, sustainability, or food security.
  • IA: Resource consumption surveys, waste audits, or water quality testing are possible.

Past Paper Trends (2019–2024)

May 2023: Water conflict in transboundary river basins.
November 2022: Food miles vs. local food systems debate.
May 2022: Energy security strategies in oil-importing countries.

  • Explain why water scarcity is as much an economic issue as a physical one.
  • Discuss the social, economic, and environmental impacts of the Green Revolution.

  • Ecological footprint > biocapacity → unsustainable.
  • Water: physical vs. economic scarcity; dams, desalination, transfers.
  • Food: security depends on access, availability, utilisation, stability.
  • Energy: fossil fuels vs. renewables vs. nuclear; energy security concerns.
  • Resource curse: wealth can hinder development without good governance.
  • Sustainability: circular economy, SDGs, fair trade.

Exam Tips

  • Distinguish between different types of scarcity and security.
  • For evaluation, weigh economic, social, and environmental factors.
  • Include maps or diagrams where appropriate.
  • Consider different scales: local, national, global.

IBG4: Resources

Overview

Freshwater examines the hydrological cycle, drainage basin management, flooding, water scarcity, and sustainable water management. This topic is highly relevant to current global challenges and is examined across all papers. Case studies of major river basins and flood events are essential.

4.1 The Drainage Basin

  • A drainage basin is an area of land drained by a river and its tributaries.
  • Watershed: boundary separating adjacent drainage basins.

Hydrological Cycle within a Basin

  • Inputs: precipitation.
  • Stores: interception, surface storage, soil moisture, groundwater.
  • Flows: overland flow, throughflow, baseflow, infiltration, percolation.
  • Outputs: evaporation, transpiration, river discharge.
Water balance: P − Q − E − ΔS = 0
P = precipitation, Q = runoff, E = evapotranspiration, ΔS = change in storage

4.2 River Discharge & Flooding

Storm Hydrograph

  • Lag time: time between peak rainfall and peak discharge.
  • Rising limb: increasing discharge.
  • Falling limb (recession): decreasing discharge.

Factors Affecting Flood Risk

  • Physical: intense rainfall, steep slopes, impermeable rock, small basin, urbanisation (reduces infiltration).
  • Human: deforestation, urban development, river channelisation, climate change.

4.3 Water Scarcity

  • Physical scarcity: not enough water to meet demand (e.g., Middle East).
  • Economic scarcity: inadequate infrastructure to deliver water (e.g., parts of sub-Saharan Africa).
  • Pollution can also cause scarcity by rendering water unusable.

4.4 Water Management Strategies

Hard Engineering

  • Dams and reservoirs: store water, generate hydroelectricity, control floods — but displace communities and damage ecosystems.
  • Levees and embankments: contain floodwater.
  • Channel straightening: speeds up flow, reducing local flood risk but increasing downstream risk.

Soft Engineering

  • Floodplain zoning: restrict development in high-risk areas.
  • Wetland restoration: natural water storage and filtration.
  • Afforestation: increases interception and infiltration.
  • River restoration: re-meandering to slow flow and improve habitats.

4.5 Groundwater

  • Aquifers: permeable rock layers storing groundwater.
  • Over-abstraction causes falling water tables, land subsidence, and saltwater intrusion in coastal areas.
  • Recharge rates vary — fossil aquifers (e.g., Ogallala) are non-renewable on human timescales.

Common Misconceptions

  • Flooding: Hard engineering does not eliminate flood risk — it transfers it (often downstream).
  • Groundwater: Groundwater is not infinite — recharge rates limit sustainable extraction.
  • Desalination: It produces freshwater but is energy-intensive and creates brine waste.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: Short-answer questions on hydrographs, drainage basin processes, and flood management.
  • Paper 2: Essays comparing hard and soft engineering, evaluating water management strategies.
  • IA: River fieldwork (velocity, discharge, sediment size) is very common.

Past Paper Trends (2019–2024)

May 2023: Groundwater depletion case study (e.g., India or USA).
November 2022: Storm hydrograph interpretation with lag time analysis.
May 2022: Evaluation of large dam projects (Three Gorges, Aswan).

  • Water scarcity: physical, economic, pollution-induced.

  • Use named case studies (e.g., Bangladesh floods, Colorado River basin).
  • For evaluation, compare hard and soft approaches on cost, sustainability, and effectiveness.
  • Include diagrams of drainage basin processes.

IBG5: Urban Environments

Overview

Extreme Environments examines hot arid and cold environments, the opportunities and challenges they present, and strategies for sustainable management. This topic is examined in Paper 1 and Paper 2, and is a popular choice for IA fieldwork in desert or glacial landscapes.

5.1 Hot Arid Environments

Characteristics

  • High temperatures, low rainfall (< 250 mm/year), high evaporation rates.
  • Diurnal temperature range is large (hot days, cold nights).
  • Vegetation is sparse and adapted (xerophytes, succulents).
  • Soils are thin, saline, and low in organic matter.

Landforms

  • Erosional: yardangs, deflation hollows, zeugen.
  • Depositional: sand dunes (barchan, seif, star), alluvial fans, playas.

Opportunities

  • Tourism (desert safaris, eco-tourism).
  • Mineral extraction (oil, uranium, phosphates).
  • Solar energy potential.
  • Irrigated agriculture (oasis farming).

Challenges

  • Water scarcity and salinisation.
  • Desertification due to overgrazing and deforestation.
  • Extreme temperatures limiting human activity.

5.2 Cold Environments

Characteristics

  • Polar and tundra climates; permafrost (permanently frozen ground).
  • Low precipitation, cold temperatures, short growing seasons.
  • Fragile ecosystems with slow recovery rates.

Landforms

  • Glacial erosion: cirques, arêtes, U-shaped valleys, roche moutonnée.
  • Glacial deposition: moraines, drumlins, eskers.
  • Periglacial: patterned ground, pingos, solifluction.

Opportunities

  • Tourism (Antarctica, Alpine skiing, Northern Lights).
  • Fishing (North Atlantic, Southern Ocean).
  • Mineral and hydrocarbon extraction (Arctic oil, Alaska).

Challenges

  • Permafrost thawing due to climate change — infrastructure damage, methane release.
  • Inaccessibility and high operating costs.
  • Environmental sensitivity — slow recovery from damage.

5.3 Sustainability in Extreme Environments

  • Desert: drip irrigation, solar desalination, controlled tourism, anti-desertification programmes (Great Green Wall).
  • Cold: protected areas (Antarctic Treaty), sustainable fishing quotas, limits on mineral extraction, permafrost monitoring.

Common Misconceptions

  • Deserts: Not all deserts are hot (e.g., Antarctica is a desert by rainfall). Not all are sandy (rocky and gravel deserts are common).
  • Glaciers: Glaciers move by internal deformation and basal sliding, not by "flowing" like liquids.
  • Permafrost: Permafrost does not mean no liquid water — active layers thaw seasonally.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1: Short-answer questions on landform identification, climate data, and human activities.
  • Paper 2: Essays on sustainability, opportunities/challenges, or climate change impacts.
  • IA: Fieldwork in arid or glacial environments (e.g., dune morphology, glacier measurement).
