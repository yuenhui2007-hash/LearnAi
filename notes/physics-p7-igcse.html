📘 Subject Context

A thorough understanding of Electric Fields is crucial for IGCSE Physics. This topic builds fundamental skills in calculation, graphical analysis, and conceptual reasoning that are tested throughout the examination.

IGCSE Physics examinations emphasise problem-solving. Paper 2 tests conceptual understanding; Paper 4 tests application and calculation. Always define your variables, show substitution into formulae, and state units clearly.

Electric Fields

0625 (IGCSE)

Detailed Notes

1. Electric Field Strength

E = F/Q (force per unit positive charge)
For a point charge: E = Q/(4πε₀r²)

Unit: N/C or V/m.

2. Electric Potential

V = W/Q (work done per unit charge)
For a point charge: V = Q/(4πε₀r)

Unit: volt (V).

3. Relationship between E and V

E = -dV/dr

The electric field strength is the negative gradient of potential.

4. Uniform Electric Field

E = V/d (between parallel plates)

5. Coulomb's Law

F = Q₁Q₂/(4πε₀r²)

Force between two point charges is proportional to the product of charges and inversely proportional to the square of their separation.

6. Electric Field Patterns

  • Point charge: radial field, lines radiate outward (+) or inward (-).
  • Parallel plates: uniform field, straight parallel lines.
  • Dipole: field lines from + to -.

Additional Applications

  • Real-world context: Engineering applications, medical physics, environmental monitoring

Common Exam Question Types

  • Definition questions — state precisely, use correct terminology
  • Calculation questions — show working, include units, check significant figures
  • Explanation questions — use "because" to link cause and effect
  • Graph questions — read axes carefully, describe trends using data
Always define quantities before using them in explanations. E.g., "Force is the rate of change of momentum, therefore..."

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing series and parallel circuit rules — in series, current is constant; in parallel, voltage is constant.
  • Forgetting that distance is a scalar while displacement is a vector — they are not interchangeable.
  • Forgetting to convert units — e.g., cm to m, g to kg, minutes to seconds before substituting into equations.
  • Forgetting that resistance increases with temperature for a filament lamp (not constant).
  • Using the wrong equation of motion — check which variable is missing (s, u, v, a, t).

✅ Key Points to Remember

  • Learn all equations of motion and when to use each — practise identifying the missing variable.
  • Memorise the circuit symbols and be able to construct series and parallel circuits.
  • Remember Newton's three laws and be able to apply them to different situations.
  • Understand the relationship between work, energy, and power — and their units.
  • Know the difference between scalar and vector quantities and give examples of each.

Worked Example 1: Unit Conversion and Prefixes

Problem

A microprocessor has a clock speed of 3.2 GHz. Express this in Hz and in MHz.

Solution

3.2 GHz = 3.2 × 10⁹ Hz = 3,200,000,000 Hz

3.2 GHz = 3.2 × 10³ MHz = 3200 MHz

Worked Example 2: Resultant of Two Perpendicular Forces

Problem

Two forces act on a body: 12 N east and 16 N north. Calculate the magnitude and direction of the resultant force.

Solution

Using Pythagoras: R = √(12² + 16²) = √(144 + 256) = √400 = 20 N

Direction: θ = tan⁻¹(16/12) = tan⁻¹(1.333) = 53.1° north of east

Worked Example 3: Analysing Motion from a Velocity-Time Graph

Problem

A velocity-time graph shows a body accelerating from 5 m/s to 15 m/s in 4 s, then moving at constant velocity for 6 s, then decelerating to rest in 5 s. Calculate (a) the acceleration in each stage, (b) the total displacement.

Solution

(a) Stage 1: a = (15 − 5) / 4 = 2.5 m/s²

Stage 2: a = 0 m/s² (constant velocity)

Stage 3: a = (0 − 15) / 5 = −3.0 m/s² (deceleration)

(b) Displacement = area under graph

= ½(5 + 15) × 4 + 15 × 6 + ½ × 15 × 5

= 40 + 90 + 37.5 = 167.5 m

Practice Questions

Question 1

A car accelerates uniformly from rest at 2.0 m/s² for 8.0 s, then maintains constant velocity for 12 s, then brakes with uniform deceleration and stops in 10 s. Draw a velocity-time graph and calculate the total distance travelled.

Answer: 304 m

Question 2

A stone is thrown vertically upwards with a velocity of 20 m/s from the edge of a cliff 30 m above sea level. Calculate (a) the maximum height reached above the cliff, (b) the time taken to reach the sea, (c) the velocity just before hitting the sea. (g = 10 m/s²)

Answers: (a) 20 m, (b) 6.0 s, (c) 40 m/s downwards

Question 3

Two tugs pull a ship with forces of 8000 N and 6000 N at an angle of 60° to each other. Calculate the magnitude of the resultant force.

Answer: 12,166 N (or 12.2 kN)

  • Draw graphs with a sharp pencil, label axes with quantities and units, and use a ruler for straight lines.
  • For ray diagrams, use a ruler and protractor — accuracy matters.

📐 Worked Example

Question: A ball is thrown vertically upwards at 20 m/s. Calculate the maximum height reached. (g = 9.81 m/s²)

Solution:
At max height, v = 0. Using v² = u² + 2as:
0 = 20² + 2(−9.81)s
0 = 400 − 19.62s
s = 400 / 19.62 = 20.4 m
Answer: 20.4 m
Using g = +9.81 m/s² when the object is moving upwards — g always acts downwards, so use −9.81 m/s² for upward motion.
Always draw a diagram and define your positive direction before solving kinematics problems.

⚡ Quick Recall

  • v = u + at (no displacement)
  • s = ut + ½at² (no final velocity)
  • v² = u² + 2as (no time)
  • s = ½(u+v)t (no acceleration)
  • All apply only for constant acceleration
