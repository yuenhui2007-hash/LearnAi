International Relations, 1871–1945

Cambridge 9389 A-Level / 0470 IGCSE

📘 IGCSE Content

The New European Order, 1871–1914

  • German unification (1871) shifted balance of power. Bismarck's alliance system (see h1): Dual Alliance 1879, Triple Alliance 1882, Reinsurance Treaty 1887.
  • Kaiser Wilhelm II (1888) abandoned Bismarck's caution. Weltpolitik; naval expansion; Morocco crises.
  • Triple Entente (1907): Britain, France, Russia. Europe divided into two armed camps.
  • Balkan crises (1908–09, 1912–13) destabilised region. Assassination at Sarajevo (28 June 1914) triggered July Crisis and WWI.

The First World War (1914–18)

  • Western Front: Schlieffen Plan failed at Marne (Sept 1914); trench warfare 1914–18; Verdun (1916), Somme (1916), Passchendaele (1917). Stalemate until 1918.
  • Eastern Front: initial Russian advances; German victories (Tannenberg 1914); Russia exited after Bolshevik Revolution (Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, March 1918).
  • War expanded: Ottoman Empire joined Central Powers (Nov 1914); Gallipoli (1915–16); Middle East campaigns (Lawrence of Arabia). Italy joined Entente (1915); Romania (1916); USA (April 1917).
  • Why did USA enter? Unrestricted submarine warfare (Lusitania 1915, resumption 1917); Zimmerman Telegram (Jan 1917) — German proposal for Mexican alliance against USA.
  • 1918: German Spring Offensive failed; Allied Hundred Days Offensive; armistice (11 Nov 1918).

The Paris Peace Settlement (1919–20)

  • Big Three: Wilson (USA), Lloyd George (Britain), Clemenceau (France). Orlando (Italy) less influential.
  • Wilson's Fourteen Points (Jan 1918): self-determination, League of Nations, freedom of seas, open diplomacy. Idealistic but vague.
  • Treaty of Versailles (June 1919): War Guilt Clause (Article 231); reparations (£6.6 billion); demilitarisation (Rhineland occupied, army limited to 100,000); loss of colonies; Alsace-Lorraine to France; Polish Corridor.
  • Other treaties: St Germain (Austria), Trianon (Hungary), Neuilly (Bulgaria), Sèvres (Ottoman, revised by Lausanne 1923).
  • League of Nations: Covenant included collective security, arbitration, disarmament. USA never joined. 42 founding members.

The League of Nations and the 1920s

  • Successes: Aaland Islands (1920), Upper Silesia (1921), Mosul (1926), Greece-Bulgaria (1925).
  • Failures: Vilna (1920) — Poland seized city; League unable to act. Corfu (1923) — Mussolini bombarded Greek island; League backed down.
  • Disarmament: Washington Naval Conference (1921–22) limited US, Britain, Japan navies. Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928) outlawed war — no enforcement mechanism.
  • Locarno Era (1925): Germany, France, Belgium guaranteed borders; Britain and Italy as guarantors. Germany joined League (1926). Dawes Plan (1924) and Young Plan (1929) rescheduled reparations.

The Road to War, 1929–39

  • Great Depression: economic nationalism; extremism rose in Germany and Japan.
  • Japan invaded Manchuria (1931): League investigated (Lytton Report); Japan left League (1933). No effective action — demonstrated League's weakness against major powers.
  • Italy invaded Abyssinia (1935): League imposed sanctions but excluded oil; Hoare-Laval Pact (secret deal to give Italy most of Abyssinia) leaked and abandoned. Mussolini conquered Abyssinia (1936).
  • German rearmament (1935); Rhineland remilitarised (1936); Anschluss (1938); Munich (1938); Czechoslovakia occupied (1939); Poland invaded (1939).
  • Appeasement: Britain and France sought to avoid war, believed Hitler had limited aims, feared communism more than fascism.
  • Nazi-Soviet Pact (Aug 1939): stunned world; enabled Hitler's invasion of Poland.
  • 1 Sept 1939: Germany invaded Poland. Britain and France declared war (3 Sept).

World War II (1939–45)

  • Blitzkrieg in Europe (1939–41): Poland, Scandinavia, Low Countries, France. Battle of Britain (1940) — first German defeat.
  • Barbarossa (June 1941): Germany invaded USSR. Pearl Harbor (Dec 1941): Japan attacked USA; Germany declared war on USA.
  • Turning points: Midway (June 1942) — Pacific; El Alamein (Nov 1942) — North Africa; Stalingrad (Feb 1943) — Eastern Front.
  • Grand Alliance: Churchill, Roosevelt, Stalin. Tehran (1943), Yalta (1945), Potsdam (1945).
  • D-Day (June 1944); liberation of France; Soviet advance from East.
  • Atomic bombs (Hiroshima 6 Aug, Nagasaki 9 Aug 1945): Japan surrendered (15 Aug). WWII dead: approx. 70–85 million.

📗 AS Level Content

The Versailles Settlement: Fair or Carthaginian?

  • "Carthaginian peace" (Keynes, Economic Consequences of the Peace, 1919): Versailles was too harsh; destabilised Germany economically and politically; led to WWII.
  • Revisionist (e.g., Étienne Mantoux, A.J.P. Taylor): Versailles was not especially harsh compared to Brest-Litovsk or German treatment of Russia. Germany could have paid reparations; problem was political will, not economic capacity. Weimar politicians used "stab-in-the-back" myth to avoid responsibility.
  • Fair assessment: Versailles was harsh in parts (guilt clause, reparations) but left Germany as largest European state with industrial potential. Failure of enforcement (Locarno spirit, appeasement) was more important than treaty terms.

Why Did the League Fail?

  • Structural: no armed force; unanimity required for action; great powers dominated but were not committed (USA absent; USSR expelled 1939; Germany/Japan/Italy left).
  • Great power rivalry: Britain and France were primary enforcers but had conflicting interests (France wanted security; Britain wanted balance).
  • Economic depression: reduced willingness to act; prioritised domestic recovery over collective security.
  • Manchuria and Abyssinia: both showed that without great power commitment, League was powerless. Sanctions were half-hearted.

Appeasement: Causes and Assessment

  • Motives (see h8): guilt over Versailles, fear of war, military unpreparedness, belief in Hitler's limited aims, anti-communism, empire concerns.
  • Alternative policies: could Britain and France have stopped Hitler earlier? Rhineland (1936) was probably last chance for easy resistance. By 1938, Germany too strong.
  • Churchill's critique: Munich was dishonourable and encouraged Hitler. Counter: Chamberlain bought time for rearmament (though he hoped for peace, not merely time).

📙 A2 Level Content

The Thirty Years' War Thesis

  • Some historians (e.g., Ernst Nolte, controversially; more broadly, Arno Mayer) see 1914–45 as a continuous crisis of European civilisation. WWI and WWII were part of same conflict with intermission.
  • Evidence: unresolved issues from WWI (German grievances, border disputes, economic instability); same powers fought; ideological extremes (fascism, communism) emerged from WWI.
  • Critics: WWII was fundamentally different — racial genocide, total war, global scope. Treating as one war risks minimising distinct causes.

Comparing WWI and WWII

  • Causes: WWI — alliance system, militarism, nationalism, miscalculation. WWII — aggressive dictatorships, failure of Versailles, appeasement, economic crisis.
  • Nature of war: WWI — trench warfare, stalemate, millions dead for limited gains. WWII — mobile warfare (Blitzkrieg), air power, civilian bombing, genocide.
  • Peace settlements: Versailles (1919) blamed Germany; created resentment. 1945 settlements (Potsdam) divided Germany, acknowledged shared responsibility, but Cold War prevented lasting peace.

Historiography of Appeasement

  • 1930s–40s: appeasement condemned as cowardly and foolish (Churchill, Guilty Men, 1940).
  • 1960s–70s: revisionist historians (e.g., A.J.P. Taylor) argued appeasement was realistic given military and economic constraints. Taylor saw Hitler as opportunist, not planner.
  • 1980s–90s: post-revisionist (e.g., R.A.C. Parker): Chamberlain had some room for manoeuvre but chose appeasement due to misjudgment, not necessity.
  • Recent: emphasis on intelligence failures, overestimation of German strength, and ideological anti-communism as factors.

Essay Frameworks

  • "The Treaty of Versailles caused WWII": War guilt, reparations, territorial losses, created resentment. Counter: Germany could have recovered; enforcement failed; Hitler's aggression was primary cause.
  • "The League of Nations was doomed from the start": No USA, no armed force, unanimity rule, great power rivalry. Counter: succeeded in minor disputes; failed because powers did not support it, not because concept was flawed.
  • "Appeasement was the only realistic policy in the 1930s": Military weakness, public opinion, empire commitments, economic crisis. Counter: early resistance possible (Rhineland 1936); encouraged Hitler; moral failure.

📜 Worked Example

Question: To what extent was Germany responsible for WWI?

Essay Plan:
FOR: Blank cheque, Schlieffen Plan, naval expansion, Fischer thesis
AGAINST: Austrian ultimatum, Russian mobilisation, alliance system, Clark thesis
Conclusion: Significant responsibility but not sole blame
Writing narrative instead of analysis. History essays need CAUSATION — explain WHY and SIGNIFICANCE.
Always use SPECIFIC EVIDENCE — dates, names, statistics. "On 28 June 1914" not "in 1914".

📝 Last-Minute Summary

  • 1871–1914: Bismarck's system → Wilhelm's Weltpolik → alliances → Sarajevo 1914.
  • WWI: Western Front stalemate; USA enters 1917; armistice 11 Nov 1918.
  • 1919: Versailles — Article 231 (war guilt), reparations, demilitarisation, League of Nations. USA never joins.
  • 1920s: League successes (Aaland, Upper Silesia); Locarno Era (1925); Kellogg-Briand (1928). Failures: Corfu (1923).
  • 1931: Manchuria — League ineffective. 1935: Abyssinia — sanctions fail.
  • 1933–39: Hitler withdraws from League, rearm, Rhineland, Anschluss, Munich, Czech occupation, Nazi-Soviet Pact, Poland invasion (1 Sept 1939).
  • WWII: Blitzkrieg 1939–41; Barbarossa June 1941; Pearl Harbor Dec 1941; turning points 1942–43; D-Day June 1944; atomic bombs Aug 1945.
  • Historiography: Versailles (Keynes vs Taylor); League (structural doomed vs power failure); appeasement (Churchill vs revisionists).
  • Exam Tip: Know the chronology of crises 1931→1935→1936→1938→1939. Link interwar failures to WWII causes. Distinguish between WWI causes (systemic) and WWII causes (aggressive dictatorships).
