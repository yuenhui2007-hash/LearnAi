A thorough understanding of Kinematics is crucial for IGCSE Physics. This topic builds fundamental skills in calculation, graphical analysis, and conceptual reasoning that are tested throughout the examination.

IGCSE Physics examinations emphasise problem-solving. Paper 2 tests conceptual understanding; Paper 4 tests application and calculation. Always define your variables, show substitution into formulae, and state units clearly.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing speed and velocity — velocity is a vector (has direction); speed is a scalar.
  • Forgetting that distance is a scalar while displacement is a vector — they are not interchangeable.
  • Mixing up constructive and destructive interference — constructive = waves in phase, amplitude increases.
  • Forgetting that resistance increases with temperature for a filament lamp (not constant).
  • Forgetting to convert units — e.g., cm to m, g to kg, minutes to seconds before substituting into equations.

✅ Key Points to Remember

  • Know the ray diagrams for convex and concave lenses and mirrors.
  • Always draw force diagrams — this helps identify the forces acting and their directions.
  • Know the difference between scalar and vector quantities and give examples of each.
  • Understand the relationship between work, energy, and power — and their units.
  • Learn all equations of motion and when to use each — practise identifying the missing variable.

Choosing the Right Equation of Motion

The five SUVAT equations describe motion with constant acceleration. The key to solving kinematics problems is identifying which variable is unknown or not needed, then selecting the equation that does not contain that variable.

Missing VariableEquation to Use
Displacement (s)v = u + at
Final velocity (v)s = ut + ½at²
Time (t)v² = u² + 2as
Acceleration (a)s = ½(u + v)t
Initial velocity (u)s = vt − ½at²
Worked Example: Free Fall

Question: A ball is dropped from a height of 45 m. Calculate (a) the time taken to reach the ground and (b) the velocity just before impact. (g = 10 m/s²)

Solution:

Given: u = 0 m/s, s = 45 m, a = g = 10 m/s²

(a) Using s = ut + ½at²: 45 = 0 + ½(10)t² → t² = 9 → t = 3.0 s

(b) Using v = u + at: v = 0 + 10(3) = 30 m/s

Alternative for (b): v² = u² + 2as = 0 + 2(10)(45) = 900 → v = 30 m/s

Worked Example: Deceleration

Question: A car travelling at 25 m/s brakes with a deceleration of 5.0 m/s². Calculate the stopping distance.

Solution:

Given: u = 25 m/s, v = 0 m/s, a = −5.0 m/s²

Using v² = u² + 2as: 0 = 25² + 2(−5.0)s

0 = 625 − 10s → s = 62.5 m

Graphical Analysis

Graphs provide visual representations of motion and are frequently tested:

  • Displacement-time graph: The gradient at any point gives the velocity. A curved line indicates acceleration.
  • Velocity-time graph: The gradient gives acceleration. The area under the graph gives displacement.
  • Acceleration-time graph: The area under the graph gives the change in velocity.
Worked Example: Velocity-Time Graph

Question: A velocity-time graph shows a car accelerating uniformly from rest to 20 m/s in 5 s, then maintaining this speed for 10 s, then decelerating to rest in 5 s. Calculate the total displacement.

Solution: The displacement equals the area under the v-t graph.

Area = (½ × 5 × 20) + (10 × 20) + (½ × 5 × 20) = 50 + 200 + 50 = 300 m

When analysing motion graphs, always identify what the gradient and area represent. In v-t graphs, a negative gradient means deceleration, not negative velocity.

Exam-Style Practice Questions

Question 1

A stone is thrown vertically upwards at 15 m/s. Calculate the maximum height reached and the total time until it returns to the thrower's hand. (g = 10 m/s²)

(Answers: 11.25 m, 3.0 s)

Question 2

A train accelerates from rest at 0.50 m/s² for 60 s, then travels at constant speed for 120 s, then decelerates at 1.0 m/s² to rest. Draw a velocity-time graph and calculate the total distance travelled.

(Answer: 4500 m)

Worked Example 1: Unit Conversion and Prefixes

Problem

A microprocessor has a clock speed of 3.2 GHz. Express this in Hz and in MHz.

Solution

3.2 GHz = 3.2 × 10⁹ Hz = 3,200,000,000 Hz

3.2 GHz = 3.2 × 10³ MHz = 3200 MHz

Worked Example 2: Resultant of Two Perpendicular Forces

Problem

Two forces act on a body: 12 N east and 16 N north. Calculate the magnitude and direction of the resultant force.

Solution

Using Pythagoras: R = √(12² + 16²) = √(144 + 256) = √400 = 20 N

Direction: θ = tan⁻¹(16/12) = tan⁻¹(1.333) = 53.1° north of east

Worked Example 3: Analysing Motion from a Velocity-Time Graph

Problem

A velocity-time graph shows a body accelerating from 5 m/s to 15 m/s in 4 s, then moving at constant velocity for 6 s, then decelerating to rest in 5 s. Calculate (a) the acceleration in each stage, (b) the total displacement.

Solution

(a) Stage 1: a = (15 − 5) / 4 = 2.5 m/s²

Stage 2: a = 0 m/s² (constant velocity)

Stage 3: a = (0 − 15) / 5 = −3.0 m/s² (deceleration)

(b) Displacement = area under graph

= ½(5 + 15) × 4 + 15 × 6 + ½ × 15 × 5

= 40 + 90 + 37.5 = 167.5 m

Practice Questions

Question 1

A car accelerates uniformly from rest at 2.0 m/s² for 8.0 s, then maintains constant velocity for 12 s, then brakes with uniform deceleration and stops in 10 s. Draw a velocity-time graph and calculate the total distance travelled.

Answer: 304 m

Question 2

A stone is thrown vertically upwards with a velocity of 20 m/s from the edge of a cliff 30 m above sea level. Calculate (a) the maximum height reached above the cliff, (b) the time taken to reach the sea, (c) the velocity just before hitting the sea. (g = 10 m/s²)

Answers: (a) 20 m, (b) 6.0 s, (c) 40 m/s downwards

Question 3

Two tugs pull a ship with forces of 8000 N and 6000 N at an angle of 60° to each other. Calculate the magnitude of the resultant force.

Answer: 12,166 N (or 12.2 kN)

🎯 Examination Tips

  • Read the question twice — underlining key information helps avoid misreading.
  • In calculation questions, always state the unit of the final answer.
  • For ray diagrams, use a ruler and protractor — accuracy matters.
  • Draw graphs with a sharp pencil, label axes with quantities and units, and use a ruler for straight lines.
  • Always write the formula first, then substitute values, then calculate — this earns method marks.
