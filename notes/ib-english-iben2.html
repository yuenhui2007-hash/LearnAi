Time & Space

IB DP English HL/SL — Topic 2

Overview

Time and Space (Topic 2) examines how texts are shaped by and respond to their historical, cultural, and geographical contexts. This topic is essential for the Individual Oral (IO), where students must discuss how a global issue is presented in two texts across different times or spaces.

2.1 Cultural Context

  • Culture: shared values, beliefs, customs, and practices of a group.
  • Cultural context in literature: how a text reflects, challenges, or reinforces cultural norms.
  • Issues to consider: gender roles, class structure, religion, colonialism, race, sexuality.

Global Issues (IO Focus)

IB specifies that the IO must address a global issue. Common examples:

  • Power, politics, and governance.
  • Identity, gender, and sexuality.
  • Culture, community, and belonging.
  • Conflict, peace, and reconciliation.
  • Environment, sustainability, and development.

2.2 Historical Context

  • Texts are products of their time — understanding historical background illuminates authorial choices.
  • Post-colonial literature: explores legacy of colonialism (e.g., Things Fall Apart, Wide Sargasso Sea).
  • Modernism: response to industrialisation and WWI — fragmentation, alienation.
  • Postmodernism: scepticism toward grand narratives, playfulness, intertextuality.

2.3 Authorial Choices

Authors make deliberate choices to represent time and space:

  • Temporal setting: when the action takes place (past, present, future, non-linear).
  • Spatial setting: where the action takes place (urban/rural, domestic/public, real/imagined).
  • Temporal structure: chronological, flashback, flashforward, circular, fragmented.

2.4 Representing Place

  • Setting is never neutral — it carries symbolic and thematic weight.
  • Domestic spaces: often represent safety or confinement (e.g., the house in A Doll's House).
  • Wilderness/nature: freedom, danger, the sublime, or colonial frontier.
  • Urban spaces: modernity, alienation, opportunity, decay.

2.5 IOC & Paper Skills

  • Individual Oral Commentary (IOC): 10-minute analysis of an unseen extract with 20 minutes preparation.
  • Individual Oral (IO): 10-minute presentation comparing two texts on a global issue, plus 5 minutes questioning.

Common Misconceptions

  • Context as biography: Do not reduce analysis to author biography — focus on textual evidence.
  • Global issue vagueness: "love" or "death" are themes, not global issues. Be specific (e.g., "the impact of patriarchal structures on women's agency").
  • IO balance: Spend roughly equal time on both texts — do not favour one.

Practice Questions

  • Analyse how a writer uses temporal shifts to create narrative tension.
  • Discuss how two texts you have studied represent the same global issue in different cultural contexts.
  • Explain the significance of setting in establishing atmosphere and theme.
  • Evaluate the extent to which historical context is necessary for understanding a literary text.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Texts are shaped by cultural, historical, and geographical context.
  • Global issues for IO must be specific and present in both texts.
  • Setting carries symbolic weight — never treat it as mere background.
  • Authorial choices about time (structure) and space (setting) create meaning.
  • IO: balanced treatment of two texts with explicit global issue focus.

Exam Tips

  • For the IO, prepare a clear thesis statement linking both texts to your global issue.
  • Use specific quotations with analysis — not summary.
  • In the IOC, speak for the full 10 minutes — silence loses marks.
  • Address the examiner, not the text — this is a presentation.
  • Practice timing — 10 minutes goes faster than you think.
