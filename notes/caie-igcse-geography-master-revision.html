Migration

📘 IGCSE Content

1.1 Basic Terminology

  • Centre (O): The fixed point equidistant from all points on the circle.
  • Radius (r): Distance from centre to any point on the circle.
  • Diameter (d): A chord passing through the centre. d = 2r.
  • Circumference: C = 2πr = πd.
  • Area: A = πr².
  • Arc: A portion of the circumference.
  • Chord: A line segment joining two points on the circle.
  • Sector: Region bounded by two radii and an arc.
  • Segment: Region bounded by a chord and an arc.
  • Tangent: A line touching the circle at exactly one point.

1.2 Circle Theorems (IGCSE)

Theorem 1 — Angle at Centre: The angle subtended by an arc at the centre is twice the angle subtended at any point on the remaining part of the circumference.

∠AOB = 2 × ∠ACB (where O is centre, C is on circumference)

Theorem 2 — Angle in Semicircle: The angle in a semicircle is a right angle.

If AB is a diameter, then ∠ACB = 90° for any C on the circle.

Theorem 3 — Angles in Same Segment: Angles in the same segment are equal.

If A, B, C, D are on the circle with C and D on the same side of AB, then ∠ACB = ∠ADB.

Theorem 4 — Cyclic Quadrilateral: Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary.

∠A + ∠C = 180° and ∠B + ∠D = 180°

Theorem 5 — Tangent-Radius: A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact.

If PT is tangent at P, then OP ⊥ PT (where O is centre).

Theorem 6 — Alternate Segment: The angle between a tangent and a chord through the point of contact is equal to the angle in the alternate segment.

If PT is tangent and PQ is chord, then ∠TPQ = ∠PRQ (where R is in alternate segment).

Theorem 7 — Equal Chords: Equal chords are equidistant from the centre. Conversely, chords equidistant from the centre are equal.

1.3 Worked Example — Finding Angles

Problem: In a circle with centre O, chord AB subtends 80° at the centre. Point C is on the major arc. Find ∠ACB.

Solution:

By Theorem 1: ∠ACB = ½ × ∠AOB = ½ × 80° = 40°

1.4 Arc Length and Sector Area

Arc length = (θ/360°) × 2πr = rθ (when θ is in radians) Sector area = (θ/360°) × πr² = ½ r²θ (radians) Segment area = Sector area − Triangle area = ½ r²(θ − sin θ)

📗 AS Level Content

2.1 Formal Proofs of Circle Theorems

Proof of Theorem 1 (Angle at Centre):

In ΔAOC, OA = OC (radii), so ∠OAC = ∠OCA = α.

In ΔBOC, OB = OC (radii), so ∠OBC = ∠OCB = β.

∠AOC = 180° − 2α and ∠BOC = 180° − 2β (angle sum of triangles)

∠AOB = 360° − (180° − 2α) − (180° − 2β) = 2(α + β) = 2∠ACB. ∎

Proof of Theorem 4 (Cyclic Quadrilateral):

∠AOB = 2∠ACB and reflex ∠AOB = 2∠ADB (Theorem 1)

∠AOB + reflex ∠AOB = 360°

Therefore 2∠ACB + 2∠ADB = 360°

So ∠ACB + ∠ADB = 180°. ∎

2.2 Intersecting Chords Theorem

If two chords AB and CD intersect at P (inside or outside the circle):

PA × PB = PC × PD

Proof (inside): Triangles APC and DPB are similar (angles in same segment). Therefore PA/PD = PC/PB, giving PA × PB = PC × PD. ∎

2.3 Tangent-Secant Theorem

If tangent PT and secant PAB are drawn from external point P:

PT² = PA × PB

2.4 Power of a Point

For point P relative to circle with centre O and radius r:

Power = OP² − r² = PA × PB (for any line through P intersecting circle at A and B)

If P is inside: power is negative. If P is on circle: power = 0. If P is outside: power is positive.

📙 A2 Level Content

3.1 Circle Equations

Standard form with centre (a, b) and radius r:

(x − a)² + (y − b)² = r²

General form:

x² + y² + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0 Centre: (−g, −f), Radius: √(g² + f² − c)

3.2 Parametric Equations of a Circle

x = a + r cos θ y = b + r sin θ

3.3 Tangent and Normal to a Circle

For circle x² + y² = r² at point (x₁, y₁):

Tangent: xx₁ + yy₁ = r² Normal: y₁x − x₁y = 0

For general circle, the tangent is found by replacing x² → xx₁, y² → yy₁, x → (x+x₁)/2, y → (y+y₁)/2.

3.4 Condition for Tangency

Line y = mx + c is tangent to circle x² + y² = r² if:

c² = r²(1 + m²)

The pair of tangents from external point (x₁, y₁) has equation SS₁ = T² where S = 0 is circle equation, T = 0 is polar/tangent form.

3.5 Director Circle

The locus of points from which perpendicular tangents can be drawn to a circle is called the director circle.

For x² + y² = r², the director circle is x² + y² = 2r².

3.6 Radical Axis and Coaxal Circles

The radical axis of two circles is the locus of points with equal power with respect to both circles. It is a line perpendicular to the line of centres.

Coaxal circles share the same radical axis. The limiting points are point circles of the coaxal system.

📐 Worked Example

Question: Find the interior angle of a regular decagon.

Solution:
n = 10
Interior angle = (n − 2) × 180° / n
= (10 − 2) × 180° / 10 = 8 × 18° = 144°
Answer: 144°
Confusing interior and exterior angles. Exterior angles of ANY polygon always sum to 360°.
For regular polygons: interior + exterior = 180°. Use this to check your answers.

⚡ Quick Recall

  • Interior angle sum = (n − 2) × 180°
  • Exterior angle sum = 360°
  • Regular polygon interior = (n−2)×180°/n
  • Pythagoras: a² + b² = c²

Settlement

📘 IGCSE Content

1.1 Definition of a Locus

A locus (plural: loci) is the set of all points satisfying a given condition. In IGCSE, loci are typically in 2D.

1.2 Standard Loci

  • Locus of points equidistant from a fixed point: A circle with the fixed point as centre.
  • Locus of points equidistant from two fixed points: The perpendicular bisector of the line segment joining them.
  • Locus of points equidistant from two intersecting lines: The pair of angle bisectors.
  • Locus of points at a fixed distance from a line: Two parallel lines, one on each side of the given line.
  • Locus of points equidistant from a point and a line: A parabola.

1.3 Ruler and Compass Constructions

All IGCSE constructions use only an unmarked ruler and compasses:

  • Perpendicular bisector of AB: With centres A and B, draw equal arcs (radius > ½AB) intersecting at P and Q. Line PQ is the perpendicular bisector.
  • Angle bisector of ∠ABC: With centre B, draw arc cutting BA at P and BC at Q. With centres P and Q, draw equal arcs intersecting at R. Line BR bisects the angle.
  • Perpendicular from point P to line l: With centre P, draw arc cutting l at A and B. Construct perpendicular bisector of AB.
  • Perpendicular at point P on line l: With centre P, draw arc cutting l at A and B. Construct perpendicular bisector of AB.
  • Copy an angle: Given ∠ABC, draw ray PQ. With centre B, draw arc cutting BA at D and BC at E. With same radius, centre P, draw arc cutting PQ at R. Measure DE with compasses, mark point S on arc from R. Line PS makes the copied angle.
  • Construct 60° angle: Draw AB. With centres A and B, radius AB, draw arcs intersecting at C. Then ∠CAB = 60°.
  • Construct 90° angle: Construct perpendicular bisector or use the fact that angle in semicircle is 90°.

1.4 Worked Example — Locus Problem

Problem: Points A and B are 8 cm apart. Shade the region of points that are: (a) less than 5 cm from A, and (b) nearer to A than to B.

Solution:

  • (a) Interior of circle centre A, radius 5 cm.
  • (b) Region on A's side of perpendicular bisector of AB.
  • The required region is the intersection: the part of the circle interior that lies on A's side of the perpendicular bisector.

1.5 Construction of Triangles

Given various combinations (SSS, SAS, ASA, RHS), construct the triangle using ruler and compasses. For SSA (ambiguous case), there may be two possible triangles.

📗 AS Level Content

2.1 Locus as Algebraic Equation

In coordinate geometry, a locus can be described by an equation.

  • Locus equidistant from (a, b) and (c, d): perpendicular bisector, linear equation.
  • Locus equidistant from origin: x² + y² = r² (circle).
  • Locus equidistant from point (0, p) and line y = −p: x² = 4py (parabola).

2.2 Derivation of Parabola as Locus

Definition: A parabola is the locus of points equidistant from a fixed point (focus) and a fixed line (directrix).

Let focus be S(a, 0) and directrix be x = −a.

For point P(x, y): PS = PM (where M is foot of perpendicular from P to directrix).

√[(x−a)² + y²] = x + a

Squaring: (x−a)² + y² = (x+a)²

x² − 2ax + a² + y² = x² + 2ax + a²

y² = 4ax

Standard parabola: y² = 4ax (focus at (a,0), directrix x = −a)

2.3 Locus Problems with Circles

Problem: Find the locus of point P such that ∠APB = 90° where A = (−2, 0) and B = (2, 0).

Solution:

Since angle in semicircle = 90°, P lies on circle with AB as diameter.

Centre = (0, 0), radius = 2.

Equation: x² + y² = 4 (excluding points A and B themselves).

2.4 Parametric Loci

A locus may be given parametrically. Eliminate the parameter to find the Cartesian equation.

Example: x = t + 1, y = t². Eliminating t: y = (x − 1)². This is a parabola with vertex at (1, 0).

📙 A2 Level Content

3.1 Conic Sections as Loci

All conic sections can be defined as loci involving a focus and directrix:

Locus: PS = e × PM where e = eccentricity, S = focus, M = foot of perpendicular to directrix.
  • e < 1: Ellipse
  • e = 1: Parabola
  • e > 1: Hyperbola

3.2 Ellipse as Locus

Standard ellipse: x²/a² + y²/b² = 1 (a > b)

Foci: (±ae, 0) where e = √(1 − b²/a²) Directrices: x = ±a/e Sum of distances from foci = 2a

3.3 Hyperbola as Locus

Standard hyperbola: x²/a² − y²/b² = 1

Foci: (±ae, 0) where e = √(1 + b²/a²) Directrices: x = ±a/e Difference of distances from foci = 2a Asymptotes: y = ±(b/a)x

3.4 Loci in 3D

  • Locus equidistant from a point: Sphere.
  • Locus equidistant from two points: Plane (perpendicular bisector plane).
  • Locus equidistant from a point and plane: Paraboloid.
  • Locus equidistant from two intersecting planes: Pair of planes (angle bisectors).

3.5 Complex Number Loci

For complex number z = x + iy:

|z − z₁| = r: Circle centre z₁, radius r |z − z₁| = |z − z₂|: Perpendicular bisector of z₁z₂ |z − z₁| + |z − z₂| = 2a: Ellipse with foci z₁, z₂ arg(z − z₁) = θ: Ray from z₁ at angle θ

Question: Find the interior angle of a regular decagon.

Solution:
n = 10
Interior angle = (n − 2) × 180° / n
= (10 − 2) × 180° / 10 = 8 × 18° = 144°
Answer: 144°
Confusing interior and exterior angles. Exterior angles of ANY polygon always sum to 360°.
For regular polygons: interior + exterior = 180°. Use this to check your answers.

⚡ Quick Recall

  • Interior angle sum = (n − 2) × 180°
  • Exterior angle sum = 360°
  • Regular polygon interior = (n−2)×180°/n
  • Pythagoras: a² + b² = c²

📝 Last-Minute Summary

  • Locus = set of points satisfying a condition
  • Equidistant from point: circle (sphere in 3D)
  • Equidistant from two points: perpendicular bisector (plane in 3D)
  • Equidistant from two lines: angle bisectors
  • Equidistant from point and line: parabola
  • Constructions: perpendicular bisector, angle bisector, copy angle, 60°, perpendicular from/to point
  • Parabola: y² = 4ax, focus (a,0), directrix x = −a
  • Ellipse: x²/a² + y²/b² = 1, foci (±ae,0), e = √(1−b²/a²)
  • Hyperbola: x²/a² − y²/b² = 1, foci (±ae,0), e = √(1+b²/a²)
  • Complex loci: |z−z₁|=r (circle), |z−z₁|=|z−z₂| (perp. bisector), arg(z−z₁)=θ (ray)

Tropical Environments

📘 IGCSE Content

1.1 Vector Basics

A vector has both magnitude and direction. Scalars have magnitude only.

Notation: **a**, a⃗, or AB⃗ (vector from A to B).

A vector in component form: **v** = (x, y) in 2D or (x, y, z) in 3D.

1.2 Vector Operations

Addition: (a, b) + (c, d) = (a+c, b+d) Subtraction: (a, b) − (c, d) = (a−c, b−d) Scalar multiplication: k(a, b) = (ka, kb) Magnitude: |**v**| = √(x² + y²) or √(x² + y² + z²)

1.3 Position Vectors

If O is the origin, the position vector of point P is OP⃗.

The vector from A to B is: AB⃗ = OB⃗ − OA⃗ = **b** − **a**

1.4 Worked Example — Position Vectors

Problem: A = (2, 3), B = (5, 7). Find vector AB⃗ and its magnitude.

Solution:

AB⃗ = (5−2, 7−3) = (3, 4)

|AB⃗| = √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5

1.5 Unit Vectors

A unit vector has magnitude 1. The unit vector in direction of **v** is:

**v̂** = **v** / |**v**|

Standard unit vectors: **i** = (1,0,0), **j** = (0,1,0), **k** = (0,0,1)

1.6 Section Formula (IGCSE Extended)

Point P dividing AB in ratio m:n has position vector:

**p** = (n**a** + m**b**) / (m + n)

For midpoint: **p** = (**a** + **b**) / 2

📗 AS Level Content

2.1 Scalar (Dot) Product

**a** · **b** = |**a**| |**b**| cos θ = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + a₃b₃

Properties:

  • Commutative: **a** · **b** = **b** · **a**
  • **a** · **a** = |**a**|²
  • If **a** ⊥ **b**, then **a** · **b** = 0

2.2 Finding Angles Between Vectors

cos θ = (**a** · **b**) / (|**a**| |**b**|)

Worked Example: Find angle between **a** = (1, 2, 2) and **b** = (3, 0, 4).

**a** · **b** = 3 + 0 + 8 = 11

|**a**| = √(1+4+4) = 3, |**b**| = √(9+0+16) = 5

cos θ = 11/15, so θ = cos⁻¹(11/15) ≈ 42.8°

2.3 Vector Equation of a Line

**r** = **a** + λ**d**

where **a** is a point on the line, **d** is the direction vector, and λ is a scalar parameter.

Cartesian form: (x − a₁)/d₁ = (y − a₂)/d₂ = (z − a₃)/d₃

2.4 Finding Line Intersections

Set the parametric equations equal and solve for parameters. If consistent: lines intersect. If inconsistent: lines are skew (in 3D) or parallel.

2.5 Shortest Distance from Point to Line

For line **r** = **a** + λ**d** and point P with position vector **p**:

Distance = |(**p** − **a**) × **d**| / |**d**|

📙 A2 Level Content

3.1 Vector (Cross) Product

**a** × **b** = |**a**| |**b**| sin θ **n̂** where **n̂** is unit vector perpendicular to both **a** and **b** (right-hand rule).

In components:

**a** × **b** = (a₂b₃ − a₃b₂, a₃b₁ − a₁b₃, a₁b₂ − a₂b₁)

Properties:

  • Anti-commutative: **a** × **b** = −(**b** × **a**)
  • **a** × **a** = **0**
  • |**a** × **b**| = area of parallelogram with sides **a** and **b**

3.2 Scalar Triple Product

[**a**, **b**, **c**] = **a** · (**b** × **c**) = det[**a** **b** **c**]

This gives the volume of the parallelepiped with edges **a**, **b**, **c**.

If [**a**, **b**, **c**] = 0, the vectors are coplanar.

3.3 Vector Equation of a Plane

**r** · **n** = d (where **n** is normal vector) or **r** = **a** + λ**b** + μ**c** (parametric form)

Cartesian form: ax + by + cz = d, where (a,b,c) = **n**

3.4 Distance from Point to Plane

Distance = |**n** · **p** − d| / |**n**| = |ax₀ + by₀ + cz₀ − d| / √(a²+b²+c²)

3.5 Line-Plane Intersection

Substitute line equation **r** = **a** + λ**d** into plane equation **r** · **n** = d.

Solve for λ. If **d** · **n** = 0: line is parallel to plane (no intersection or line lies in plane).

3.6 Vector Geometry Proofs

Example: Prove that the medians of a triangle are concurrent (centroid divides each median in ratio 2:1).

Proof: Let vertices have position vectors **a**, **b**, **c**.

Midpoint of BC: **m** = (**b** + **c**)/2

Point on median from A dividing it 2:1: (**a** + 2**m**)/3 = (**a** + **b** + **c**)/3

By symmetry, this same point lies on all three medians. ∎

  • Vector magnitude: |**v**| = √(x²+y²+z²)

  • Section formula: **p** = (n**a** + m**b**)/(m+n)

Coastal Environments

📘 IGCSE Content

1.1 Types of Transformations

  • Translation: Sliding without rotation or resizing. Described by vector (a, b).
  • Reflection: Mirror image across a line. The mirror line is the perpendicular bisector of any segment joining a point to its image.
  • Rotation: Turning about a fixed point (centre) through a given angle. Positive = anticlockwise.
  • Enlargement: Scaling from a centre point by scale factor k. If k < 0, the image is on the opposite side of the centre.

1.2 Describing Transformations

Always state:

  • For translation: the column vector.
  • For reflection: the equation of the mirror line.
  • For rotation: the centre, angle, and direction (clockwise/anticlockwise).
  • For enlargement: the centre and scale factor.

1.3 Combining Transformations

Transformations are performed right to left. If T₁ followed by T₂, the combined transformation is T₂ ∘ T₁.

Note: The order matters! Reflection then rotation is generally different from rotation then reflection.

1.4 Worked Example — Describing a Transformation

Problem: Triangle ABC with A(1,1), B(3,1), C(2,3) is mapped to A′(−1,1), B′(−3,1), C′(−2,3). Describe the transformation.

Solution:

Points are reflected across the y-axis (x = 0). The y-coordinates stay the same, x-coordinates change sign.

Transformation: Reflection in the line x = 0 (the y-axis).

1.5 Matrix Representation (IGCSE Additional)

A point (x, y) can be represented as a column vector (x, y)ᵀ.

A 2×2 matrix transforms points by multiplication:

(x′, y′)ᵀ = M (x, y)ᵀ

📗 AS Level Content

2.1 Standard Transformation Matrices

Reflection in x-axis: [1 0; 0 −1] Reflection in y-axis: [−1 0; 0 1] Reflection in y = x: [0 1; 1 0] Reflection in y = −x: [0 −1; −1 0] Rotation 90° anticlockwise about O: [0 −1; 1 0] Rotation 180° about O: [−1 0; 0 −1] Rotation θ about O: [cos θ −sin θ; sin θ cos θ] Enlargement scale factor k, centre O: [k 0; 0 k] Stretch x-direction factor a: [a 0; 0 1] Stretch y-direction factor b: [1 0; 0 b] Shear x-direction factor k: [1 k; 0 1]

2.2 Matrix Multiplication for Combined Transformations

If T₁ has matrix M₁ and T₂ has matrix M₂, then T₂ followed by T₁ has matrix M₁M₂.

Note: Matrix multiplication is not commutative! M₁M₂ ≠ M₂M₁ in general.

2.3 Determinant and Area Scale Factor

For matrix M = [a b; c d]: det(M) = ad − bc Area of image = |det(M)| × Area of object

If det(M) > 0: transformation preserves orientation (direct).

If det(M) < 0: transformation reverses orientation (opposite).

2.4 Inverse Transformations

The inverse of transformation matrix M is:

M⁻¹ = (1/det(M)) [d −b; −c a]

The inverse represents the transformation that maps the image back to the original.

2.5 Invariant Points and Lines

  • Invariant point: A point that maps to itself.
  • Invariant line: A line that maps to itself (points on it may move, but stay on the line).

For matrix M, invariant points satisfy M**v** = **v**, i.e., (M − I)**v** = **0**.

📙 A2 Level Content

3.1 3D Transformation Matrices

Transformations in 3D use 3×3 matrices:

Rotation about z-axis by θ: [cos θ −sin θ 0; sin θ cos θ 0; 0 0 1] Rotation about x-axis by θ: [1 0 0; 0 cos θ −sin θ; 0 sin θ cos θ]

3.2 Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

An eigenvector of matrix M is a non-zero vector **v** such that:

M**v** = λ**v

where λ is the eigenvalue.

Eigenvectors represent invariant directions. Eigenvalues represent the scale factor in those directions.

To find eigenvalues: solve det(M − λI) = 0 (characteristic equation).

3.3 Diagonalisation

If matrix M has eigenvectors **v₁**, **v₂** with eigenvalues λ₁, λ₂, then:

M = PDP⁻¹ where P = [**v₁** **v₂**] and D = diag(λ₁, λ₂)

This simplifies computing Mⁿ: Mⁿ = PDⁿP⁻¹.

3.4 Affine Transformations

An affine transformation preserves collinearity and ratios of distances along lines. It combines linear transformations with translations.

**x′** = M**x** + **t**

Using homogeneous coordinates (x, y, 1), affine transformations can be represented as 3×3 matrices:

[x′; y′; 1] = [M t; 0 0 1] [x; y; 1]

3.5 Singular Value Decomposition (Introduction)

Any real matrix M can be decomposed as M = UΣVᵀ where U and V are orthogonal and Σ is diagonal. This reveals the fundamental geometric action: rotation, scaling along axes, then rotation.

📐 Worked Example

Question: Find the interior angle of a regular decagon.

Solution:
n = 10
Interior angle = (n − 2) × 180° / n
= (10 − 2) × 180° / 10 = 8 × 18° = 144°
Answer: 144°
Confusing interior and exterior angles. Exterior angles of ANY polygon always sum to 360°.
For regular polygons: interior + exterior = 180°. Use this to check your answers.

⚡ Quick Recall

  • Interior angle sum = (n − 2) × 180°
  • Exterior angle sum = 360°
  • Regular polygon interior = (n−2)×180°/n

📝 Last-Minute Summary

  • Translation: vector (a, b)
  • Reflection: state mirror line
  • Rotation: state centre, angle, direction
  • Enlargement: state centre, scale factor k
  • Rotation matrix: [cos θ −sin θ; sin θ cos θ]
  • Reflection in y=x: [0 1; 1 0]
  • Determinant: ad − bc = area scale factor
  • Inverse: (1/det) [d −b; −c a]
  • Combined transformations: right to left, matrix multiply
  • Eigenvalue: M**v** = λ**v
  • Characteristic equation: det(M − λI) = 0
  • 3D rotations: about x, y, or z axis using 3×3 matrices
