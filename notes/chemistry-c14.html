Enzymes and Enzyme Kinetics

Enzymes are biological catalysts, typically proteins, that dramatically increase the rate of biochemical reactions without being consumed. They exhibit remarkable specificity and efficiency.

📘 Key Definitions

Enzyme: A biological catalyst that speeds up metabolic reactions.

Active site: The region of an enzyme where substrate molecules bind and undergo chemical transformation.

Substrate: The molecule upon which an enzyme acts.

Lock and key model: The substrate fits precisely into the active site like a key in a lock.

Induced fit model: The active site changes shape slightly to accommodate the substrate.

Enzyme Kinetics — Michaelis-Menten Curve:

Rate increases linearly with [S] at low substrate concentration, then levels off to Vmax at high [S] when all enzyme molecules are saturated.

Km (Michaelis constant): The substrate concentration at which the reaction rate is half of Vmax. A low Km indicates high affinity of the enzyme for its substrate.
Competitive inhibitors bind to the active site, competing with substrate. They can be overcome by increasing substrate concentration. Non-competitive inhibitors bind elsewhere, changing the enzyme's shape and reducing Vmax. They cannot be overcome by adding more substrate.
Worked Example: Enzyme Inhibition

Malonate is a competitive inhibitor of succinate dehydrogenase. Explain how malonate inhibits the enzyme and how the inhibition can be overcome.

Solution: Malonate has a similar shape to succinate and competes for the active site. It binds reversibly. Increasing the concentration of succinate can overcome the inhibition by outcompeting malonate for the active site.

Photosynthesis and Respiration

Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose.

6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂     (requires light and chlorophyll)

Light-dependent reactions: Occur in thylakoid membranes; produce ATP and NADPH; water is split (photolysis) to release O₂.

Light-independent reactions (Calvin cycle): Occur in stroma; CO₂ is fixed by RuBisCO enzyme and reduced to glucose using ATP and NADPH.

Cellular Respiration

Respiration releases energy from glucose through a series of controlled oxidation reactions.

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O     ΔH = −2808 kJ mol⁻¹

Glycolysis: Glucose → 2 pyruvate (cytoplasm, anaerobic)
Krebs cycle: Pyruvate → CO₂ (mitochondrial matrix)
Electron transport chain: Produces ATP using proton gradient (inner mitochondrial membrane)
Aerobic respiration produces ~38 ATP per glucose. Anaerobic respiration produces only 2 ATP per glucose (lactate in animals, ethanol and CO₂ in yeast).

Atmospheric Chemistry

The Ozone Layer

Ozone (O₃) in the stratosphere absorbs harmful UV radiation (200–315 nm), protecting life on Earth.

Ozone formation: O₂ + UV → 2O•; O• + O₂ → O₃
Ozone destruction (natural): O₃ + UV → O₂ + O•; O₃ + O• → 2O₂
CFC destruction: CCl₂F₂ + UV → CClF₂• + Cl•
Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂
ClO• + O• → Cl• + O₂
The chlorine radical is regenerated — it acts as a catalyst, destroying many ozone molecules.
One chlorine atom from CFCs can destroy up to 100,000 ozone molecules. The Montreal Protocol (1987) phased out CFC production. Replacement compounds like HFCs don't contain chlorine but are potent greenhouse gases.

The Greenhouse Effect

Greenhouse gases (CO₂, CH₄, H₂O vapour, N₂O, CFCs) absorb infrared radiation emitted by Earth's surface and re-radiate it back, warming the atmosphere.

Water Treatment and Pollution

Water Treatment Process

  1. Screening: Remove large debris.
  2. Coagulation and flocculation: Add alum (aluminium sulfate) to make suspended particles clump together.
  3. Sedimentation: Heavy particles settle to the bottom.
  4. Filtration: Pass through sand and gravel beds.
  5. Disinfection: Add chlorine or use UV/ozone to kill microorganisms.
  6. pH adjustment: Adjust to prevent pipe corrosion.

Eutrophication is the excessive growth of algae due to nutrient enrichment (nitrates and phosphates). When algae die, their decomposition by bacteria depletes dissolved oxygen, killing fish and other aquatic organisms.

Green Chemistry

📘 Key Definition

Green chemistry: The design of chemical products and processes that reduce or eliminate the use and generation of hazardous substances.

Principles of Green Chemistry

  1. Prevention — prevent waste rather than treat it
  2. Atom economy — maximise incorporation of reactants into products
  3. Less hazardous synthesis — use non-toxic substances
  4. Design safer chemicals — preserve function while minimising toxicity
  5. Safer solvents — use water or benign solvents
  6. Energy efficiency — conduct at ambient T and P
  7. Use renewable feedstocks
  8. Reduce derivatives — minimise temporary modifications
  9. Catalysis — use selective catalysts
  10. Design for degradation — biodegradable products
  11. Real-time analysis — monitor pollution
  12. Inherently safer chemistry — minimise accident potential
Atom Economy:
Atom economy = (molecular mass of desired product / sum of molecular masses of all reactants) × 100%

Example: Hydrogenation of ethene
C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆
Atom economy = 30 / (28 + 2) × 100% = 100%

Example: Substitution
CH₃CH₂OH + HBr → CH₃CH₂Br + H₂O
Atom economy = 109 / (46 + 81) × 100% = 85.8%
Worked Example: Comparing Atom Economies

Route 1: CH₃CH₂OH + PCl₅ → CH₃CH₂Cl + POCl₃ + HCl

Route 2: CH₃CH₂OH + SOCl₂ → CH₃CH₂Cl + SO₂ + HCl

Route 3: CH₃CH₂OH + HCl → CH₃CH₂Cl + H₂O

Route 3 has the highest atom economy (only water as by-product).

Exam-Style Questions

📝 Exam Question 1

(a) Explain why CFCs are effective at destroying ozone in the stratosphere. [3]

(b) Write equations to show how chlorine radicals catalyse ozone destruction. [2]

Mark Scheme:
(a) CFCs are stable in troposphere / unreactive (1). UV light in stratosphere breaks C-Cl bond releasing Cl• (1). Cl• regenerated in catalytic cycle / one Cl• destroys many O₃ molecules (1).
(b) Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂ (1). ClO• + O• → Cl• + O₂ (1).
📝 Exam Question 2

(a) Define atom economy. [1]

(b) Calculate the atom economy for the production of 1-bromobutane from butan-1-ol using HBr. [2]

(c) Suggest why atom economy is an important consideration in green chemistry. [1]

Mark Scheme:
(a) (Molar mass of desired product / sum of molar masses of all reactants) × 100% (1).
(b) C₄H₉OH + HBr → C₄H₉Br + H₂O (1). Atom economy = 137 / (74 + 81) × 100% = 88.4% (1).
(c) Reduces waste / more sustainable / less environmental impact (1).

📝 Quick Summary

  • Enzymes: biological catalysts; active site binds substrate; lock-and-key / induced fit
  • Enzyme activity affected by temperature, pH, substrate concentration, inhibitors
  • Competitive inhibition: overcome by increasing [S]; non-competitive: cannot be overcome
  • Photosynthesis: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ (light-dependent + Calvin cycle)
  • Respiration: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ATP
  • Ozone: absorbs UV; destroyed by Cl• from CFCs in catalytic cycle
  • Greenhouse gases: CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, CFCs; trap IR radiation
  • Water treatment: screening → coagulation → sedimentation → filtration → disinfection
  • Eutrophication: excess nutrients → algal bloom → oxygen depletion
  • Atom economy = (mass desired product / mass all reactants) × 100%

💡 Exam Tips

  • Distinguish clearly between competitive and non-competitive inhibition
  • Remember that catalysts are regenerated — show this in ozone equations
  • Greenhouse effect: absorption and re-radiation of IR, not trapping
  • Atom economy differs from percentage yield — it's about waste reduction
  • Eutrophication sequence: nutrients → algae → death → decomposition → O₂ depletion → death
