Biological Approach

IB DP Psychology HL/SL — Approach 1

Overview

The Biological Approach (Approach 1) examines how biological structures and processes influence behaviour. It is assessed in Paper 1 (short answer and essay) and is a compulsory topic for both SL and HL students. Understanding the interaction of biology and behaviour is essential for the IA, where biological variables may be investigated.

1.1 Brain Anatomy & Behaviour

  • Localisation of function: specific brain areas control specific functions.
    • Frontal lobe: decision-making, planning, personality (Phineas Gage case study).
    • Temporal lobe: auditory processing, language (Wernicke's area).
    • Parietal lobe: spatial awareness, somatosensory processing.
    • Occipital lobe: visual processing.
    • Motor cortex: voluntary movement.
    • Broca's area: speech production.
  • Neuroplasticity: the brain's ability to reorganise itself by forming new neural connections (e.g., recovery from stroke, London taxi drivers study — Maguire et al.).

1.2 Neurotransmitters

  • Serotonin: mood regulation, sleep, appetite. Low levels linked to depression (SSRI drugs increase serotonin).
  • Dopamine: reward, motivation, motor control. Excess linked to schizophrenia; deficit linked to Parkinson's disease.
  • Acetylcholine (ACh): learning and memory. Deficit linked to Alzheimer's disease.
  • Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA): inhibitory neurotransmitter; low levels linked to anxiety.

Worked Example: Neurotransmitter Explanation

Question: Explain how dopamine may be involved in schizophrenia.

Answer:

The dopamine hypothesis suggests that excess dopamine activity in the mesolimbic pathway causes positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions). Antipsychotic drugs (e.g., chlorpromazine) block dopamine D2 receptors, reducing symptoms. However, the hypothesis does not fully explain negative symptoms, suggesting other neurotransmitters (e.g., glutamate) are also involved.

1.3 Hormones

  • Cortisol: stress hormone released by adrenal glands. Chronic elevation linked to impaired immune function and memory (hippocampus damage).
  • Adrenaline (epinephrine): fight-or-flight response; increases heart rate and glucose availability.
  • Oxytocin: bonding, trust, social behaviour ("love hormone").
  • Testosterone: linked to aggression (correlational evidence from prisoners).

1.4 Genetics & Behaviour

  • Twin studies: compare concordance rates between monozygotic (MZ, identical) and dizygotic (DZ, fraternal) twins.
    • Higher MZ concordance suggests genetic influence.
    • Bouchard's Minnesota Twin Study: MZ twins reared apart showed significant behavioural similarities.
  • Adoption studies: compare adopted children with biological and adoptive parents to separate genetic and environmental effects.
  • Gene–environment interaction: diathesis-stress model — genetic vulnerability + environmental trigger = disorder.

1.5 Physiology of Behaviour

  • Brain imaging: fMRI (functional), PET (metabolic activity), EEG (electrical activity).
  • Lesion studies: examining behaviour after brain damage (e.g., HM — hippocampus removal caused anterograde amnesia).

Common Misconceptions

  • Localisation: Functions are localised but also distributed — the brain works as an integrated system.
  • Correlation = causation: A correlation between neurotransmitter levels and behaviour does not prove causation.
  • Twin studies: MZ twins share 100% of genes but also often share more similar environments than DZ twins — this is a confounding variable.

Exam-Specific Information

  • Paper 1 (Section A): Three short-answer questions (8–10 marks each) on biological concepts.
  • Paper 1 (Section B): One essay (22 marks) comparing the biological approach with another approach.
  • IA: Biological experiments (e.g., stress hormones, reaction time) are possible but require ethical approval.

Practice Questions

  • Explain how one neurotransmitter influences behaviour, using one empirical study.
  • Discuss the use of brain imaging technology in understanding the relationship between biology and behaviour.
  • Evaluate twin studies as evidence for the genetic basis of behaviour.
  • Explain the diathesis-stress model using a specific example.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Localisation: specific brain areas control specific functions (frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital lobes).
  • Neuroplasticity: brain reorganises in response to experience or damage.
  • Neurotransmitters: serotonin (mood), dopamine (reward/schizophrenia), ACh (memory), GABA (inhibition).
  • Hormones: cortisol (stress), adrenaline (fight-or-flight), oxytocin (bonding), testosterone (aggression).
  • Genetics: twin and adoption studies; gene–environment interaction (diathesis-stress).

Exam Tips

  • Always name and briefly describe empirical studies — marks are awarded for evidence.
  • For evaluation, consider methodological limitations (e.g., animal studies, correlational data).
  • Use biological terminology precisely (e.g., "synapse," "receptor," "neurotransmitter").
  • In essays, integrate biological explanations with other levels of analysis.
  • Clearly distinguish between structure (anatomy) and function (physiology).
