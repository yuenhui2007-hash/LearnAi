Cell Division

9700 · 0610

Detailed Notes

1

The Cell Cycle

The cell cycle is the series of events that take place in a cell leading to its division and duplication. It consists of interphase (G₁, S, G₂) and the mitotic phase (mitosis and cytokinesis).

Interphase: The period between cell divisions when the cell grows, carries out normal metabolic activities, and replicates its DNA. It accounts for about 90% of the cell cycle.

Stages of Interphase

  • G₁ phase (Gap 1): Cell grows, synthesises proteins and organelles, carries out normal functions. This is the primary growth phase. At the end of G₁, there is a checkpoint (restriction point) where the cell decides whether to divide.
  • S phase (Synthesis): DNA replication occurs. Each chromosome is duplicated to form two identical sister chromatids joined at the centromere. Histone proteins are also synthesised.
  • G₂ phase (Gap 2): Cell continues to grow, synthesises proteins needed for division (e.g., tubulin for spindle fibres). Another checkpoint ensures DNA replication is complete and damage is repaired.
A cell in G₀ phase has exited the cell cycle and no longer divides. Most neurons and muscle cells are in G₀ permanently. Some cells (e.g., liver cells) can re-enter the cycle if needed.
2

Mitosis

Mitosis: Nuclear division that produces two genetically identical daughter nuclei, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent nucleus. It is followed by cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm).

Mitosis produces two identical diploid cells from one diploid cell. It is used for growth, repair, and asexual reproduction.

The Stages of Mitosis (PMAT)

Prophase
  1. Chromatin condenses and coils to become visible as chromosomes (each composed of two sister chromatids joined at centromere).
  2. Nuclear envelope breaks down and disappears.
  3. Nucleolus disappears.
  4. Centrioles (in animal cells) move to opposite poles of the cell.
  5. Spindle fibres (microtubules) begin to form between the centrioles.
Metaphase
  1. Chromosomes line up along the equator (metaphase plate) of the cell.
  2. Spindle fibres from each pole attach to the centromeres of the chromosomes.
  3. Each chromosome is attached to both poles (one chromatid to each pole).
Anaphase
  1. Centromeres divide, separating sister chromatids.
  2. Chromatids are now individual chromosomes.
  3. Spindle fibres shorten, pulling chromosomes toward opposite poles.
  4. The cell elongates as polar microtubules push poles apart.
Telophase
  1. Chromosomes reach opposite poles and begin to uncoil (decondense) back into chromatin.
  2. Nuclear envelope re-forms around each set of chromosomes.
  3. Nucleolus reappears.
  4. Spindle fibres break down.
  5. Cytokinesis begins — division of the cytoplasm.

Cytokinesis

  • Animal cells: A cleavage furrow forms as a ring of actin filaments contracts, pinching the cell in two.
  • Plant cells: A cell plate forms across the equator, developing into a new cell wall with plasmodesmata connecting adjacent cells.
At the end of mitosis and cytokinesis, two genetically identical diploid daughter cells are produced. Each has the same number and type of chromosomes as the parent cell.
Worked Example: Identifying Mitosis Stages

Question: A cell is observed with chromosomes aligned at the equator and spindle fibres attached to centromeres. Name the stage and describe what happens next. [3 marks]

Answer:

  • This is metaphase (1).
  • In the next stage (anaphase), the centromeres divide and sister chromatids separate (1).
  • Spindle fibres shorten, pulling the chromatids (now chromosomes) to opposite poles (1).
3

Meiosis

Meiosis: Nuclear division that reduces the chromosome number by half, producing four genetically different haploid cells from one diploid cell. It involves two divisions: meiosis I and meiosis II.

Meiosis is essential for sexual reproduction because it produces gametes (sperm and egg cells) with half the chromosome number. When gametes fuse during fertilisation, the diploid number is restored.

Meiosis I (Reduction Division)

The chromosome number is halved from diploid (2n) to haploid (n).

Prophase I
  1. Chromatin condenses into chromosomes (each with two sister chromatids).
  2. Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis) to form bivalents.
  3. Crossing over occurs: non-sister chromatids exchange segments of DNA at chiasmata. This creates new combinations of alleles (genetic recombination).
  4. Nuclear envelope breaks down; spindle forms.
Metaphase I
  1. Bivalents line up at the equator.
  2. Spindle fibres attach to centromeres.
  3. Independent assortment: The orientation of each bivalent is random — maternal or paternal chromosome can face either pole. This creates genetic variation.
Anaphase I
  1. Homologous chromosomes separate and move to opposite poles.
  2. Sister chromatids remain attached at centromeres.
Telophase I
  1. Chromosomes reach poles; nuclear envelope may reform.
  2. Cytokinesis divides the cell into two haploid cells (each chromosome still has two chromatids).

Meiosis II (Equational Division)

Similar to mitosis. Separates sister chromatids.

Prophase II → Metaphase II → Anaphase II → Telophase II
  1. Chromosomes condense again (if they decondensed).
  2. Chromosomes line up at equator (metaphase II).
  3. Sister chromatids separate (anaphase II).
  4. Four haploid cells form after cytokinesis.
Meiosis produces 4 genetically different haploid cells. The genetic variation comes from: (1) crossing over in prophase I, and (2) independent assortment in metaphase I.
Worked Example: Comparing Mitosis and Meiosis

Question: Compare mitosis and meiosis in terms of the number and genetic composition of daughter cells produced. [4 marks]

Answer:

  • Mitosis produces two daughter cells; meiosis produces four daughter cells (1).
  • Mitosis produces diploid cells (2n); meiosis produces haploid cells (n) (1).
  • Mitosis produces genetically identical daughter cells; meiosis produces genetically different daughter cells (1).
  • Meiosis involves crossing over and independent assortment, which creates genetic variation; mitosis does not (1).
Common Misconception: Many students think crossing over occurs in mitosis. Crossing over ONLY occurs during prophase I of meiosis. Also, remember that in meiosis I, homologous chromosomes separate; in meiosis II, sister chromatids separate (like mitosis).
4

Binary Fission

Binary fission: A form of asexual reproduction in prokaryotes where a cell divides into two genetically identical daughter cells.
Steps of Binary Fission
  1. The circular DNA replicates — each daughter cell will receive one copy.
  2. The cell elongates and the two DNA molecules move to opposite poles.
  3. The cell wall and membrane begin to grow inward at the centre.
  4. A new cell wall forms, dividing the cell into two identical daughter cells.
Binary fission is rapid — some bacteria can divide every 20 minutes under optimal conditions. This exponential growth can lead to very large populations quickly.
🔬 Real-World Application: Cancer and Uncontrolled Cell Division

Cancer arises when the normal control mechanisms of the cell cycle fail. Mutations in genes that regulate the cell cycle (oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes) can cause cells to divide uncontrollably. Understanding mitosis and its regulation is essential for developing cancer treatments such as chemotherapy (which targets rapidly dividing cells) and radiation therapy.

Last-Minute Summary

  • Cell cycle: Interphase (G₁, S, G₂) + mitotic phase (mitosis + cytokinesis)
  • Mitosis: PMAT — Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase. Produces 2 identical diploid cells.
  • Meiosis: Two divisions. Produces 4 genetically different haploid cells.
  • Crossing over: Occurs in prophase I of meiosis — exchanges DNA between non-sister chromatids, creating recombinant chromosomes.
  • Independent assortment: Random orientation of bivalents at metaphase I creates genetic variation.
  • Binary fission: Prokaryotic cell division — DNA replicates, cell divides into two identical cells.
  • Cytokinesis: Animal cells form cleavage furrow; plant cells form cell plate.

💡 Exam Tips

  • In mitosis, chromosome number stays the same; in meiosis, it halves
  • Crossing over occurs during prophase I of meiosis, NOT mitosis
  • Independent assortment in metaphase I increases genetic variation
  • Always describe what happens at each stage in the correct order
  • Distinguish clearly: mitosis = growth/repair; meiosis = gamete formation
  • Remember that after meiosis I, cells are haploid (n) but chromosomes still have two chromatids
  • After meiosis II, each gamete has one chromatid (one copy of each chromosome)
  • Plant cytokinesis = cell plate; animal cytokinesis = cleavage furrow
