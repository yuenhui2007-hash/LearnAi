General Properties of Transition Metals

Transition metals are d-block elements that form at least one stable ion with a partially filled d subshell. The first transition series is Sc to Zn (period 4). They exhibit characteristic properties due to their d electrons.

📘 Key Definitions

Transition element: A d-block element that forms one or more stable ions with a partially filled d subshell.

Scandium and zinc are NOT transition elements: Sc forms only Sc³⁺ ([Ar]), Zn forms only Zn²⁺ ([Ar] 3d¹⁰) — neither has a partially filled d subshell in their only common ion.

Characteristic Properties

PropertyExplanation
Variable oxidation statesSuccessive ionisation energies are similar; 4s and 3d electrons have similar energies
Coloured compoundsd-d transitions absorb visible light; colour is complementary to absorbed wavelength
Catalytic activityVariable oxidation states allow formation of unstable intermediates; surface adsorption
Formation of complex ionsPartially filled d orbitals and empty/occupied orbitals can accept lone pairs from ligands
ParamagnetismUnpaired d electrons create magnetic moments
Hard, high density, high mpStrong metallic bonding from delocalised d electrons

Variable Oxidation States

Transition metals can exist in multiple oxidation states because the energy difference between successive ionisation energies is relatively small. The 4s and 3d orbitals are close in energy.

Worked Example: Redox Reactions of Transition Metals

Manganate(VII) titration with iron(II):

MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O + 5Fe³⁺

Explanation: Purple MnO₄⁻ is reduced to colourless/pale pink Mn²⁺. Fe²⁺ is oxidised to Fe³⁺. This is used to determine the concentration of Fe²⁺ solutions.

When balancing redox equations involving transition metals: 1. Identify oxidation states 2. Write half-equations for oxidation and reduction 3. Balance atoms (add H₂O for O, H⁺ for H in acidic solution) 4. Balance charge by adding electrons 5. Multiply half-equations to equalise electrons 6. Add together and simplify

Complex Ions and Ligands

📘 Key Definitions

Complex ion: A central metal ion surrounded by ligands.

Ligand: A molecule or ion that donates a lone pair of electrons to a metal ion to form a coordinate (dative) bond.

Coordination number: The number of coordinate bonds to the central metal ion.

Monodentate ligand: Forms one coordinate bond (e.g., H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻).

Bidentate ligand: Forms two coordinate bonds (e.g., ethanedioate C₂O₄²⁻, 1,2-diaminoethane NH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂).

Worked Example: Ligand Substitution

When excess NH₃ is added to aqueous copper(II) sulfate:

[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺(aq) + 4NH₃(aq) → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺(aq) + 4H₂O(l)

Colour change: pale blue → deep blue

This is a ligand substitution reaction. NH₃ is a stronger ligand than H₂O.

Ligand substitution can also cause colour changes: • [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ (blue) + 4Cl⁻ → [CuCl₄]²⁻ (yellow) + 6H₂O • [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ (pink) + 4Cl⁻ → [CoCl₄]²⁻ (blue) + 6H₂O These are used as tests for the presence of different ligands.

Catalytic Activity

Transition metals and their compounds are excellent catalysts due to their ability to show variable oxidation states and adsorb reactants onto their surfaces.

Homogeneous Catalysis

Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ Catalysis of S₂O₈²⁻/I⁻ Reaction

S₂O₈²⁻ + 2I⁻ → 2SO₄²⁻ + I₂ (slow uncatalysed)

Catalysed route:

Step 1: S₂O₈²⁻ + 2Fe²⁺ → 2SO₄²⁻ + 2Fe³⁺

Step 2: 2Fe³⁺ + 2I⁻ → 2Fe²⁺ + I₂

Fe²⁺ is oxidised to Fe³⁺, then reduced back to Fe²⁺.

Heterogeneous Catalysis

ProcessCatalystReaction
Haber processIronN₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃
Contact processV₂O₅2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃
HydrogenationNiC=C + H₂ → C-C
Catalytic converterPt/Pd/Rh2CO + 2NO → 2CO₂ + N₂
Heterogeneous Catalysis Mechanism
  1. Reactant molecules adsorb onto the catalyst surface.
  2. Bonds in reactants weaken; new bonds begin to form.
  3. Product molecules desorb from the surface.

Colour in Transition Metal Compounds

Colour arises when d electrons absorb visible light and are promoted to higher energy d orbitals (d-d transitions). The colour observed is the complement of the colour absorbed.

Crystal Field Theory (simplified):
In an octahedral complex, the d orbitals split into two sets:
• t₂g (lower energy): dxy, dxz, dyz
• eg (higher energy): dx²-y², dz²
ΔE = energy gap between t₂g and eg
When ΔE corresponds to visible light energy, d-d transitions occur and colour is observed.
ΔE = hν = hc/λ
Factors affecting colour: • Oxidation state of metal (affects ΔE) • Nature of ligand (strong field ligands increase ΔE) • Geometry of complex (octahedral vs tetrahedral) • Strong field ligands: CN⁻ > NH₃ > H₂O > OH⁻ > Cl⁻

Exam-Style Questions

📝 Exam Question 1

(a) Explain why zinc is not classified as a transition element. [1]

(b) State two typical properties of transition elements and explain how they arise from electronic structure. [4]

Mark Scheme:
(a) Zn²⁺ has a complete d subshell / [Ar] 3d¹⁰ / no partially filled d subshell in any stable ion (1).
(b) Any two from: variable oxidation states (1) because 4s and 3d electrons have similar energies (1); coloured compounds (1) because d-d transitions absorb visible light (1); catalytic activity (1) because variable oxidation states allow formation of intermediates / adsorption on surface (1). Max 4.
📝 Exam Question 2

When excess ammonia is added to aqueous copper(II) sulfate, a colour change is observed.

(a) Write an equation for this reaction. [2]

(b) Explain why the colour changes. [2]

Mark Scheme:
(a) [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ + 4H₂O (2). [1 for correct formulae, 1 for balancing]
(b) Ligand substitution occurs / H₂O replaced by NH₃ (1). Different ligand causes different splitting of d orbitals / different ΔE / different wavelength absorbed (1).

📝 Quick Summary

  • Transition elements have partially filled d subshells in at least one stable ion
  • Sc and Zn are NOT transition elements
  • Properties: variable oxidation states, coloured compounds, catalytic activity, complex formation
  • Common oxidation states: Fe (+2, +3), Cu (+1, +2), Mn (+2, +4, +6, +7), Cr (+3, +6)
  • Ligands donate lone pairs; coordination number = number of coordinate bonds
  • Colour from d-d transitions; ligand strength affects ΔE and therefore colour
  • Homogeneous catalysis: catalyst in same phase; heterogeneous: different phase

💡 Exam Tips

  • Always state that Sc and Zn are not transition elements — examiners love testing this!
  • When balancing redox equations, check atoms and charges carefully
  • Ligand substitution equations must include all ligands, especially water
    • • Colour changes must be explained in terms of d-d transitions and ΔE • For catalysis, explain both homogeneous (intermediates) and heterogeneous (adsorption) mechanisms
