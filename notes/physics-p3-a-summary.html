Forces, Density & Pressure

9702 (A-Level)

Last-Minute Revision Summary

  • Newton's Laws form the foundation of classical mechanics -- understand them deeply, not just memorise
  • Inertia is the resistance to change in motion; quantified by mass
  • Equilibrium means zero resultant force -- object may be stationary OR moving at constant velocity
  • Free-body diagrams show all forces on ONE object -- essential for solving dynamics problems
  • Weight W = mg acts vertically downward; normal reaction R acts perpendicular to surface
  • Friction opposes relative motion: f <= mu R (limiting friction when object is about to slip)
  • For connected particles over a smooth pulley: same tension, same acceleration magnitude
  • On inclined planes: always resolve weight into parallel (mg sin theta) and perpendicular (mg cos theta) components
  • Momentum p = mv is a vector; conserve momentum separately in perpendicular directions if needed
  • Impulse = area under F-t graph = change in momentum = F_avg x delta t

Key Formulas

F = ma
W = mg
f <= mu R
p = mv
Impulse = F delta t = delta p

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing Newton's Third Law pairs with balanced forces -- they act on different objects
  • Forgetting friction when an object is projected UP an inclined plane -- both gravity and friction oppose motion
  • Assuming normal reaction always equals weight -- on inclined planes, R = mg cos theta
  • Forgetting momentum is a vector and not accounting for direction changes

Quick Exam Tips

  • Always draw clear, labelled free-body diagrams -- examiners award marks for correct diagrams
  • Define your positive direction before writing equations, and use it consistently
  • For Newton's Third Law questions, explicitly state that forces act on different bodies
  • When applying F = ma, use the RESULTANT force, not just one individual force
  • In connected particle problems, write separate equations for each object before combining
  • For inclined planes, resolve weight -- never resolve R or friction (they are already parallel/perpendicular)
  • In momentum problems, be careful with signs when objects reverse direction
  • Always check that answers are physically reasonable (tension > 0, acceleration sensible)
  • Show all substitution steps clearly; method marks are generous in mechanics
